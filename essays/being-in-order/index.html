Being in Order

Substance, Relation, Poinsot’s Modes, and the Ontology of Culture

Scholarly working essay · 31 October 2026 · 19 sections

Aquinas's two constitutive orders of being, the proof and limits of substantial unity and change, Poinsot's account of real modes and relations, and the emergence of objectively constituted cultural wholes belong to one inquiry: what makes an order actual rather than merely conceived? This essay distinguishes the many kinds of unity involved, reconstructs the arguments, and preserves their hardest objections.

§ 01

The reality of order

What makes a living organism one being rather than a system of interacting materials? What makes one actual quantity greater than another, if no additional object called greater-than appears between them? What makes a political office or university a real order when it cannot be identified with any one building, individual, or material object? These questions concern the same metaphysical difficulty: the actuality of unity, dependence, reference, and organization.

Two common alternatives are unsatisfactory. One treats reality as a set of independently identifiable individuals and reduces their relational features to conceptual comparisons. The other moves too quickly from the intelligibility of organized wholes to an ideal or exemplary order supposed to ground them. Neither explains how actual parts, powers, habits, and operations constitute the relational configurations under investigation. What is needed first is a proximate ontology of constitution, modes, and order, before any inference to ultimate causes.

Aquinas provides the principal distinction between substantial actuality and the order of already existing things. John Poinsot—central to this essay—develops the ontology of modes, relations, and signs. His views sometimes conflict with those of Suárez. Later interpretations by John Deely and Ralph Austin Powell help extend the account to experience, causal systems, and culture. They are not being presented as though they belonged to one undifferentiated school.

§ 02

What Aquinas calls ordo

At the beginning of his commentary on the Nicomachean Ethics, Aquinas writes that wisdom belongs to the person who orders. He distinguishes the order of parts within a whole from the order of things toward an end, and holds the second to be more fundamental: an army's arrangement of members exists for the sake of its ordered operation. He then identifies four orders in relation to human reason: a natural order that reason considers but does not make; the logical order reason establishes among its concepts and arguments; an order reason establishes in voluntary action; and the order it establishes in things produced through art. [1]

Order is therefore not equivalent to a temporal sequence or to an arrangement of ideas. The order of discovery may run from a visible effect to its invisible cause, while the causal order runs the other way. A material potentiality is prior to its actualization in one respect, whereas actuality is prior in formal intelligibility and explanation. A principle is something first in an order, although not every such principle is an efficient cause. Aquinas also differentiates an essential ordering of dependence from a mere historical succession of causes.

Ontologically, ordo names a determinate bearing among distinct but connected principles or members according to some rule of priority, proportion, causal dependence, cooperation, or finality. This is a reconstruction from several Aquinas texts, not a single verbatim definition. The term tells us that there is an ordering; the metaphysician must then identify its foundation, its kind of unity, and its actual mode of being.

§ 03

The two intrinsic orders of a corporeal substance

Aquinas recognizes two fundamental compositions within a created material substance. First, matter is ordered toward substantial form as potency to the act that constitutes a determinate natural substance. Second, the whole constituted essence is ordered toward its participated actus essendi: the act by which that nature actually exists. This is explicit in De substantiis separatis and Summa contra Gentiles II.52–54. [2]

Composition of a natureMatter → FormPotentiality actualized as a substance of a determinate kind.
Actuality of the existentEssence → EsseA constituted nature participating in its own act of being.

Neither arrow indicates that the first principle once existed as a complete thing without the second. Prime matter never subsists as a fully actual substance; a composite essence is not a complete existent awaiting a subsequent coating of existence. Aquinas is analyzing one actual being through distinct orders of dependence and actuality, not placing complete beings one beside the other.

These two compositions demand different proofs. The matter–form argument begins with the unity and change of natural things. The essence–esse argument invokes participation in actuality, the finitude of the determinate nature, and the uniqueness of unreceived subsisting existence. The ability to understand what something is without knowing whether it exists establishes distinct intelligible accounts, but cannot by itself establish two really distinct intrinsic principles. That stronger inference needs a defended metaphysics of act, potency, and causation. [3]

§ 04

Substantial unity and unity of order

Aquinas distinguishes a natural substance from an aggregate or artifact. A house is composed of materials arranged to serve an end. Its constituents retain substantial identities as the physical materials of a house. A living organism is different in the Thomistic account: its organs are what they are through their participation in the life of the one individual. The heart, eye, and limbs have distinct operations, but their characteristic actuality as living organs derives from the substantial unity of the animal.

In Summa theologiae I.76, Aquinas argues that the human being is one subject who lives, senses, and understands. If its soul were merely an external director of an already complete body, or if successive complete substantial forms made one thing first a body and then an animal and then a human, the result would be a unity of interacting actual beings rather than one substance. The soul is therefore said to be substantial form: it actualizes the body as an intrinsically living whole. After death, recognizable bodily parts persist materially but cease to have the relevant complete living operations. [4]

This argument is philosophically stronger than asserting that organisms are extraordinarily complicated machines. Functional coordination, feedback, and interdependence also characterize machines and institutions. What must be shown is the deeper claim that the capacities of an organism's parts are constituted as parts of one substance, rather than simply controlled by a complex arrangement of already complete substances. Organic integration is evidence to investigate, not an indisputable demonstration of a substantial form.

§ 05

What could actually prove substantial unity?

An argument for substantial unity has to make the difference between unum per se and unum per accidens do explanatory work. One may begin from the unified individual that maintains itself through an integrated developmental trajectory, has characteristic acts attributable to the whole, replaces material constituents without losing its identity, and loses the actuality of its organs when its life ceases. But none of these observations alone automatically defeats a sophisticated account of organized, dynamically coupled components.

The Thomist must defend the thesis that the organic whole constitutes a principal subject of action whose unity is not exhausted by the separate complete actuality of its constituents. If all the parts were independently complete substances related only through causal coordination, their integration would be of the order of a machine or army. If the organism is genuinely one substance, there must be an intrinsic formal source of the unity through which the parts possess their natural capacities as its parts.

There is room for a serious objection. A sufficiently rich systems ontology might attribute self-maintenance, integrated agency, causal closure at appropriate scales, and stable identity to the organization itself. Why does this not suffice? The Thomist cannot simply repeat the word 'substantial'. The dispute must turn on the identity and powers of parts, what persists through material turnover, whether the whole is a principal cause rather than a mere ordered assemblage, and whether the forms of the parts are complete independently of the whole. This remains a live metaphysical question. [5]

§ 06

The argument from substantial change—and its vulnerability

Accidental change presupposes an actual substance that persists while becoming hotter, larger, displaced, or otherwise determined. Substantial change is the generation or corruption of the very substance, not merely a change in one of its accidents. Aquinas argues in De principiis naturae that if a newly generated substance were simply a further form attaching to an already complete substantial subject, its acquisition would be accidental. Natural substantial generation instead requires a real potential principle—matter—not already complete as the generated substance, and an actualizing substantial form. [6]

But the syllogism depends on the premise that substantial generation really occurs. The death of an animal, a chemical reaction, or the development of an embryo presents actual phenomena; it does not supply its own metaphysical interpretation. A reductionist can say that enduring physical constituents undergo changes in arrangement and operation. A defender of substantial change must argue that this account fails to explain why a substantial subject has ceased to be or come into being.

The strongest cumulative case looks at the loss or acquisition of an individual range of characteristic operations, the dependence of parts on the living whole, the intrinsic unity of development, and the difference between a subject changing state and a subject's ending. Even this is a contested inference to constitutive principles, not a direct sighting of prime matter in separation. Without a demonstration that the relevant change is substantial rather than accidental or merely configurational, the argument would be circular. [5]

§ 07

Poinsot's substantial whole and the rejection of a third complete unity

Poinsot investigates substantial unity within a natural philosophy of ens mobile. In his Cursus philosophicus, Philosophia naturalis I, q. VI, he denies that the actual substantial whole requires another complete thing added to matter and form already united. The united principles constitute the whole, rather than forming a provisional collection that must then be completed by a new substantial intermediary. This is a distinction between the parts as united and the mere aggregate of parts considered apart from their substantial communication. [7]

More sharply, Poinsot resists the account of a separately superadded real mode of union serving as a tertium quid between matter and substantial form. Matter is intrinsically potency for form, and form is intrinsically its substantial act. To posit an additional connector as what causes their union risks begging for another account of how that connector actually unites them. Poinsot's argument is that the aptitude for substantial union is already present in the complementary principles. He does not thereby deny that substantial union is actual; he denies that its actuality is explained by a further distinct formal mediator. [8]

This point is easily misunderstood when discussing modes. Calling union a mode of the constituted composite is not automatically an assertion that a distinct union-entity is inserted between otherwise complete things. Poinsot distinguishes what describes the constitution or completion of something from the ontological claim that there must be a separately superadded uniting principle. This is one reason his theory of modes matters more here than merely borrowing a generic Suárezian distinction.

§ 08

Poinsot's theory of modes: constitutive and extrinsic

Poinsot's Material Logic explicitly divides modes into two broad groups. The first concerns the constitution or completion of a nature. He mentions union of substantial principles, subsistence, inherence, and determinate degrees of quality. These modes are reduced to the category of that which they complete. The second group concerns determinations involving an extrinsic form or principle, such as where a thing is or the disposition of its parts; ubi and situs can therefore occupy distinct predicamental places. Poinsot reports that some philosophers also place relation within this second group. [9]

Constitutive or completing modesUnion · Subsistence · InherenceWays in which a nature, supposit, accident, or quality is completed or determined.
Modes involving an extrinsic principleUbi · Situs · Some relationsWhere, posture, and other determinations whose intelligibility involves something beyond the subject.

In Poinsot a modal distinction can be real without being a distinction between two complete things. A person and this posture, a thing and its local presence, or a relation and its proximate foundation may differ through a dependent real determination. The mode is not a second independently subsisting substance. Nor is it merely an act of naming or comparing. This is why he distinguishes a real-modal distinction from a distinction made solely by the intellect.

Poinsot's account should not be flattened. He can admit constitutive modes without treating every instance of union as a further superadded real connector. Nor must the same modal principle explain a thing's being locally situated, a form's substantial communication with matter, and an intellect's reference to an object. His modes belong to a structured metaphysics of distinct sorts of determination. David Svoboda's critical study notes that Poinsot's account of precisely how modal distinctions are grounded is sometimes frustratingly brief. [10]

§ 09

Is a real relation a mode? Poinsot's answer

Poinsot analyzes categorical relation through subject, fundamentum, and terminus. The subject is what is denominated relative; the foundation is what grounds the relation's actuality; the terminus is that toward which it is ordered. Its specific formal character is esse ad, being-toward-another. Nevertheless, it is a created accident dependent upon its foundation for existence. It is not a third self-sufficient object suspended in the gap between things. [11]

The relation of similarity between two stones, for example, is grounded in their actual qualitative determinations. The quality of one stone remains even if the other is destroyed, but the actual similarity to that terminus no longer obtains. Poinsot treats the relation as modally distinct from its proximate foundation. It is formally irreducible to the individual quality precisely because its being is reference to another; yet it is ontologically dependent upon the relevant real foundation.

His conclusion is subtler than that relations are little entities additional to bodies. It is realism about a distinctive relative mode of being. Suárez's different view can be summarized briefly: a real relation may be sufficiently founded through the intrinsic reality of the relata without requiring another modally distinct relation in addition to the foundation. The disagreement concerns the ontology of real relational denomination, not whether two rods can genuinely be equal. [10]

§ 10

Respectus and transcendental relation

Respectus names bearing or reference toward another. Considered generally it does not determine whether that reference is real, rational, constitutive, or additional. Poinsot distinguishes pure categorical relation, whose entire proper formal character is reference toward a terminus as terminus, from an intrinsic or transcendental ordering in which a nature's dependence or specification includes another. Matter is essentially ordered toward form; a cognitive faculty is ordered toward its appropriate object. Neither is therefore merely another accident added to a self-enclosed subject. [11]

'Transcendental' here means crossing the boundaries of the individual Aristotelian predicaments, not occupying an elevated spiritual realm. This sense is historically linked to the transcendentals of being, one, true, and good, but transcendental relation is not one more property convertible with every being in exactly that same sense. It marks intrinsic dependence or directedness found in several categorical domains.

Poinsot associates transcendental relative being with relativum secundum dici: the primary thing signified has its own intrinsic nature but refers to another through its character or function. A categorical relation is a pure relativum secundum esse. Suárez classifies these differently; historical discussion must therefore preserve the terminological disagreement. The shared insight is that essential order and categorical relation are not interchangeable.

§ 11

Respectus considered in itself: an Avicennian analogy

Poinsot writes that relation considered generically is understood as its ratio et quidditas, its common intelligible account and quiddity, rather than as an actual exercise of reference toward a determinate terminus. This resembles Avicenna's account of a quiddity considered in itself, neither already universal in the intellect nor already individuated outside it. But the analogy has a limit: horsehood specifies a substantial nature; pure relation is understood only through reference toward another. [11]

Three levels must be separated. The act of conceiving is a real psychological state in the knower. The conceptual content is the intelligible reference considered. An actual relation may or may not obtain between independent things. Imagining two equally sized fictional objects constitutes a rational comparison without creating two physical rods; comparing two actual rods does not create their quantitative foundations. This prevents treating the common intelligibility of respect as either a fiction or a separately subsisting universal object.

§ 12

Subjective, suprasubjective, and objective

In Deely's ontological usage, subjective does not mean opinion-based. A material substance, its intrinsic whiteness, and an actual psychological perception are all subjective in the older sense: they exist as individual subjects or modifications of subjects. An object represented by a psychological act need not itself be an existing external substance. 'Objective' concerns what is given as known; 'subjective' in this context concerns actual subjects and their inherent characteristics. [12]

Relation is suprasubjective formally because its reference is not confined to the intrinsic actuality of one subject. The similarity of two stones is not identical with the whiteness of either taken alone. If both subjects actually exist with the relevant foundations, the relation can be intersubjective; if the relation is constructed among fictional or absent objects, it may be purely objective. In neither case does 'supra' mean a floating material entity or an immaterial substance of relation.

The qualification in Aquinas is essential: a real relation as an accident depends upon a subject and its foundation, whereas as relation it is formally toward another. Deely develops the formal second aspect, but his talk of relations 'between' subjects should not be interpreted as a literal denial of accidental inherence. A relative mode can depend on a subject without being exhausted by the subject's intrinsic characteristics. [13]

§ 13

Why greater-than is not necessarily a mental invention

Consider two rods, one longer than the other. Both possess real quantities. The intellect compares them and concludes that one exceeds the other. Yet if one rod is lengthened in an empty room, their proportion changes without anyone comparing them. This supports an objective quantitative foundation. But what does it prove about the additional ontology of the relation?

Poinsot's answer is a real relative mode, founded in existing quantities and distinct from the quantity through its formally terminative character. Another realist may hold that the real qualities and their grounded denomination suffice without a further distinct mode. A conceptualist may recognize the mind-independent truth of the comparative judgment while refusing to add any extramental relational accident. The issue is not simply whether comparison is justified; it is whether the ontology of the foundations alone explains the real reference. [10]

Real relation does not require continuous causal interaction or symmetrical influence. Quantitative equality, actual filiation, and the relation of a power to its object have different foundations. That a relation is not separately visible means little: substantial form, real capacity, and an act of existing are also not proper visible objects. The philosophical justification must arise from the explanatory necessity of the relation, not from seeing a relational 'thing' with one's eyes.

§ 14

The final categories: location, posture, time, and having

One can now avoid the hasty claim that the remaining categories must themselves be transcendental relations. Ubi expresses a subject's local presence; quando its temporal determination; situs the local disposition of its parts; and predicamental habitus being clothed or equipped. They are relationally intelligible, but each is not simply a pure categorical relation or an intrinsic potency-to-act dependence. The last must also be distinguished from acquired operative habitus, a quality such as virtue or scientific knowledge.

Poinsot's grouping of modes gives the better starting point: the question is whether a real determination completes a nature, depends upon extrinsic circumstances, or is constituted as a pure relative mode. A body's posture may be objectively real without being a separate substantial form; a person's being clothed can be truly grounded in the garment's actual external arrangement without a newly inherent accident called being-clothed. These are not merely arbitrary concepts, but they do not all require identical ontological machinery.

§ 15

Extrinsic denomination and formal specification

An extrinsic denomination asks why a predicate is true of a subject when the immediate denominating factor lies outside it. A tree is seen because an observer is seeing it; the tree need not acquire an intrinsic quality of being-seen. A person is clothed because clothing surrounds and fits them. The term indicates the source of the attribution, not by itself the ontology of every order or relation involved.

Extrinsic formal causality addresses a different question: what specifies an act or relation as this kind of act or relation? Poinsot distinguishes the terminus as a pure terminus from the external nature under which it specifies an interaction or object. The same whiteness can ground similarity toward another white object and dissimilarity toward a black one. The foundation and terminus together complete the determinate relational meaning without contributing separate physical halves to a third substance. [14]

Powell applies this to scientific mechanisms. A lightning strike is physically specified through the electrical and material properties of participants rather than through the accidental description 'a strike on a barn'. Causal systems are objective and repeatable without being additional individual substances. Powell argues further that actual causal coordination reveals intrinsic dispositions and teleological order. That additional step requires philosophical defence; it is not entailed simply by describing an interaction as real. [15]

§ 16

A dynamic example: watching a player on television

A viewer follows a footballer on a screen. Changing light patterns stimulate the eye; visual and neural processes contribute to movement tracking; moving the eye changes retinal input and supplies further feedback. This involves genuinely operating causal powers and a determinate sensorimotor coordination, not simply an intellectual judgment that two already visible objects are 'related'.

Three orders nonetheless overlap without becoming identical. There is the physical interaction between screen and visual system. There is the cognitive power's intrinsic ordination toward visible objects. And there is the relation of signification through which an image represents a player who might be far away or may not presently exist. The eye's motion does not ordinarily alter the broadcast screen, so even this feedback process need not be mutually causal at every level. Real ordering is not universally symmetrical determination.

The example therefore clarifies both what is distinctive about operative causal relation and why not all real relations are operative: greater-than and equality may be real without changing their terms through active interaction. It also clarifies why signification cannot be reduced either to the luminous particles of the display or to a private mental act.

§ 17

The sign as a relation and the emergence of culture

Poinsot's doctrine of signs provides the decisive transition beyond substances conceived in isolation. A spoken word or printed mark exists as a subjective physical determination. But it signifies only through a relation that presents something other than the vehicle to a cognitive power. The sign properly considered is not identical with the ink, the sound, or an internal image; its being as sign involves vehicle, signified, and the cognitive terminus. Deely and later semioticians explain why the relation can be realized among actual objects or operate through absent and imagined ones. [11]

A conventional sign can become embedded in real habits through repetition. Poinsot carefully distinguishes an original stipulation from the acquired, customary capacity through which actions and recognitions come to signify with relative regularity. This does not turn every custom into a natural necessity or every habit into a sign. It shows how convention becomes enacted in stable qualities, practices, and operations. [16]

This supplies a common ontological framework for real bodies, real habits, deliberate practices, and purely objective meanings. It does not license their reduction to a single class of relational entities. The act of perceiving is a subjective cognitive actuality; its object is apprehended through a relation; the content represented can exceed the presently existing physical world. Culture can therefore be genuinely organized through signifying relations without consisting of additional natural substances.

§ 18

Relational configurations and the unity of institutions

The organized bed has a determinate arrangement and load-bearing capacity missing from a disordered heap of components. The army involves more than soldiers standing near one another: it involves command, training, authority, coordinated action, and a common end. A university involves individuals, buildings, records, offices, contractual powers, rules, teaching and research practices, and relations of recognition. It may survive a change of members without becoming an individual natural substance.

Deely offers a striking test. A police officer crosses the limit of a jurisdiction. The officer's hands, equipment, and bodily powers remain, but the law's authorization may cease. The legal difference cannot be found as a newly removed molecular property; nor is it merely the private fancy of the observer. The institution is sustained through signs, practices, offices, and normative recognition. [12]

To explain these cases we need the unity of an ordered whole, not merely a list of pairwise relations. Some relations depend upon real powers and physical interactions; others upon public acts and authority; still others upon signification and interpretive habits. The resulting system may be stable, effective, and objectively consequential without being unum per se in the same way as a natural organism. What makes it a university rather than an arbitrary network is the structured ordering of roles and capacities to its institutional ends.

Realism about these institutional realities must be paired with normative analysis. That an office is intelligible does not in itself justify its authority, and that a practice is customary does not guarantee it is good. Law, practical reason, obligation, and the ends of human life remain indispensable to the ontology of culture.

§ 19

What remains to be demonstrated

The cumulative framework makes possible a coherent account of things that are neither separately subsisting material substances nor merely mental fictions. Natural substances have intrinsic principles of unity; those substances and their accidents have real modes and founded relations; cognitive powers apprehend objective meanings through signs; persons acting under conventions form artifacts, organizations, and institutions with genuine unities of order.

Yet the critical questions must not be softened into slogans. An integration of operations is not automatically a demonstration of one substantial form. Observed destruction of an organism is not, without philosophical premises, a conclusive proof of substantial corruption. An intellect's distinction between essence and existence is not itself a demonstration of their real composition. A true comparative judgment does not by itself decide whether a modally distinct relation must be posited beyond its foundation. Poinsot offers particularly subtle resources for these analyses but not a universal shortcut around them.

The investigation should therefore proceed from observable and intelligible phenomena to the proximate principles required to explain them. Only after their actual constitution has been adequately accounted for is it appropriate to consider deeper causal or exemplary grounds. The ambition is a unified Aristotelian–Thomistic ontology of nature, knowledge, and culture, but the unity sought is an ordered explanation of different modes of being, not an indiscriminate reduction of all being to relation.
