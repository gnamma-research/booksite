Prophetic beginnings: guidance addressed to human life

Ādam received knowledge, accepted responsibility, erred and repented. Nūḥ called a people attached to their accustomed ways; Ibrāhīm left inherited worship and placed his household under a covenant with God. Through Ismāʿīl and Isḥāq the prophetic family came to be remembered in Arabia and the Levant. Yūsuf's enslavement and eventual authority brought the family of Yaʿqūb into Egypt; Mūsā led the Israelites out of servitude and taught them a law ordering both worship and their dealings with one another. Dāwūd and Sulaymān united prophetic authority with kingship, while later messengers challenged rulers and communities that had neglected their obligations. These lives were transmitted as a history of the same divine summons meeting very different human circumstances. A household, a people in bondage, a settled kingdom and a scattered community each required guidance suited to its condition. By the time of Muḥammad, the stories of earlier prophets were already known in communities across the Near East. His proclamation would renew their central summons while addressing the particular claims of sanctuary, kinship and power in Arabia.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The Qurʾān recounts Ādam's responsibility, Nūḥ's warning, Ibrāhīm's rejection of idols, Yūsuf's enslavement and eventual authority, and Mūsā's liberation and legislation (2:124–129; 7:59–93; 11:25–99; 19:41–58). These accounts affirm worship and moral accountability but neither date Ādam nor supply a continuous dynastic chronology. Jewish and Christian chronologies assign different dates to early patriarchs; those calculations do not establish dates accepted by Muslims. The prophets addressed households, oppressed peoples, ruling courts and settled communities under different historical conditions.

Muḥammad challenged inherited worship in Makkah as Ibrāhīm had challenged the idols of his people. In Madinah he judged disputes, organized collective obligations and taught law, but the institutions of his community differed from those of the Israelites under Mūsā. The prophetic missions shared a summons to worship God and act justly without reproducing identical political arrangements.

Jewish and Christian communities transmitted scriptural memories through families, worship, scribes, sanctuaries, teaching and translated texts. Exile, imperial administration, trade and the movement of teachers carried these traditions through different languages and settlements. Their documented histories explain how earlier accounts of prophecy remained known in the Near East and Arabia; they do not establish a continuous genealogy of political institutions from Ibrāhīm to seventh-century Makkah, nor do they constitute a material cause of revelation.

Land, settlement and the political worlds of the Near East

Along the Nile and the Tigris–Euphrates, farming communities learned to organize labour around rivers, store crops and support towns whose officials did not work the fields. Egypt and Mesopotamia developed kingship, record keeping, taxation, temples and armies; merchants and pastoral groups connected them to settlements beyond their direct rule. The Levant lay between these larger powers, with cultivable valleys, upland villages, coastal ports and routes toward Arabia. Its towns could prosper through exchange and suffer when armies demanded tribute or fought for the same territory. During the second millennium BCE Egyptian and other imperial powers contested Syria and Canaan; around 1200 BCE several palace-based political systems weakened or disappeared. New polities—including Phoenician cities, Aramaean kingdoms and the emerging kingdoms of Israel and Judah—developed amid the older fields, roads and sanctuaries. This matters to the later religious history because the inhabitants of these places did not encounter prophecy in an institutional vacuum. Their rulers levied resources, their households held land and debts, and their sanctuaries ordered communal life. The claims of the prophets addressed people embedded in these relationships.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

A settlement became a town through a combination of harvests, craft specialization, surplus storage and the ability to mobilize labour. Record keeping and royal courts made durable administration possible, but also allowed rulers to collect taxes, conscript workers and concentrate land. The Levant was especially exposed because competing states could reach it by land and sea. A change of empire therefore altered not just a ruler's name but security, tribute, local elites and the movement of goods. Pastoral groups and traders continued to cross ecological zones, negotiate protection and carry goods along routes beyond continuous state control. This provides a social setting for the later problems of kingship and justice in Israelite history.

River valleys supported offices that recorded irrigation, crops and levies, while travellers through arid corridors relied on negotiated access to wells and passage. Both demand cooperation, but the relevant political powers differ: a canal requires maintenance, surveying and control over fields; a caravan needs hosts, agreements and knowledge of routes. Makkah's sanctuary operated through local ritual protections and clan agreements, not the palace administration that governed many Mesopotamian cities. Egyptian and Mesopotamian rulers employed centralized offices, while many Arabian clans depended on negotiated protection and local alliances; both arrangements regulated recurring disputes in different ways. It also prevents the early chapters from functioning as a tour of famous ancient civilizations unrelated to the Sīrah.

Religious instruction could travel more readily than the fiscal records and irrigation systems tied to a particular territory. An irrigation register belongs to a particular land and administration; a remembered command, prayer formula or legal text can be taught beyond that locality. The latter still needs institutions—teachers, language, copies, patronage—but can survive a change of ruler. Judean communities maintained religious teaching after losing political sovereignty; centuries later, Muslim scholars circulated books and instruction across dynastic borders.

The evidence comes from separately dated archaeological and written corpora—Uruk administration, Egyptian records, second-millennium diplomatic texts, and later Iron Age inscriptions. It does not verify the chronology of Ādam or Nūḥ. Nor does resemblance between ancient caravan systems and Meccan alliances prove an unbroken institutional inheritance across two millennia. The narrower, useful connection is that religious instruction regularly met structures of property, dependence and political authority. The loss of Judah’s monarchy and sanctuary made the continued teaching of communal obligations under foreign rule a practical historical problem.

Israel and Judah: kingship, destruction and life beyond a kingdom

In the Israelite remembrance, Dāwūd and Sulaymān ruled a people responsible for worship and justice; prophets later recalled both rulers and subjects to the covenant. In the Iron Age Levant, the kingdoms of Israel and Judah occupied lands contested by stronger neighbours. Assyria conquered Israel's capital, Samaria, in 722/721 BCE. A century later Babylon imposed its power on Judah, took Jerusalem in 597 and destroyed its temple in 587/586 BCE, deporting members of the population while others remained. People who had associated worship with land, king and sanctuary now had to maintain their identity in dispersed communities. Persian conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE enabled a restored temple at Jerusalem, but did not bring all Judeans home. Priestly service, teaching of the Torah, festivals and household observances sustained Jewish communities under rulers of different languages and religions. During subsequent centuries Jewish communities would live from Mesopotamia to the Mediterranean and eventually in several Arabian settlements. By the Prophet's lifetime, Jews in Yathrib participated in local alliances and cultivation as well as scriptural traditions. Their presence belonged to a long history of Jewish life outside independent Jewish kingship, though the origins of particular Arabian groups were diverse and are not securely reconstructed.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The transition from monarchy to dispersion changes the central historical problem. When the temple could no longer function and the king no longer secured the territory, continuity depended more heavily on scribal teaching, kin relations, calendar, prayer, law and ritual practice. The return under Persian supremacy restored a centre but not a self-sufficient sovereign state. This helps explain how identifiable Jewish communities could persist through Greek, Roman and Sasanian changes of rule. Jewish communities maintained religious institutions in Arabia, but the surviving evidence does not trace every family's ancestry to the Babylonian exile.

The changing medium of religious continuity is the decisive bridge. Royal patronage can maintain a central sanctuary; a dispersed community must teach children, preserve forms of worship, settle questions internally and recognize people of the same religious tradition elsewhere. Some of these activities developed over long periods, and their exact early chronology is contested. The conceptual connection to Islam's later history is nevertheless concrete: after the caliphate fragmented, Muslim communities too relied upon learning, law and travel beyond the reach of one political centre. Judean communities transmitted scripture through priests and scribes; Islamic madrasas formed much later under different political and educational conditions.

The Arabian connection must remain deliberately modest. Evidence that Jews were established in Yathrib in the Prophet's lifetime is not evidence identifying the specific route by which each family reached it. Jewish communities established roots in Arabia over different periods, but surviving sources do not establish one ancestry or migration date for all of them. The stronger line of explanation is how religious observance and distinctive communal law could exist under governments not controlled by those communities.

The Qurʾān recounts the missions of Mūsā, Dāwūd and Sulaymān (2:47–61; 21:78–82; 34:10–14); Kings, Chronicles and Ezra–Nehemiah preserve related but distinct histories, while Near Eastern evidence helps date the conquests of 722, 597 and 587/586 BCE. The Qurʾān and the biblical books recount some shared prophetic figures but differ in emphases, narrative structure and theological claims. Late-antique Jewish communities could be connected by learning and ritual despite local variations in language, social hierarchy and political status. This will later matter in Madinah, where the Prophet did not encounter an abstract “Judaism” but households and groups with land, alliances and particular obligations. Before that, the Persian and Greek worlds changed the languages and intellectual settings in which such communities lived.

Religious disciplines and the changing world of the first millennium BCE

After the destruction of Jerusalem, Jewish communities had to sustain worship and teaching without the independence of their former kingdom. Meanwhile the Persian kings linked the Levant, Mesopotamia and Iran through imperial government, and Greek cities developed their own modes of philosophical inquiry. Farther east, teachers in northern India debated ritual and liberation, while Chinese thinkers disputed the moral obligations of rulers and families. These developments unfolded across different centuries; they were not the work of a single generation or one demonstrable worldwide upheaval. In the western Near East, however, the lines of contact can be followed. Persian administration brought diverse communities under common rule; Alexander's conquests replaced it with Greek-speaking dynasties; and Greek became a language through which Jews, Egyptians, Syrians and others could explain their traditions to wider audiences. Jewish scripture was translated and interpreted in Greek, and schools of argument developed in cities where different religious communities met. The Roman world would inherit much of this linguistic and urban setting. It was within Jewish life under Roman authority—not in an undefined 'age of religions'—that the movement associated with ʿĪsā began. The other Eurasian transformations belong in our horizon of comparison, but only the routes that can actually be traced should be described as historical connections.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Karl Jaspers later grouped several developments under the label “Axial Age”; their different dates and settings do not establish one common historical cause. The dates of the Buddha, Zoroaster, Laozi and individual early Greek teachers are disputed to varying degrees. Connections must therefore be shown individually: an imperial road or shared administrative language can support movement of people and texts, whereas similarity between two doctrines does not by itself prove borrowing. Persia's administrative reach and the Hellenistic spread of Greek give stronger grounds for certain western Eurasian contacts than for a single transformation spanning India, China and Greece.

Guénon's argument also requires a correction often missing in its popular retellings. He considered the sixth century a threshold of what he called ordinary historical accessibility, but surviving Mesopotamian, Egyptian and other sources allow detailed chronology much earlier. His historical premise cannot be accepted without qualification, and his judgment of Buddhism or philosophy reflects his own conception of orthodoxy and intellectual hierarchy. The intellectually fruitful question is narrower: did some traditions respond to changing social conditions by differentiating ritual, practical discipline and contemplative knowledge? That can be studied separately in each tradition. It matters for the later Islamic account because philosophy, law, revelation and spiritual realization also came to be distinguishable modes of teaching, though their boundaries were drawn differently.

Guénon's metaphysical interpretation of sacred cycles and the dated history of individual teachers address different kinds of claims. Achaemenid government, Hellenistic cities and Greek scriptural translations created identifiable political and linguistic links across the western Near East. For China and India, the comparisons concern developing traditions of moral and contemplative training; their presence in one paragraph is not an argument that they directly shaped first-century Christianity or the seventh-century Ḥijāz. Guénon's sixth-century claim is a metaphysical-periodizing judgment with its own disputed historical assumptions, not an empirical explanation of the co-occurrence of named founders.

René Guénon's The Crisis of the Modern World, chapter 1, gives a different reading: he associates transformations around the sixth century BCE with an increasingly differentiated and outward historical phase of traditional civilizations. His claim concerns spiritual cycles and transformations in the accessibility of principial knowledge, not simply a chronology of founders. Guénon interprets the changes through spiritual cycles; the available dates do not demonstrate that a single rupture occurred across Eurasia. The Prophet's contemporaries inherited a world of earlier scriptural communities, philosophical traditions and rival institutions; none of those inheritances alone explains the emergence of his message. The immediate lineage leading to the seventh-century Near East passes through Hellenistic and Roman Judaea, Christian churches and the imperial frontiers.

Jesus and the communities that carried his message

In first-century Judaea, Roman governors, local rulers, priestly families and teachers of the Law operated within a population whose hopes and disagreements were shaped by worship at Jerusalem and memories of earlier prophets. Yaḥyā summoned people to repentance, and ʿĪsā ibn Maryam called Israel to sincere worship, mercy and righteousness. His disciples gathered around his teaching; after his earthly mission, communities devoted to him arose in Judaea and spread into Greek-speaking cities. Their members had to decide how Jews and non-Jews could share worship and discipline, what authority the apostles' teaching carried, and how the life of Jesus should be told. Letters, narrative accounts, readings and liturgies travelled with these communities. Jerusalem's destruction in 70 CE transformed Jewish religious life; Christian assemblies, meanwhile, multiplied around the Mediterranean and eastward. When imperial patronage followed Constantine in the fourth century, debates already underway concerning Christ's status acquired new councils, offices and political consequences. Greek, Syriac, Coptic and other churches did not interpret those debates identically. Monasteries became places of prayer, learning and hospitality, and missionaries and traders carried Christian life beyond the imperial frontier. By Muḥammad's lifetime the Christian people encountered by Arabs were neither a single doctrinal party nor a distant abstraction: they were neighbours, traders, rulers, monks and communities bound to different churches.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The Islamic account of ʿĪsā and Maryam belongs to revealed history (notably Qurʾān 3:33–55; 5:110–120; 19:16–36). The earliest recoverable Christian histories rest on a different kind of evidence: first-century letters, Gospel narratives, later patristic writings and documents of councils. Christian creeds developed through councils and theological disputes; their later formulations cannot automatically be attributed to the earliest disciples. The controversy surrounding Nicaea (325), Ephesus (431) and Chalcedon (451) needs to be understood as sustained argument among Christian communities, not as a single moment when Christianity became homogeneous.

Christianity also changed the geography of learning. In Greek-speaking eastern cities, philosophical and theological argument shared a vocabulary capable of crossing political boundaries. Syriac translators and teachers moved between theological, medical and philosophical work, sometimes under rulers of different religions. This made them potential mediators when Muslim patrons later sought Greek learning in Arabic. The intellectual bridge is therefore not a vague statement that Islam was 'influenced by Christianity'; it is the documented existence of bilingual scholars, texts and schools available within the regions that Muslim armies and merchants later encountered. In the Sīrah itself the immediate issues were worship, prophecy and protection, not the ninth-century translation of Aristotle.

A concrete mechanism in Christian expansion is linguistic and urban. Letters to congregations could circulate along roads and shipping routes connecting Greek-speaking cities; local assemblies could read them, dispute their meaning and transmit instruction even without a common local bishop. Later councils and imperial patronage made certain disputes politically consequential. At the Arabian frontier, however, Greek, Syriac, Coptic and Geʿez Christian lives did not form one uniform audience for Islam. Each source needs to identify the community it actually describes.

The causal thread is institutional as well as doctrinal. Greek and Syriac writing made teachings travel; churches offered regular worship and communal leadership; monasticism provided spaces for disciplined life, copying and teaching. Imperial patronage offered resources and created new occasions for controversy, since bishops and emperors could have different interests. Aksum's Christianity and Syriac-speaking communities in Persian territory meant that the Prophet's contemporaries encountered Christian practices beyond Constantinople's control. Muslims encountered Christian neighbours, envoys, protectors, clergy and monks as well as arguments about the person of Christ. Their routes of movement traversed empires and Arabian borderlands where religion, commerce and military protection intersected.

The sixth-century crossroads of Rome, Persia, Ethiopia and Arabia

The world bordering sixth-century Arabia was divided between powerful courts that also depended upon local allies. From Constantinople the Romans administered Syria and Egypt; Sasanian kings governed Iran and Iraq; Arab federates guarded approaches, mediated trade and sometimes fought one another along the imperial frontier. To the south, conflict between Ḥimyar, Aksum and Persian interests drew Yemen into Red Sea diplomacy. The persecution of Najrān's Christians in the 520s and Aksumite intervention showed how Arabian religious disputes could involve neighbouring kings. Yet the Ḥijāz lay outside direct Roman or Sasanian administration. Its towns depended more immediately on local sanctuaries, families, oases and alliances, while travellers carried news from the wider world. Jews and Christians lived in different Arabian regions; local shrines and tribal devotions continued alongside them. In 602 the Romans and Sasanians entered a devastating war. Syria and Egypt fell under Persian occupation before Roman recovery in the 620s. Around the beginning of that war Muḥammad was approaching maturity in Makkah; around 610 his public mission began to take form. Neither empire appointed the chiefs of Quraysh or the alliances of Yathrib, yet the conflict would soon alter the political setting into which the Muslim community expanded. The Prophet's earliest hearers lived among households governed by Quraysh's sanctuary customs and clan protections rather than direct imperial administration.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

An empire's presence on the frontier did not make every caravan route a branch of its administration. The Ghassanid and Lakhmid networks involved military patronage, kinship and competition, while the Red Sea introduced Ethiopian, South Arabian and Persian interests with different reach. Literary accounts of Najrān and the Aksumite invasion need comparison with epigraphic and archaeological evidence; Valentina Grasso's analysis of sixth-century South Arabia cautions against reducing every political intervention to a single religious motive. Frontier rulers mobilized military allies, collected revenues and offered varying degrees of protection to religious communities.

The battle between imperial powers also reshaped what an Arabian leader might encounter as expansion proceeded. Muslim forces moving into Syria entered territory with military roads, bishoprics and taxation already organized; movements into Iraq encountered Sasanian estates and administrative practices. The conquest did not invent those systems and could not rule without engaging them. Yet the Prophet's first listeners were not Roman subjects receiving a centrally enforced imperial creed. Meccan believers depended on personal protection and voluntary allegiance; after conquest, Muslim rulers administered the taxes and offices of former imperial provinces.

The Roman–Sasanian war of 602–628 explains part of the international setting, but it should not be made the mechanical cause of revelation. It did weaken both imperial systems before major Muslim conquests. Makkah's particular situation instead depended on sanctuary customs, Quraysh's clan arrangements and regional trade. Early Muslim sources place the Year of the Elephant near the Prophet's birth, but their accounts of the expedition and its dating differ. Abū Ṭālib protected Muḥammad through kinship obligations even though he did not adopt the new proclamation.

The chain from Najrān to seventh-century Arabia should not be turned into a single cause of Muḥammad's proclamation. Najrān documents the presence of Christianity, regional persecution and cross-Red-Sea intervention; it does not prove that the Meccan message arose from imperial conflict. The Roman–Persian war matters differently: it transformed the military position of the two states into whose provinces Muslim forces would later move. Thus the local origin of the mission and the geopolitical environment of its later expansion have distinct histories.

Book II — The formation of believers in Makkah · 07 / 46

The sanctuary, the clans and the obligations of protection

Makkah was organized around a sanctuary that drew people whose clans might otherwise have been rivals. Quraysh families maintained the Kaʿbah and its services, arranged trade, and relied upon agreements that made travel and exchange possible across tribal boundaries. A person's safety ordinarily depended upon kinship or a protector willing to defend him; those with few allies or enslaved status had much less room to refuse a powerful household's demands. The sanctuary could restrain violence at particular places and times without abolishing wider inequalities or feuds. Among Quraysh, Banū Hāshim held an established place, and Abū Ṭālib would protect his nephew Muḥammad even when he did not join his religious summons. Beyond Makkah, Yathrib was a settlement of orchards and fortified neighbourhoods where the Aws, Khazraj and several Jewish groups were linked by cultivation, alliances and longstanding disputes. These two towns offered different possibilities. In Makkah the first call would be heard under the protection and pressures of established clans; in Yathrib rival groups would eventually seek a leader capable of mediating and joining them. The Hijrah thus became possible through relationships developing long before the emigrants departed.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Kinship and patronage determined who could challenge prevailing religious customs without immediately losing protection. A free man with an influential guardian, an enslaved person and a visitor without an alliance did not face the same risks when accepting a new religious allegiance. This social difference helps explain why early conversions followed ties of trust and household contact, and why some believers could resist pressure while others suffered violence. It would be anachronistic to describe Quraysh's sanctuary as a modern government. Tribal obligation, ritual privilege, negotiated protection and exchange could overlap without creating one centralized state. The Sīrah tradition's accounts of the Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl and the Prophet's protection by Abū Ṭālib are valuable evidence for how contemporaries remembered justice and patronage; the wording and sequence of those accounts need comparison across transmitters.

The protection system helps explain both the survival of the Prophet's mission and the injustice suffered by vulnerable converts. Abū Ṭālib's defence of his nephew was a recognizable kin obligation, even while religious disagreement persisted. A client or enslaved convert could lack a similarly effective protector. Accepting the new call was therefore not one uniform act of social defiance. The religion's claim of common accountability sat within highly unequal capacities to resist. Later migration did not remove the importance of kinship immediately; it placed inherited loyalties into new relations with people whose commitment rested upon worship rather than birth.

Mecca's exact commercial scale is disputed. Traditional narratives of Quraysh's seasonal journeys should be distinguished from the stronger and weaker claims that particular Meccan families controlled long-distance luxury trade. The protection system is more immediately relevant to the first believers: clan standing could shield a dissenting member while enslaved or client households faced coercion. That difference explains the uneven distribution of suffering without implying that the first converts belonged to one social class.

Quraysh organized commercial journeys, but historians disagree about the volume and geographical reach of Meccan trade; evidence does not establish that its clans controlled a vast trading empire. Qurʾān 106 emphasizes journeys, security and provision, while the institutional details come through later Sīrah and genealogy. A proclamation that challenged sacred objects and inherited status also threatened the loyalties through which clans maintained protection and authority. In Yathrib, meanwhile, the aftermath of conflicts such as Buʿāth provided a different political setting. The earliest instruction would begin in Muḥammad's household, where personal confidence and protection mattered before public authority existed.

Ḥirāʾ, Khadījah and the beginnings of a teaching community

Muḥammad grew up in the clan of Banū Hāshim, without his father, and after his mother's death under the care of his grandfather and then his uncle Abū Ṭālib. He married Khadījah, whose household joined affection, economic security and social standing. In middle life he withdrew at intervals to Ḥirāʾ, away from the crowded obligations of Makkah. There the first revelation confronted him with a command to recite and the knowledge of a Creator who teaches. He returned shaken to Khadījah, who listened, comforted him and sought counsel from Waraqah ibn Nawfal. Khadījah’s assurance drew on her long knowledge of his conduct and established the first intimate setting in which the experience was received and discussed. Revelation continued, and the new summons took shape through shared prayer, recitation, learning and the practical support of a small circle. ʿAlī, Zayd, Abū Bakr and others entered that circle through differing relationships to the household. In those gatherings believers learned to pray, give to others and govern their conduct in expectation of judgment. When the message moved into public hearing, the circle would be exposed to the protections and pressures of Quraysh's clans. Its beginnings in a household helped it survive those pressures.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

ʿĀʾishah's report in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (no. 3) recounts the first revelation, Khadījah's reassurance and Waraqah's recognition of the prophetic encounter. Khadījah's response is more than a gesture of reassurance: she names traits of conduct that can be tested in ordinary social relationships—kinship, hospitality, truthfulness and aid to the burdened. The report connects an extraordinary encounter to recognizable moral character without making a private experience into a publicly verifiable event of the same kind as a dated treaty. The initial call in Qurʾān 96:1–5 brings recitation, creation and knowledge together; passages such as 74:1–7 connect warning with purification and patience. Different reports arrange the earliest revelations and conversions differently. The surviving reports do not establish one unambiguous chronological order among the first converts.

There is an intellectual distinction between the event of revelation and the social process by which people learned to trust its messenger. The former, within Muslim belief, is God's address to the Prophet; the latter involves repeated teaching, memory, recognition of moral conduct and decisions taken by particular persons. Khadījah's role links those orders without merging them. Her reassurance addresses the Prophet personally, but the qualities she cites later become intelligible to others as grounds for trust. Similarly, an early follower's prayer is an act of worship and at the same time a repeated practice through which a shared form of life becomes recognizable.

Khadījah appealed to Muḥammad's established conduct, while Waraqah connected the encounter with earlier prophecy. In Bukhārī's opening revelation report, Khadījah's reassurance appeals to observable qualities of Muḥammad's conduct, while Waraqah places the encounter within remembered prophecy. That is not the same as independent archival corroboration of the private experience at Ḥirāʾ. In the transmitted account, Khadījah first reassured Muḥammad through her knowledge of his character, and Waraqah subsequently related his encounter to earlier prophecy. Khadījah, members of the household and other early believers heard the recitations, learned practices of worship and formed the first circle of instruction.

The transition from a revealed word to a growing community required human processes: someone had to hear it, learn its recitation, recognize the teacher's trustworthiness, practise prayer and make a judgment about allegiance. Khadījah's home, Abū Bakr's friendships and other networks of instruction each helped that process. Yet the new allegiance would eventually alter the meaning of those older ties. A believer might remain a son, client or merchant while refusing a form of worship expected by his household. That friction grew as teaching moved beyond familiar households into a public proclamation demanding changes in conduct.

The Meccan proclamation: worship, reckoning and new loyalties

As recitation reached people beyond the first household, it summoned them to worship one God and to answer for their lives after death. Wealth and ancestry could not secure a person's standing before God; powerful households now faced obligations toward slaves, orphans, debtors and hungry neighbours. Such teaching challenged ordinary habits of speech, possession, giving and judgment as well as established sanctuary rites. Quraysh's response was uneven. Some men and women of leading families accepted the call; others feared dishonouring ancestors, losing authority or unsettling inherited relationships. Those without a protective clan suffered most directly: Bilāl, the family of Yāsir and other vulnerable believers became known through reports of coercion. The new community learned endurance through circumstances that did not equally threaten all its members. Prayer, recitation, gifts and emancipation offered ways to practise new loyalties before the believers possessed collective political power. They could help and teach one another, but could not yet guarantee the security of everyone who joined them. When persecution made such vulnerability intolerable, some sought protection across the Red Sea. The movement's first institutional problem was therefore not how to govern an empire but how a community of conscience could preserve worship and people when its opponents controlled local protection.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Meccan revelation joins resurrection and judgment to demands for generosity, truthfulness and care for the vulnerable. Passages such as Qurʾān 81:1–14; 90:11–18; 92:5–21 and 107:1–7 join eschatological accountability to care for the vulnerable and critique ostentatious worship. The Meccan proclamation did not prohibit wealth or commerce as such; it demanded truthfulness, generosity and accountability before God in their pursuit and use. Recurring oaths, warnings, questions and scenes of the Last Day gave believers memorable language for examining daily conduct.

This is also why outward reforms and the formation of the heart should not be split into separate 'social' and 'spiritual' histories. A person asked to free a slave must relinquish property; a person asked to give food must resist retaining it; a person commanded to endure mockery must discipline anger. The practices do not prove that every participant attained the virtue intended, but they identify occasions in which character was tested and cultivated. When later authors discuss malakah, intention or purification, they supply analytical language for such processes. Later writers developed theories of habit and virtue around practices that early believers already undertook: giving, prayer, restraint and endurance.

When the proclamation made generosity and accountability obligatory, its claim reached the actual power relationships of a city. The master who owned a slave, the guardian handling an orphan's property and the rich merchant dispensing or withholding support faced newly articulated duties. The proclamation challenged exploitation, but Meccan masters and patrons continued to wield coercive power. The historical effect in Makkah was first the formation of a community that judged such conduct by different standards, often without the power to enforce them.

Later ethical writers such as al-Ghazālī describe the formation of stable dispositions through repeated action, examination of intention and correction of appetite. This offers a useful account of how practices may form character, but it is a later analytical reconstruction. Al-Ghazālī developed his technical account of moral psychology centuries after the earliest converts practised prayer, giving and patient endurance. Historically, the relevant evidence for persecution comes largely from transmitted reports about Bilāl, the family of Yāsir and other early believers; severity, chronology and the details of speeches need individual examination. Their different social protections made a common religious commitment costly in unequal ways. As persecution intensified and clan protection failed some believers, migration across the Red Sea offered an alternative source of safety.

Aksum: faith before political safety

Opposition in Makkah could not be met by one response, because the believers did not possess equal means of protection. Some were shielded by respected relatives; others were subjected to pressure, confiscation or violence. Groups therefore crossed the Red Sea to Aksum, where a Christian ruler was remembered as giving them refuge despite Meccan efforts to secure their return. The migrants had left their familiar homes but had not yet established a new Muslim polity. They lived under the justice and authority of another ruler, preserving worship and fellowship while explaining their beliefs to people of a different religious tradition. In Makkah the Prophet continued to teach despite increasing pressure on Banū Hāshim and his supporters. Separated from the believers in Makkah, the emigrants maintained allegiance to the Prophet through trust and reports carried across the Red Sea. Aksum demonstrated that believers could survive beyond the reach of Meccan clan sanctions, but it did not solve the problem of how the Prophet's mission might become public and secure within Arabia. That problem would return with force when the Prophet lost two of his closest protectors.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The Aksum episode is usually narrated through Ibn Isḥāq as preserved by Ibn Hishām and other Sīrah collections. The dialogue reported between Jaʿfar ibn Abī Ṭālib and the Negus illuminates how later transmitters understood the Muslim–Christian encounter, especially the claims made about ʿĪsā and Maryam. It must not automatically be treated as a verbatim transcript. The religious setting is nonetheless plausible and important: Aksum was a Christian kingdom with Red Sea connections, and Arabia's western shore participated in a network wider than Quraysh.

Aksum also clarifies a difference between spiritual steadfastness and political capability. Perseverance may enable an individual to withstand loss, but it does not create a lawful jurisdiction under which his children can live. The migrants needed a ruler willing to refuse extradition and a place where worship could continue; those conditions were supplied by a polity not organized around their own community. The emigrants remained loyal to the Prophet while living under the Negus's protection across the Red Sea. Yathrib later supplied a different combination: local believers not only offered protection but undertook responsibilities of a common public order. Confusing these two forms of security obscures the development of the Hijrah.

The emigrants in Aksum could worship under the protection of a Christian sovereign without possessing jurisdiction over their hosts. That institutional difference matters more than anachronistic claims about modern citizenship or interfaith policy. The transmitted exchange before the Negus places Jesus and Mary at the centre of the emigrants' explanation of their faith; later Sīrah accounts preserve differing details of the speeches. Yathrib's pledges would introduce a new kind of protection—shared responsibility for defence and settlement within Arabia.

The institution of asylum mattered because it shifted the conditions of survival. Makkah's protection was commonly personal and tribal; in Aksum, the migrants depended upon a foreign sovereign who was not part of their kinship system. They did not have to become citizens of a modern pluralist state for the episode to show a real form of interreligious coexistence. The ḥadīth and Sīrah materials should be checked for the number and dating of the migrations and their participants. The spiritual meaning of hijrah as leaving what distances one from God was developed in devotional interpretation, but the historical movement also involved parents, spouses, property and the uncertainty of return. Their experience prepares the later Hijrah without making the two migrations institutionally identical.

The long report from ʿĀʾishah through ʿUrwah and al-Zuhrī in al-Bukhārī 3905 also preserves a narrower Meccan episode: Abū Bakr received the protection of Ibn al-Daghinah, was initially permitted to worship privately, and then drew public attention by reciting in his courtyard. The Hijrah: neighbours, newcomers and a shared order

The migrants reached Yathrib with their religious fellowship intact but without much of the property, kin protection or livelihood that had supported them in Makkah. Their hosts cultivated dates, lived among fortified neighbourhoods and remained bound by obligations to their own families and allies. The Aws and Khazraj had a history of fighting, while Jewish groups possessed land and separate alliances. The Prophet founded a mosque for prayer, instruction and public consultation, and bonds of assistance connected emigrants with local believers. Sharing food and resources eased immediate hardship, but a town could not be governed by occasional generosity alone. Agreements made with participating groups addressed compensation for deaths, ransom, expenditure in defence and the security of allies. Existing kin groups continued to bear recognizable duties; these were now coordinated under wider commitments so that a claim or attack need not restart a private feud. Groups named as Jews retained their religious difference within the obligations actually specified in the surviving agreements. Madinah thus provided the migrants something Aksum had not: they participated in a community able to make common decisions, adjudicate disputes and defend its arrangements. As prayer and recitation became ordinary features of town life, marriage, debt, inheritance and trade presented further occasions for guidance.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The key phrase in the Medinan document is the designation of a single community, umma wāḥida, alongside clauses retaining the emigrants and several groups of Yathrib within their existing arrangements of blood compensation and captive ransom. Ibn Hishām and Abū ʿUbayd preserve versions of the Medinan agreements; Michael Lecker compares their clauses, parties and transmission in The Constitution of Medina (2004, with later revisions). Saïd Amir Arjomand and other scholars argue over its composition and the distribution of its parts. The witnesses should be collated before assigning every clause one date. The term “constitution” is convenient but potentially misleading: the transmitted text is a set of concrete obligations, not a modern theory of equal citizenship or centralized sovereignty. Participating groups assumed specific responsibilities for blood compensation, ransom of captives and contributions to common defence.

These provisions turned commitments of mutual aid into specified duties between groups that already possessed their own obligations. A believer can be generous toward a newcomer today but not tomorrow; an agreed rule about compensation and mutual aid makes some duties predictable and adjudicable. Neither law nor generosity replaces the other. Where kin groups retained responsibility for ransom and blood money, the agreement did not eliminate their internal authority; it directed their activity into a wider protected association. The Prophet was teaching worship while resolving issues that required enforceable cooperation. The Medinan community linked shared worship to concrete public duties of compensation, ransom, defence and adjudication.

Lecker's 2004 study and substantially updated 2024 edition warn against treating the transmitted kitāb as a modern constitution or assuming that every Jewish group in Madinah signed it. In the published text the emigrants and several Yathrib groups continue their customary blood-money and ransom responsibilities even as a wider umma is asserted. This combines continuity of local kin obligations with wider public coordination. The agreements retained named clan duties while placing them within wider obligations of mutual protection.

The social problem was acute. An emigrant had surrendered the protection and resources of his old household; an Anṣārī who helped him still had children, farms and obligations to relatives. Material generosity was therefore a test of allegiance, while the mosque made collective worship and teaching a regular practice. Qurʾān 59:8–9 describes the deprived emigrants and the hosts who preferred them despite their own need. The passage praises mutual assistance, while the transmitted Medinan agreements specify separate obligations for participating clans and allied groups. The Prophet's leadership united teaching with adjudication, but its historical forms remained responsive to local conditions. Public judgment increasingly concerned family obligations, property and markets, matters no less important to the community's development than warfare. The inner and outer are linked here in a demonstrable way: a disposition toward generosity mattered because real people needed food and shelter, while public obligations made mutual assistance more reliable than generosity alone.

The document's wording imposes a useful restraint on our terminology. A phrase designating a single community stands next to recurrent clauses retaining named clans' earlier compensation obligations. The legal relationship is one of enlarged coordination, not a documented abolition of kin groups. Equally, the Jewish clauses cannot be read as proof that every Jewish group of the oasis had accepted precisely the same terms. Lecker's study argues for narrower participation and separately negotiated arrangements; the recensions require collation before any one clause can be dated with certainty. Book III — Madinah: how a religious fellowship became a public community · 13 / 46

Worship takes shape in the routines of a town

In Madinah religious allegiance entered the rhythm of domestic and public life. Congregational prayer gave the day repeated points of assembly; fasting marked a common season of restraint and provision; almsgiving directed wealth toward those in need; the orientation of prayer toward the Kaʿbah joined the new community to the sanctuary from which many had been expelled. Families asked about marriage, divorce, inheritance, household conflict and care for dependants. Traders needed rules for exchange, borrowing, debt and truthful dealing. A judgment in one household might affect a wider kin group, while relief for a poor newcomer could involve resources held by a prosperous neighbour. The Prophet's instruction therefore appeared in recitation, example, consultation and decisions arising from particular situations. People did not receive a completed legal encyclopedia at the Hijrah. Obligations developed as the community encountered circumstances that called for guidance. A person who fasted and prayed also bought and sold, inherited property, resolved anger and supported relatives. These were not unrelated occupations: the same allegiance was to govern the believer's conduct when worship ended and when private interests clashed.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Medinan rulings developed at different times; tafsīr, ḥadīth and Sīrah preserve evidence for their sequence, though not every date is secure. Qurʾān 2:142–150 concerns the direction of prayer; 2:183–187 fasting; 4:7–12 inheritance; 2:282 debts; 4:35 familial arbitration. These passages have different occasions and literary forms. Not every later fiqh rule can be assigned directly to an identifiable day in Madinah. What can be shown is how the community's economic and domestic life continually generated questions for religious judgment. Daily prayer and fasting structured believers' time and repeatedly demanded attention, restraint and remembrance, although individual conduct still varied.

A marriage rule, for example, joins several orders: the parties' intentions, their consent and responsibilities, the property consequences of a union, the obligations of kin and the community's means of resolving disputes. Likewise, a rule about debts depends on trust, recording, witnesses and a procedure if someone defaults. Worship and commerce intersect because the same person must judge how to act in both, not because fasting automatically settles an unpaid loan. This helps later usūl al-fiqh make sense. The jurist's task is to draw from transmitted guidance the rules applicable to cases that recur in changing social forms, while the spiritual teacher asks what dispositions enable those rules to be lived rightly.

A marriage dispute or unpaid debt could bring households with competing loyalties before a shared authority whose judgment affected both parties. Qurʾān 2:282 articulates recording and witnessing for deferred debt, while 4:35 addresses arbitration in marital discord. Those passages do not establish that every Medinan transaction followed a uniform later fiqh textbook. They reveal why new public obligations required habitual instruction, practical judgment and means of recording claims.

A later example helps explain the system without retrojecting its terminology. Al-Ghazālī organized the Iḥyāʾ into worship, customary dealings, destructive traits and saving virtues. He could do so because ritual acts, everyday conduct and the condition of the heart had long been treated as related objects of religious instruction, even though his precise psychological scheme was eleventh-century. The Prophet's household reports and practical judgments are thus important historical evidence in addition to public campaigns. Conflict with Quraysh soon made the same problems of property, trust, discipline and judgment matters of military survival.

Badr: conflict tests allegiance and public judgment

Leaving Makkah had not ended the confrontation with Quraysh. The emigrants had lost property and protection, Meccan trading parties still passed through routes near Madinah, and both sides saw the other's growing alliances as a threat. In the second year after the Hijrah, a confrontation connected with a Quraysh caravan led to battle near Badr. A smaller Muslim force defeated an army sent from Makkah, killing important Quraysh leaders and taking captives. Victory gave the new community standing it had not possessed before, but also brought decisions that a circle of persecuted believers had scarcely faced: how to deal with prisoners, what to do with goods taken in war, and how to distribute the benefits and burdens of fighting. The victory strengthened Madinah's position while intensifying Quraysh's resolve to retaliate. Participation in common defence required believers to accept command and share risks with people outside their immediate kin. Those demands could not be settled by enthusiasm alone; they called for rules, judgment and forms of trust strong enough to survive the reversals that followed.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The Qurʾān discusses Badr in passages including 3:123–127 and Sūrat al-Anfāl, which addresses fear, victory, captives, spoils and cohesion. Detailed troop movements, numbers and dialogues belong mainly to the later maghāzī tradition; they require comparison of versions, especially Ibn Isḥāq's material and relevant hadith. A battle's broad occurrence does not license treating each remembered speech as independently verified. Badr's importance as an institution-forming event is clearer when we ask who now bore responsibilities that had previously been limited to kin groups: commanders coordinated armed men from several backgrounds; the community faced captives and property disputes; alliances elsewhere in Arabia were affected by the perceived result.

Captivity offers a sharp case of institutional change. Before a public community could hold prisoners, conflicts of capture or ransom might be managed primarily through family or tribal retaliation. After Badr, the Prophet and the believers confronted prisoners from powerful Meccan households, whose treatment affected diplomacy and future exchange. Spoils raised related disputes among fighters who had taken different risks. These were not abstract problems added by later jurists: the community was learning to assign claims when common military action produced consequences beyond any one family's control. Later law systematized matters already encountered here, while the Qurʾānic address contested pride and entitlement arising from victory.

In Qurʾān 8 the aftermath of Badr is not celebrated merely as successful fighting. Disputes over spoils and the treatment of captives bring human desires into the immediate interpretation of events. That permits one to connect personal formation to public judgment without claiming that believers were uniformly free of rivalry. The Qurʾānic passages address fear, victory, captives and spoils; later maghāzī reports supply fuller speeches and troop counts whose transmission differs.

The spiritual dimensions should not be attached as a detachable moral after the report. The participants faced fear, conflicting expectations and the temptation to read success as personal entitlement. Qurʾān 8 addresses disputes over spoils and requires obedience and collective responsibility after victory. Ibn Khaldūn's later analysis of solidarity offers one angle on how a threatened group could act collectively; revelation, shared worship and the Prophet's authority provide the actors' own grounds of commitment. Quraysh returned the next year with an army seeking revenge. Near Uḥud the Muslims initially held their ground, but the departure of some archers from an assigned position exposed them to attack; the Prophet was wounded and Ḥamzah was killed. The loss forced the community to confront how desire for gain and uncertainty about command could undo its common effort. A larger coalition later threatened Madinah. Defenders excavated a trench to deny the attackers an easy approach and endured a siege in which supplies, labour and the reliability of allies mattered as much as courage. The war also sharpened tensions among groups bound by different agreements in and around the town. Banū Qaynuqāʿ and Banū al-Naḍīr had been expelled in earlier conflicts. After the Trench, Saʿd ibn Muʿādh pronounced judgment concerning Banū Qurayẓah, with executions and captivity described in the transmitted accounts. By the end of these campaigns, Madinah had learned to defend itself through planning, common labour and coordinated action. It had also become clear that agreements, religious allegiance and ordinary kinship could pull individuals and groups in different directions under danger.

Khaybar, the deferred pilgrimage and changing relations beyond Madinah

The truce with Quraysh opened space for action elsewhere. A campaign against Khaybar ended with Muslim control over its fortified settlements. Its Jewish cultivators continued working the land under an arrangement giving them half the produce, enabling agricultural life to continue after the change of authority. The settlement required decisions about land, labour and income that were more complicated than distributing movable spoils after battle. The following year the Muslims performed the pilgrimage visit previously denied to them, entering Makkah for a limited period under the negotiated agreement. The journey reunited the emigrants with the sanctuary and demonstrated that the treaty's terms could be observed. Other envoys and delegations entered the Prophet's growing circle of relations, and people outside Madinah had to decide whether to form alliances, resist, or acknowledge his authority while maintaining aspects of local life. The community was no longer concerned only with sheltering its own emigrants. It increasingly needed to govern relationships with towns and agricultural communities whose wealth, commitments and religious identities differed from its own. Those arrangements would become more extensive once Quraysh's treaty network broke down.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The Khaybar traditions are distributed across hadith, maghāzī and legal sources; Bukhārī's chapters on cultivation and the reports about the Prophet's dealings with Khaybar's inhabitants are important. Exact terms and subsequent changes should be compared, especially where later jurists drew rules about land and crop-sharing from the precedent. Khaybar's agricultural settlement preserved cultivation while transferring control over land revenues. Agricultural land cannot be treated as a bag of captured goods: cultivation requires labour, access to water, seasonal effort and some expectation about shares. Continuing arrangements therefore put the new authorities in relation to people who did not all become part of the same religious household.

The agricultural dimension also gives the later Islamic law of landholding a historical problem to solve. Crop-sharing is a continuing relationship, not a one-time transaction: access to fields, division of produce and the labour of cultivators must be sustained season after season. New authority could enforce an arrangement while relying upon the knowledge of existing cultivators. The deferred pilgrimage instead tested whether Quraysh and the Muslims would honour the agreed truce and access arrangements. Placing the episodes together is useful only if their distinct effects are explained: one enlarged the community's fiscal responsibilities, the other demonstrated the public credibility of negotiated commitments.

A tangible contrast helps the two episodes belong together. The deferred ʿumrah tested whether public promises about access to the sanctuary would be honoured; the Khaybar agreement concerned agricultural labour over repeated harvests. Bukhārī 2329 transmits Ibn ʿUmar's report of an arrangement for half the produce of fruit or crops, with later provisions for the Prophet's wives also narrated. The original arrangement and its later administration should be separated. Khaybar involved continued cultivation under an agricultural arrangement; the deferred pilgrimage depended on a negotiated agreement with Quraysh.

The deferred pilgrimage under the treaty also had a distinct function. It was an embodied return to a sanctuary that had shaped Meccan worship and identity, now undertaken by a community whose collective commitments had changed. Religiously it confirmed the continuity of the Kaʿbah with Abrahamic worship; politically it showed that an agreement could restrain old enemies while allowing public rites. Later narratives sometimes run treaty, Khaybar and pilgrimage together, but their institutional consequences should remain distinguishable. The linked expansion of alliance and obligation made the eventual breach between Quraysh-associated and Muslim-associated tribes especially consequential.

The reported agreement allowed local cultivators to work the land in return for a stipulated share of its produce. Ibn ʿUmar's report in al-Bukhārī 2331 and 4248 says that Khaybar's Jewish cultivators were to work the land for half of its produce. The two versions pass through the same Ibn ʿUmar–Nāfiʿ line, so their agreement is not independent documentary corroboration. The return to Makkah and the incorporation of Arabia

When a conflict between allied tribes broke the arrangements of al-Ḥudaybiyyah, the Prophet marched toward Makkah in 630 with a force large enough to limit organized resistance. He entered the city, the idols in the Kaʿbah were removed, and the sanctuary's worship was redirected to the one God. Meccan families who had opposed the mission now faced a ruler who was also their kinsman; accounts of the entry emphasize grants of security and acts of reconciliation, though particular exceptions and incidents require careful reading. The new position did not make every Arabian allegiance secure. A coalition including Hawāzin confronted the Muslims at Ḥunayn, and campaigns, settlements and distribution of property followed. Delegations came to Madinah from different regions, while teachers, collectors and appointed officials carried instructions beyond the Prophet's immediate presence. The challenge changed with the scale of allegiance: people needed to know how to pray, contribute, settle claims and maintain obligations after an envoy departed. The mission that had begun in a single household now involved a network of communities and local leaders, whose integration depended upon teaching and administration as well as military strength.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The conquest is discussed in Qurʾān 110 and in hadith and Sīrah accounts; the Ḥunayn reversals in 9:25–27. The exact numbers, speeches and scope of amnesty are not all equally secure. The principal Muslim accounts describe Muḥammad's entry into Makkah and removal of idols; reports about individual exceptions differ and require separate evaluation. The Meccan sanctuary was not discarded: it was redefined within the worship taught by the Prophet. That mattered because pilgrimage, kin memory and the practical gathering of tribes were already rooted in it.

Integration also depended on the limits of central presence. A new Muslim household in a distant region could not consult the Prophet about every prayer, marriage or commercial dispute. Envoys and local teachers therefore had to communicate a core of practice while learning what questions the community actually faced. Tax collection brought another kind of encounter: people had to know the amount, purpose and authority of payment, and collectors had to be trusted not to abuse their power. Religious identity could expand rapidly after military victory, but the competence to live that identity required sustained instruction. After the Farewell Pilgrimage, the Companions carried the Prophet's instructions into communities that had not witnessed his conduct directly.

Incorporation is not an event completed by the removal of idols. After entry into Makkah, teachers, delegates and collectors had to make ritual competence and recurrent payments possible in regions distant from the Prophet. Newly allied groups could acknowledge the Prophet's authority before teachers and revenue agents established regular religious practice; formal allegiance alone did not establish inward conviction. The exact accounts of amnesty, individual exceptions and numbers of troops require report-level comparison.

Once many groups entered new relations, instructions required carriers. An envoy could convey belief and prayer, but regular contributions and disputes called for local collectors, teachers and judgments. The Prophet's dealings with delegations thus show the beginnings of a transmission problem: guidance could not depend indefinitely upon every person travelling to Madinah. Religious language, social custom and economic arrangements varied across Arabia. The Farewell Pilgrimage would give a public and widely witnessed example of worship while drawing attention to common obligations; the Prophet's subsequent death would make questions of transmission and authority unavoidable.

The Farewell Pilgrimage and the responsibilities that survived the Prophet

At the final pilgrimage the Prophet returned publicly to the rites of the sanctuary that had once been closed to his followers. Believers from different districts saw him perform the pilgrimage and heard instructions that joined worship to obligations toward other people. The rites ordered movement between Makkah, ʿArafāt, Muzdalifah and Minā; the address emphasized the inviolability of life and property, duties within families and the ending of inherited exploitative practices. Delegates and teachers carried these teachings to places whose inhabitants could not all attend a gathering in Madinah. The Prophet's household transmitted much that public assemblies could not preserve, including private worship, speech, conduct with children and the treatment of dependants. In his final illness, prayer and concern for the community continued while companions faced the prospect of his absence. When he died in 632, the revelation would not be renewed through another prophet. Worship, property obligations, local judgments and the communication of his teaching nevertheless continued. The community had to distinguish tasks previously united in one life: governing, preserving recitation and reports, issuing judgments and learning how to act. Different arrangements for those tasks became major features of subsequent Islamic civilization.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The pilgrimage's actions are preserved through differing Companions, notably the extended report of Jābir in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Book of Pilgrimage, no. 1218 in common numbering. Qurʾān 5:3 is linked in the received tradition to the completion of religion, while legal details arise through multiple reports and juristic comparisons. Versions of the Farewell Sermon differ in wording, while repeatedly addressing the sanctity of life and property, inherited abuses and obligations between members of the community. The historical significance of embodied instruction is strong: when an act is publicly demonstrated before witnesses, its later transmission can draw upon what people saw as well as what they heard. But witnesses differ in attention and memory, explaining why later scholars examined reports and reconciled apparent discrepancies.

The later multiplication of disciplines is easier to understand if we first separate the questions. What did the Prophet teach or do? Who witnessed it? Which of those actions binds others, and under what conditions? Who has authority to judge a dispute now? What dispositions make the instruction inwardly fruitful? Hadith criticism addresses the reliability of transmission; jurisprudence its normative application; political theory the government of the community; spiritual instruction its realization in character. In the Prophet's lifetime these functions met in a single authoritative person, though many companions already served as teachers or agents. His death therefore did not create knowledge out of nothing, but changed how different kinds of authority were accessed and coordinated.

The Prophet's death made an analytical distinction unavoidable. His authority included receiving revelation, teaching its meaning, judging disputes, leading worship and directing communal affairs. The end of prophecy did not end any of the community's practical needs, yet the functions could not all be inherited in the same manner. Sunni accounts emphasize the formation of the caliphate and the wider transmission of knowledge by the Companions; Imāmī accounts place particular interpretive authority in the divinely designated Imams. Sunni and Shiʿi accounts of the Prophet's final instructions later informed distinct understandings of political leadership and religious authority. Al-Ghazālī, Shādhilī authors and Akbarian thinkers later explored prophetic inheritance as the formation of character or realization of spiritual wisdom. These are serious retrospective interpretations, not evidence that the first assembly at Saqīfah already used their technical distinctions. The demands of leadership and expansion made these distinctions urgent long before jurists and spiritual authors gave them their later technical forms.

The Farewell Pilgrimage provides a public model of worship, but a great deal of the Sunnah reached later generations through domestic life and instruction outside large gatherings. Reports through ʿĀʾishah, Umm Salamah and others matter particularly for private conduct and interpretation of what the Prophet intended. Later scholars' distinctions between common practice, judicial rulings and prophetic particularities are analytical responses to the differing settings of those reports; they are not a ready-made classification uttered at the final pilgrimage.

The later question of succession arises because different functions performed by the Prophet could be transmitted in different ways without implying that revelation continued. A transmitted report could preserve his practice; a judge could apply it; a teacher could cultivate virtues exemplified in it; political leadership required a decision about public allegiance and coercive power. The Sunni and Shiʿi discussions of rightful leadership should be set at the relevant early chapters without equating them with the entire history of spiritual inheritance. Later technical accounts of walāyah must be traced through their own authors and texts (Dossier 7).

After the Prophet: allegiance and the survival of obligations

After the Prophet's death the Anṣār met at the Saqīfah of Banū Sāʿidah to consider leadership; Abū Bakr and other leading emigrants joined the discussion, and he received allegiance, followed by broader acceptance. The Prophet's family had not taken part in the initial meeting, and disagreement over leadership and certain property claims remained. Meanwhile, some Arabian groups questioned obligations that had bound them to Madinah, disputed zakāt payments or followed rival claimants to prophecy. Abū Bakr insisted that the public obligations established during the Prophet's life were not dissolved by his death. The campaigns of the Ridda, including the fighting against Musaylimah at al-Yamāmah, restored control over much of Arabia at considerable cost. The same period stimulated concern for preserving the recited text and for maintaining officials and commanders across distant communities. Abū Bakr's caliphate lasted only two years, but it settled an immediate question of government in practice: a common religious life would require leadership capable of acting after the Messenger's death. It did not settle every later theological question about who possessed rightful interpretive and spiritual authority.

Conquest and the problems inherited from older empires

Abū Bakr had struggled to maintain the obligations of communities already associated with Madinah. Under ʿUmar, armies moving beyond Arabia confronted the established powers of Syria, Iraq and Egypt. The battles at al-Yarmūk and al-Qādisiyyah brought military victory, but governing the conquered lands demanded skills unlike those needed to win them. Towns still required grain; fields still needed irrigation; churches, monasteries and village communities had their own leadership; tax officials understood accounts in Greek, Coptic or Persian. Soldiers expected payment and the people who remained on the land expected to cultivate it. The new rulers could not make all these arrangements anew. They negotiated submissions, retained many local officials and gathered revenue through modified versions of older systems. Garrison settlements, especially al-Baṣrah, al-Kūfah and al-Fusṭāṭ, kept substantial bodies of troops together without dispersing them among every village. Victory thus brought the Muslims into daily responsibility for populations whose religious affiliations, languages and economic practices had not changed with their governors. These responsibilities created the setting in which later debates about land, taxation, public justice and the rights of non-Muslim communities became unavoidable.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Conquest gave commanders territory, but administering that territory required decisions about cultivators, local officials, soldiers and revenue. Armies could acquire territorial authority in a campaigning season; provisioning troops and assessing land required continuing negotiation with cultivators, notables and former officials. Al-Balādhurī's Futūḥ al-buldān preserves accounts of treaties and settlements, but details vary across recensions and may reflect subsequent fiscal controversies. Administrative papyri and coins offer independent controls on gradual changes in government and language. Muslim governors adapted some existing fiscal practices and altered others; the conquered provinces did not immediately share one uniform tax code. The Syrian, Iraqi and Egyptian arrangements followed different trajectories.

The division between garrison and countryside helps explain why the conquerors' language and legal practices could spread without immediately making the subject population Muslim. Tax resources went to military stipends and government, while established cultivators and religious institutions continued many activities. The eventual change of language, conversion and local custom therefore took generations and differed by region. This is where the question of just administration began to be pursued beyond personal adjudication in Madinah: the ruler had to decide how much to levy, who possessed land, which agreements were binding and how to restrain troops. The moral language of justice was inherited from the Prophetic teaching, but its application now involved a more complex set of agents and documents. Hugh Kennedy and the New Cambridge History of Islam document the variation among conquest settlements; surviving treaty reports do not preserve one uniform agreement.

Dated receipts and correspondence from Egypt preserve administrative practices that continued or changed under Muslim rule. A landholder and a soldier encountered power differently: the soldier needed regular provisions; the cultivator needed a predictable demand and protection from arbitrary seizure. Their interaction connected military policy to harvest cycles and irrigation. Nor is the word jizyah consistently used for one identical early fiscal arrangement in all sources; terminology and practice need local comparison. This matters to the later juristic tradition because arguments about the rights of cultivators and protected communities emerged in the aftermath of real negotiated settlements, some remembered through legalizing retrospective narratives. After victory, officials collected revenue, cultivators worked the land, and commanders supplied troops; these recurring duties sustained political control beyond the battlefield.

The garrison, the treasury and the provincial community

In the new garrison towns, soldiers lived with their families, received stipends and gradually made places for merchants, freed persons, jurists and teachers. Their quarters often followed tribal affiliations; common prayer and military command therefore existed beside familiar bonds of kin and patronage. ʿUmar's registers of payment recognized differences of service and standing, and the distribution of revenue required information about households, campaigns and rights of recipients. In Iraq, keeping extensive cultivated land under continuing taxation, rather than dividing it all permanently among victorious soldiers, sustained payments beyond a single generation. Arab tribal groups had become provincial communities, but the money that sustained them came increasingly from settled agricultural populations. At the same time, new Muslims entered these communities through conversion, marriage, alliance and clientage. Their claims to social equality and fiscal treatment did not always fit the privileges of the earliest military settlers. The government's efforts to maintain cohesion created problems that accompanied the Umayyad period: who counted as a member of the ruling community, how service was rewarded, and whether the treasury belonged to an army, a dynasty or the wider body of believers.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The dīwān was a register and payment system, not the equivalent of a modern civil service or a census of all residents. Reports about its foundation and the hierarchy of stipends must be compared with later practice. Fred Donner traces the interaction of military recruitment, settlement and revenue administration during the early Islamic conquests. The precise policy on Iraqi land is known partly through retrospective juristic accounts in which allocation of conquered lands had become a legal problem.

This is an appropriate place for Ibn Khaldūn as a later interpreter: solidarity can gather and defend a military group, while durable rule depends on the ability to administer territories and revenues wider than the original solidarities. Yet he cannot substitute for the details of seventh-century stipends and provinces. A converted Persian client in al-Kūfah, a landed Christian family in Syria and an Arab soldier receiving payments did not enter the same arrangement. Soldiers, cultivators, clients, officials and scholars held different claims on revenues, property and public authority. It also explains why towns of conquest became centres of learning: they housed people who needed judgments on marriage, sale, slavery, inheritance and disputes involving communities with different practices. Students learned from Companions and successors amid these recurring problems rather than in a social vacuum.

Differences in military stipends shaped household income, prestige and competition for public resources. If an early participant in the campaigns held a recognised claim on the treasury, a later convert or client might not possess an equivalent claim even when sharing the same worship. That tension helps explain debates about the mawālī and the eventual pressures on Umayyad government. It should not be asserted that every client was excluded or every province followed one discriminatory rule. Some were wealthy and influential, and recruitment and fiscal practice changed. The closer historical explanation follows a particular garrison's register, its sources of revenue and its social composition. The relation between inherited solidarity and religious universalism becomes visible through conflicts over payment and status. It also clarifies why instruction mattered: a growing town needed means to identify valid testimony, settle competing property claims and teach new residents obligations that had developed first among a much smaller community.

Civil war and the limits of loyalty

Expansion increased the powers of provincial commanders and the resources at stake in their appointments. Complaints against ʿUthmān's government sharpened until rebels killed him in Madinah in 656. ʿAlī received allegiance while others demanded that the murderers first be punished; in the disorder no agreement existed about who could enforce that punishment or command the armies needed to do so. Fighting followed near al-Baṣrah and later at Ṣiffīn against Muʿāwiyah's Syrian forces. Arbitration, proposed to halt the conflict, divided ʿAlī's supporters and led to further violence with the Khārijites. ʿAlī was killed in 661. Al-Ḥasan then made a settlement with Muʿāwiyah, bringing an end to that phase of warfare. Supporters divided over allegiance to a caliph, responsibility for ʿUthmān's killing, obedience to commanders and the Prophet's family's claims to leadership. Those disputes survived the immediate fighting and later shaped accounts of legitimate authority, grave sin and rebellion.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The broad order of events is well attested in early Muslim historiography; the words attributed to individual participants, precise motives and details of arbitration require more caution. Al-Ṭabarī brings together reports from transmitters with different affiliations, and these are not independent eyewitness accounts merely because they appear side by side. Sunni histories recognize the leading Companions' standing while preserving accounts of disputed decisions, armed conflict and deaths among their supporters. Shiʿi accounts give the rights and designation of ʿAlī a different constitutional and theological meaning. Sunni and Shiʿi arguments about legitimate authority developed amid disputes over retaliation, military allegiance and the duties of government.

The civil war also had geographical and material mechanisms. The garrison army of Iraq and the Syrian command were organized forces with their own leadership, pay and attachments. Muʿāwiyah could rely upon a developed provincial military apparatus; ʿAlī had to govern amid divided coalitions and disputes about the killing of ʿUthmān. Later kalām arguments about a grave sinner and political obedience grew in a world that remembered those choices. Rival factions invoked justice, but disputed who possessed authority to punish ʿUthmān's killers and whether political allegiance must precede retaliation. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Balādhurī preserve differing accounts of the first fitna, especially in reported speeches and motives; their transmissions do not independently establish each participant's private intentions.

Later theological classifications of the first fitna do not establish the exact beliefs or motives of every participant in the original conflicts. The battle at Ṣiffīn and the division over arbitration help explain how political disagreement prompted debates over the relation between judgment and divine law. The outcomes of the battles do not establish that every participant held the fully developed doctrines later associated with Khārijites, Sunnis or Shiʿa. The extant speeches are transmitted through literary and partisan filters. The strongest causal account connects the troops' alliances, demands about the killing of ʿUthmān and the procedural problem of retaliation. The later theological writings can then be read as responses to the remembered crisis, with their own dates and purposes. The disputes over allegiance, retaliation and ʿAlī's authority shaped later Sunni and Shiʿi understandings of legitimate government.

Damascus, Karbalāʾ and the making of an imperial administration

Muʿāwiyah ruled from Damascus with Syrian military support and an administration able to draw on older provincial practices. His nomination of Yazīd placed hereditary succession at the centre of a dispute that the settlement with al-Ḥasan had not finally resolved. Al-Ḥusayn refused allegiance, travelled toward Kūfah after appeals from supporters, and was killed with members of his family and companions at Karbalāʾ in 680. The event gave the claims of the Prophet's household an enduring place in Muslim religious memory. Civil war continued until ʿAbd al-Malik consolidated authority. Under him and his successors, the state issued new coinage, enlarged the role of Arabic in administration and invested in prominent buildings, including the Dome of the Rock. Governors and armies extended authority toward the Iberian Peninsula, Central Asia and Sind. The resulting empire was increasingly visible as a single political order, but its provinces differed in language, law and social composition. The government could regulate revenue and command troops more readily than it could determine what every believer should accept as authoritative religious teaching.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Al-Ḥusayn and his companions were killed at Karbalāʾ; reports of speeches and exact casualty figures vary among later transmitters. The death of al-Ḥusayn and his companions is foundational to Shiʿi historical memory, while Sunni accounts also affirm the wrong and grief of the event, even as theological readings diverge. Umayyad coins, inscriptions and buildings record official policies and dynastic claims that later chronicles recount through their own traditions. Currency reform mattered because weights, taxes and payments connected distant provinces through common state symbols and procedures. Arabic administrative change was uneven, and the presence of Arabic in registers did not immediately displace Greek, Coptic and Persian from society. Schools of Qurʾānic recitation, ascetic teaching and early legal discussion grew within and sometimes against the horizons of state power. Al-Ḥajjāj's governorship and the experience of Iraqi opposition also reveal coercive dimensions of the imperial project. The distinction between the caliph's political authority and a teacher's credibility becomes more intelligible through these particular institutional pressures.

The Dome of the Rock supplies a particularly revealing contrast between official and social evidence. Its Arabic inscriptions present theological claims in a public imperial setting; its construction dates and physical form can be investigated independently of late court anecdotes. Coin inscriptions and administrative documents trace changes in how the state represented and financed itself. Yet none of these tells us what every market trader believed or how every household taught the Qurʾān. That difference matters: an empire may adopt common public signs while local traditions continue in distinct languages and social groups. In later decades the mawālī, tax burdens and provincial grievances entered contests over rule. The Abbasid revolution and a change of ruling centre

By the middle of the eighth century, the Umayyad empire possessed formidable fiscal and military resources but faced opposition among groups dissatisfied with provincial rule, status and the distribution of authority. In Khurāsān, networks of Arab settlers and others were mobilized in a movement promising rule associated with the Prophet's family. Under Abū Muslim's command, its armies advanced west and defeated the Umayyads in 750. The Abbasids became caliphs, and the centre of government moved toward Iraq. The revolution did not redistribute authority equally among its supporters: the new rulers built a dynasty with its own claims, commanders and administrators, while ʿAlid expectations persisted. Al-Manṣūr chose a new site on the Tigris for Baghdad, bringing the court closer to Iraqi agricultural revenues, eastern communication routes and the skills of Persianate secretarial households. What had begun as a contest over legitimate rule became the foundation of a metropolis where political power, commerce and scholarship would meet. The earlier garrison cities remained significant, but the new capital drew specialists from several regions and languages into a shared urban economy.

Baghdad and the work of a learned city

Baghdad drew court officials, traders, physicians, craftsmen and students to a place where goods and ideas arrived by river, road and caravan. The treasury paid armies and officials, but private households and patrons also supported books, teachers and specialized work. Physicians inherited Greek and Syriac medical learning and adapted it to new patients and institutions; mathematicians and astronomers addressed problems arising from calculation, observation and earlier sciences. Translators could work across Syriac, Greek and Arabic, while jurists and transmitters met learners who came to study accounts of the Prophet and their implications. Paper and book markets made it easier to reproduce, annotate and circulate substantial texts. Yet the city's different enquiries did not form one institution: some occurred in courts, others in mosques, private houses, bookshops or medical circles. The caliphs could sponsor scholars and also coerce them. As the city expanded, its intellectual authority depended increasingly on the quality of transmission and argument within communities of learning as well as the favour of those who ruled it.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Hārūn al-Rashīd's court and al-Maʾmūn's patronage are important, but the familiar story of a single 'House of Wisdom' producing all the sciences suppresses the multiplicity of patrons and workplaces. Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq's translation projects, al-Kindī's philosophical circle and the mathematical writing associated with al-Khwārizmī had distinct aims and constituencies. Dimitri Gutas, Greek Thought, Arabic Culture, and research on medicine and translation distinguish court translation from wider patterns of scholarly patronage. The adoption of paper expanded the possibilities for copying without making manuscripts cheap for every reader.

Baghdad gathered translators, booksellers, physicians, administrators and patrons who sustained scholarly work through different institutions. Secretarial administration required numeracy and records; medicine attracted patrons and clinical expertise; juridical differences demanded teachers able to transmit and reason; rich households could sustain independent specialists. This is the sort of joint historical explanation Hodgson was seeking when he discussed the interaction of state, merchant and learned interests. But the learned world was not always harmoniously plural. In the miḥnah, caliphal demands about the createdness of the Qurʾān brought public theology and state coercion together. Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal's refusal and later memory of the episode exemplify a struggle over the boundary between the ruler's command and a scholar's religious authority. It would be misleading either to call this a conflict between 'religion' and 'science' or to identify all speculative inquiry with official coercion.

The history of translation is most persuasive when treated through a defined task. Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq's work on Greek medical texts involved comparing copies, choosing technical equivalents and revising earlier translations; a practitioner then had to interpret the resulting Arabic work in relation to patients and other medical knowledge. The procedure differed from al-Khwārizmī's mathematical writing or a jurist's evaluation of a report, even when they shared bookshops and patrons. For this reason 'Islamic science' is a historical umbrella, not the name of one single method. During the miḥnah, caliphal authorities sponsored intellectual inquiry while pressuring scholars to affirm a particular doctrine about the Qurʾān. Intellectual life became durable through a plurality of communities capable of criticising, transmitting and sponsoring knowledge, although their relative independence varied. Individual translations passed through named patrons, scribes, translators and readers, often with several stages of revision.

Travelers, households and the authentication of transmitted learning

A student wishing to learn the Prophet's teaching could begin with a parent or a local teacher, but difficult reports often sent students to other towns. They asked who had heard a narration, compared its wording, and learned how particular authorities prayed, judged or answered questions. The collections associated with al-Bukhārī and Muslim emerged within this world of travel, questioning and written compilation. Women as well as men preserved and taught reports, beginning prominently with members of the Prophet's household and continuing through later scholarly families. These practices made religious memory portable, yet they also demanded a discipline of verification: a report's presence in a book did not alone determine what it meant or how it should be applied.

Some students transmitted from personal audition, some from reading or authorization, and scholars debated these methods. Differences among ṣaḥīḥ, ḥasan, ḍaʿīf and other classifications developed through particular critical conventions; the two canonical Ṣaḥīḥs were not produced under one uniform ancient state procedure. The introductory material in Muslim's Ṣaḥīḥ, al-Bukhārī's chapter headings and later discussions by Ibn al-Ṣalāḥ help explain the criteria. The historical date of a report and a ḥadīth critic's judgment of its acceptability answer separate questions. Harald Motzki compares chains and wording to reconstruct stages of transmission; scholars contest some of the conclusions drawn from those comparisons.

The household was not marginal to this process. ʿĀʾishah was asked about private worship, domestic conduct and the circumstances of legal pronouncements because these subjects often lay outside male public observation. Later women transmitters and teachers, documented in biographical dictionaries, were associated with scholarly households and institutions, although participation varied by region and century. Households, mosques and traveling students preserved Prophetic practice across cities without relying on a single caliphal archive. Traveling students and scholarly correspondence gradually connected distant communities while leaving room for local practices and disagreements.

Report critics compared transmissions from different cities to identify common wording, variations and possible shared sources. If two students in different cities transmitted what they said was the same Prophetic report, the critic might compare the named links in their chains, the wording of the two texts and reports about the reliability and contact of the transmitters. Agreement might reflect shared earlier transmission rather than independent corroboration; difference might arise from abbreviation, memory or a genuine alternate wording. Inclusion in a collection did not settle a report's wording, original setting or legal application. A jurist might accept a report's transmission yet debate whether an action was obligatory, recommended or context-specific. Jurists then assessed whether reliable reports established obligation, recommendation, permission or a ruling specific to one occasion. Women such as ʿĀʾishah transmitted knowledge through identifiable households and teaching relationships; their roles and opportunities varied among regions and generations.

Transmission became accountable precisely because different students could preserve different wordings or levels of detail. A particularly strong example comes from the Muwaṭṭaʾ: El Shamsy compares the recensions of Yaḥyā al-Laythī, Abū Muṣʿab and Ibn Bukayr with quotations in al-Shāfiʿī's Umm. Some variants alter only phrasing; others affect the degree of agreement a position claims, the narrator chain, or the teacher's attached explanation. Legal schools emerge from the need to decide recurrent cases

Merchants still sold on credit, spouses disputed entitlements, heirs divided estates and judges faced disagreements between neighbours. New places brought new customs and documentary practices, while transmitted guidance had to be applied to circumstances not described in identical terms by earlier reports. Teachers in Iraq, the Ḥijāz, Syria and Egypt developed methods for reaching and defending judgments. Abū Ḥanīfah's students preserved and extended Iraqi legal discussions; Mālik gathered reports and the practice of Madinan scholars; al-Shāfiʿī examined the authority of different kinds of proof; Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal became a prominent model of reliance on transmitted evidence. Over generations, their successors compared opinions, taught precedents and produced manuals. The resulting schools preserved characteristic procedures without freezing every judgment at the founder's death. They allowed communities far from the first centres to ask similar questions in a shared vocabulary, even while arriving at different rulings. Lawyers and judges thereby acquired influence independent of any single dynasty, although appointments, patronage and local social hierarchies continued to shape their work.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Disputes over sales, agency and liability required jurists to apply transmitted rulings and broader principles to new transactions. Suppose a merchant gives goods to an agent who sells to a third party, then disputes the price or liability for loss. A jurist needs to know the contractual terms, local usage, rules of agency and the relation of evidence to general principle. Competing schools may differ because they classify the transaction differently, trust different reports or apply analogical reasoning under different restrictions. The legal concepts are formed precisely to make difficult cases teachable and repeatable. Al-Shāfiʿī's al-Risālah, Mālik's Muwaṭṭaʾ, writings transmitted from Muḥammad al-Shaybānī, and early Ḥanbalī collections should be read together with later school manuals, not treated as interchangeable finished codes.

The legal schools were historically made by students, teachers and later jurists. For this reason a school's 'position' in a late manual cannot simply be attributed to its eponymous founder. Theological commitments, juristic reasoning and actual courts intersected without being identical. Jurists trained students to classify acts, resolve conflicts among reports and reason from principles when inherited formulations did not settle a new case.

A revealing manuscript-level comparison concerns Mālik's Muwaṭṭaʾ. Its transmitted recensions differ, and the version associated with Muḥammad al-Shaybānī sometimes sets Mālik's Medinan positions beside Ḥanafī objections. Teachers carried texts between cities, compared their variants and argued about the rulings attributed to earlier authorities. Later western recensions had their own histories of circulation and authority. A student who learns one line of argument about sales, inheritance or ritual practice may therefore enter a dialogue already shaped by several teachers. The school becomes a discipline when these arguments can be preserved, criticised and extended while remaining recognisably related. Wael Hallaq's histories of school formation provide a model for examining this institutional process, but individual recensions and their manuscript witnesses remain the primary controls.

One Muwaṭṭaʾ report describes Ibn ʿUmar sprinkling water in his eyes during ghusl, while recensions differ over Mālik's accompanying juristic judgment. One recension preserves the practice without Mālik's judgment; al-Shāfiʿī quotes Mālik's comment that it was not accepted practice, while two other recensions give comparable statements that it was not obligatory. The recensions distinguish Ibn ʿUmar's reported conduct, Mālik's interpretation of its legal force and the rulings attributed to later schools. Endowments, colleges and the social life of scholarship

A teacher could attract students through learning and reputation, but lessons required rooms, books, food and sometimes long years free from earning a living. Rulers, merchants and wealthy families established endowed mosques, hostels and colleges to support teachers, students, Qurʾān readers, jurists and visitors. The Niẓāmiyyah founded in Baghdad in the eleventh century became a prominent example of organized patronage. Other towns developed institutions suited to local political and family arrangements. Endowments could secure a scholar's work beyond a patron's lifetime, yet appointments to teaching chairs also became prizes in contests among households and officials. Learning thus acquired durable places while remaining dependent on personal instruction and recognition. A student still needed a teacher who could explain difficult passages and authorize their transmission; a building did not automatically certify understanding. The college, household library, mosque and Sufi lodge therefore became connected, sometimes competing settings in which law, grammar, recitation, theology and spiritual conduct were taught.

The institution of waqf connected property to an intended continuing purpose under legally defined conditions. Michael Chamberlain documents how medieval Damascene colleges operated through endowments, elite patronage, scholarly households and personal relationships rather than modern university bureaucracy. The Prophet's mosque sustained public worship and instruction; later cities with large populations required more differentiated means of financing teachers and accommodating traveling students. Translation, theological controversy and the uses of reason

Muslims confronted disagreements not only over actions but over what could be known about God, the world and human responsibility. Earlier debates about free choice, divine justice, attributes and the status of the Qurʾān developed among theologians who used argument to defend positions drawn from revelation and inherited instruction. Greek works on logic, natural philosophy and metaphysics became available in Arabic through translators working with Syriac and Greek learning. Thinkers such as al-Kindī and al-Fārābī fashioned new accounts of knowledge and its demonstration; physicians and mathematicians pursued different kinds of inquiry under the same broad literary language. Theologians and philosophers met on questions of causation, the soul and the beginning of the world, but did not necessarily mean the same thing by knowledge or proof. Al-Ashʿarī and al-Māturīdī emerged from different disputational settings and gave enduring Sunni theological vocabularies to these controversies. The encounter made the disciplines more technically precise while leaving unsettled which claims demonstration could establish and where revelation decisively guided judgment.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Philosophers and theologians disputed whether created causes act with inviolable necessity and how such causation relates to miracles and divine agency. Different answers presuppose different accounts of nature, regularity and possibility. It would be a mistake to summarize all Ashʿarīs as holding a single identical philosophy of causation or to equate the whole of falsafah with denial of miracles. The problem is debated in al-Ghazālī's seventeenth discussion of the Tahāfut, but it had a longer prehistory in kalām and philosophical accounts of nature. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's 'Occasionalism' entry gives a useful map of disagreement even about how to read al-Ghazālī's ultimate position.

Arguments about God's attributes, moral obligation and human action also arise from different sources. Early Muʿtazilī positions are not interchangeable, and Sunni Ashʿarī and Māturīdī schools later developed their own internal debates. Ibn Sīnā's distinction between essence and existence and his proof of the necessary existent entered many later theological treatises, despite strong criticism of other Avicennan doctrines. The crucial historical change is not that 'Greek reason defeated' or 'Islam rejected reason.' Methods of inquiry moved across disciplines, were adapted to particular problems, and became objects of criticism by new authors. Primary controls include al-Ashʿarī's Maqālāt, al-Māturīdī's Kitāb al-Tawḥīd, al-Fārābī's works on the sciences, Ibn Sīnā's Shifāʾ and al-Ghazālī's Tahāfut. Differences in authorship, date and formulation can change the meaning of a philosophical proposition and the objections later commentators raise against it.

The miḥnah offers an early case of theological language becoming an instrument of public authority. During the miḥnah, caliphal officials demanded declarations about the Qurʾān's createdness, making conformity to a theological position a condition of public service. Muʿtazilī theologians developed arguments about divine justice and unity beyond the court's coercive policies; later Sunni theologians also employed rigorous dialectical argument. Later Sunni theologians used intricate logical methods while rejecting the imposition of a doctrine by a caliph. The controversy involved distinct claims about divine speech, legitimate proof and the ruler's authority to enforce a doctrinal declaration. These are distinct but interacting processes.

Al-Ghazālī, al-Rāzī and the remaking of philosophical theology

As knowledge became more specialized, the scholar who mastered one discipline could no longer assume that its methods answered every question. Al-Ghazālī studied legal reasoning, theological controversy and philosophy, then examined both the power and the limits of their claims. His later teaching joined the discipline of outward action to the examination of intention and character. Ibn Rushd, writing in al-Andalus, defended particular philosophical demonstrations against objections raised in al-Ghazālī's critique. In the eastern lands, Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī interrogated Ibn Sīnā's arguments about existence, definition, knowledge and the soul; Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī later answered and reorganized parts of that critical legacy. Their works entered new teaching chains through commentary and gloss, often in the same institutions that taught grammar, law and theology. A student could therefore meet rival accounts of the human intellect and causation within a shared curriculum of religious learning. Commentators preserved rival arguments, formulated objections and replies, and taught students to test the force of a demonstration rather than assume agreement.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

These are two interacting but distinct intellectual histories. In Tahāfut al-falāsifah, al-Ghazālī objects to identifiable claims associated with the philosophers, including an argument about causation and miracle; Ibn Rushd's Tahāfut al-tahāfut responds to the critique. The disagreement cannot be reconstructed adequately by the bare sentence 'reason and revelation were reconciled' or by assigning one universal causal theory to al-Ghazālī. His Iḥyāʾ is another undertaking, concerned with the ordering of worship, everyday action, destructive motives and saving virtues. Al-Ghazālī's critique of philosophical theses and his treatment of worship and character pursued related concerns within different genres and arguments.

A second history develops through the criticism of Ibn Sīnā's al-Ishārāt. Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī uses detailed commentary to expose problems in essence, existence, definition and demonstration; al-Ṭūsī's replies help establish a continuing debate. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on al-Rāzī documents particular positions and the care needed in inferring his considered conclusions from dialectical writings. This is a more exact account of the 'post-Avicennan' tradition than a list of names. Commentaries became intellectual instruments: by setting out a lemma, objection, response and further objection, they required students to inhabit the logic of a question before accepting a resolution. That is relevant to the larger history of formation because the discipline of judgment can be cultivated through argument as well as through repeated moral actions. The achievements and failures of those pedagogic practices must still be judged from actual texts and institutions, not an idealization of scholasticism.

The dispute over essence and existence turned on what definitions establish about things outside the mind. Ibn Sīnā argues that a thing's essence can be understood without thereby affirming its existence; al-Rāzī questions what such a distinction establishes about real beings and whether a definition yields knowledge of an external essence. The reply cannot be assessed by saying merely that the later thinker 'synthesized' theology and philosophy. The relevant lemmas and objections must be located, and different formulations of al-Rāzī's own position compared before selecting one. The later classroom practice of commenting line by line preserved these difficulties for students. It also introduced a risk of treating subtle distinctions as verbal contests detached from demonstration; whether that happened is a question about particular curricula and commentaries, not a verdict on late scholasticism as a whole.

The work of the heart becomes a teachable discipline

A believer could fulfil an outward obligation and still struggle with vanity, anger, envy or attachment to reputation. The first generations had learned to examine intention through prayer, almsgiving, fasting, companionship and restraint. Teachers developed particular methods for addressing what correct outward action might leave unformed. The long conflict joined military resources, religious legitimacy and urban institutions without making the history of all those institutions reducible to crusading warfare.

Yet transmission required readers who could interpret a technical term, patrons able to finance copying and teachers who corrected mistakes. The world of shared books and spiritual idioms therefore extended beyond any single sovereign's territory. Persianate culture did not replace Arabic learning; the two frequently occupied different but related roles in the education, administration and devotion of the same persons.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Hodgson distinguishes Islam as a religion from the broader Islamicate culture of poetry, commerce and royal architecture; some authors nonetheless understood those cultural practices as means of religious realization. His categories themselves require qualification, especially where authors understood philosophy or poetry as an integral way of realizing religious truth. Shahab Ahmed argues that many Muslim authors understood philosophy, poetry and other Islamicate practices as integral to their religious lives.

Manuscript colophons, ownership records, citations and teaching certificates document particular routes by which texts passed among scholars. Niẓāmī's Persian narratives, Saʿdī's ethical works, or the legal and Sufi texts carried to South Asia are possible case studies, but their particular routes cannot be assumed from later popularity. Manuscript dates, ownership notes, colophons, reading certificates and library inventories provide stronger evidence. The intellectual consequence of bilingual learning also deserves attention: a technical Arabic discipline could be explained in Persian and eventually local vernaculars, changing who could enter a tradition without necessarily changing the tradition's formal terms. Such processes explain the civilization's adaptability more concretely than the phrase 'cultural synthesis.'

One route of inquiry is through the reconstruction of a book's movement. A Persian text copied at one court might reach India with a secretary, be read in a learned household and be cited in a later ethical manual. A documented textual route requires dated manuscripts, ownership notes, biographical notices or explicit citations; similar vocabulary alone does not establish transmission. Literary Persian also provided an intermediary language for local translations and commentaries. Students who learned Arabic legal terminology through Persian explanations could participate in a wider scholarly community while continuing to speak vernacular languages at home. Persian also mediated technical learning in administration, law and spiritual instruction among multilingual communities. It is about the practical mediation by which technical learning became teachable across regions without requiring every learner to begin with the same mother tongue.

Mongol conquest, conversion and the survival of scholarly worlds

In the thirteenth century Mongol armies destroyed cities, displaced populations and dismantled established regimes across Central and Western Asia. Baghdad was taken in 1258, ending the ruling Abbasid court there. Yet other Muslim centres survived, and new rulers needed scribes, merchants, physicians and administrators to govern the territories they had conquered. Mamluk armies stopped a Mongol force at ʿAyn Jālūt in 1260, while Cairo and Damascus continued to support judges, schools and the movement of books. In Iran, Mongol rulers first patronized several religious communities; Ghāzān's conversion to Islam in 1295 marked a major development in Ilkhanid political life. Rebuilding, new endowments and courtly histories followed among continuing wars and fiscal pressures. Older scholarly practices could thus be adopted by conquerors who had initially stood outside them. Teachers and merchants also moved along Mongol-controlled routes, even as warfare made many local livelihoods precarious. The loss of a capital did not extinguish Islamic learning, but the recovery of learning depended on surviving people, books, towns and resources, rather than on an abstract continuity immune to violence.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The difference between a dynasty's conversion and a population's religious transformation is essential. Peter Jackson's The Mongols and the Islamic World and the Encyclopaedia Iranica account of the Mongols trace the varied trajectories of the Ilkhanids, Jochids and Central Asian khanates. Aḥmad Tegüder had already ruled as a Muslim Ilkhan; Ghāzān's accession later established a more durable public Islamic orientation at the court. Rashīd al-Dīn's histories should be read in the context of his court position, alongside Mamluk and other sources. Motives given to a conqueror by an official chronicler are evidence for political representation as much as for private conviction.

The mechanism of institutional persistence is visible in Cairo and Iran. A waqf could support a school across changes of dynasty, but endowments might be ruined or reallocated; a jurist's students could copy his work even when his patron was defeated. Mongol administrators also adopted Persian fiscal and literary practices for practical reasons. An Ibn Khaldūnian model can illuminate the transition from conqueror solidarity to the difficulties of administering settled societies, while local documents decide how far the analogy holds. After the Mongol wars, scholars continued to produce works in theology, logic, astronomy and Sufi thought, although warfare altered their centres, resources and patrons.

A later chronicler who says that Ghāzān's accession brought Islam to the Ilkhanate is reporting a major change in public royal identity, not the conversion of everyone under his rule. Aḥmad Tegüder and Muslim Mongol soldiers preceded Ghāzān's accession and therefore demonstrate earlier Islamic affiliations within Ilkhanid society. Later Mongol rulers' patronage of scholars, foundations and monuments also served political purposes, but religious conviction cannot be disproved merely by identifying those interests. The contrasting Mamluk and Ilkhanid reports should therefore be compared as partisan accounts of a contested transformation. Courts, colleges, manuscripts and commercial routes carried learned and devotional practices through changing dynastic conditions. Mongol campaigns devastated cities and scholarly households, while surviving people and institutions later rebuilt teaching and textual circulation under new rulers.

The Middle Niger: markets, kings and independent scholars

South of the Sahara, Muslim merchants had long crossed deserts to exchange salt, metals, textiles and other goods. Their routes linked North African towns with settlements and courts along the Niger. Rulers sometimes welcomed Muslim traders and employed literate specialists before most of their subjects adopted Islamic practices. Mali became a powerful kingdom, and Mansa Mūsā's pilgrimage in 1324 brought its wealth and connections to wider notice. Under later Songhay rulers, towns such as Timbuktu and Jenne supported jurists, merchants, teachers and book copying. Scholars acquired standing through knowledge, family networks and their ability to mediate disputes; some sought autonomy from rulers while relying on the same commercial communities for resources. Rural devotional life, local languages and courts did not always develop at the same pace. West African Muslim scholarship therefore emerged through local institutions and debates, not merely through the arrival of books from the north. Its authors participated in wider Mālikī and Sufi conversations while writing about the needs of their own societies.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The Tārīkh al-Sūdān, learned biographies, manuscripts, archaeological evidence and reports by travellers such as Ibn Baṭṭūṭah preserve different aspects of the Middle Niger's history. Zachary Wright traces West African scholarly authority through teaching communities and inherited learned relationships, which did not depend wholly on appointment by royal courts. Northwestern University's West African Arabic manuscript collections and catalogues also make local textual production accessible.

Timbuktu supported learned families, libraries and teaching circles whose organization differed from modern universities; surviving manuscript inventories vary greatly in age and provenance. The age, provenance and content of particular libraries need separate examination. A study of 31 private libraries found variation in the age and provenance of collections, limiting claims of uninterrupted scholarly transmission across every household. The surviving libraries preserve evidence of local scholarship but differ in the periods and networks they document. Trade supported towns and literacy; rulers patronized institutions; scholars organized teaching and defended authority within those changing conditions. West African scholars used Arabic and Mālikī learning within local commercial, political and educational arrangements, adapting inherited disciplines to their own institutions.

Aḥmad Bābā (d. 1627), a West African scholar and author, offers a named route into this history. His works on Mālikī scholarship and learned biographies belong to a world that maintained ties with North Africa; his career was also affected by Moroccan intervention in Songhay and the removal of scholars from Timbuktu. Aḥmad Bābā's scholarly career and forced removal after Moroccan intervention connected the independence of learned households with struggles over political power. Yet the presence of learned authors must not be turned into an unsupported claim that every town possessed a college comparable to Cairo's, nor that all surviving manuscript collections were assembled in the same period. Their ownership and contents vary. Dated manuscripts and biographical records document particular West African scholars and libraries without establishing identical educational institutions in every town.

Across the monsoon seas: ports, households and the Malay world

Muslim merchants travelled the Indian Ocean with sailors and traders of many religions, following seasonal winds between the Red Sea, Persian Gulf, East Africa, western India and Southeast Asia. A merchant entering a port needed lodging, partners, trusted weights and a means of settling disputes; resident communities built mosques, married locally and taught their children alongside commercial work. In the fifteenth century Melaka became a major harbour where rulers protected visiting merchants, organized customs and governed a population whose residents changed with the monsoon. The ruling court's Islamic identity connected it to other Muslim sovereigns, but the port's prosperity also depended on Chinese, South Asian and island Southeast Asian exchanges. Malay-language instruction and texts in Arabic-derived scripts developed within these changing communities. Islam moved through merchants, families, teachers and courts, with different sequences in different places. The spread of shared religious practice therefore depended on practical relations that made sustained teaching and settlement possible, while local languages and institutions gave it forms unfamiliar to early Arabia.

Indian Ocean port communities linked mercantile trust, resident families, worship and instruction through sustained settlement. Trust among commercial partners is not identical to faith, but people who lived together could build prayer spaces, commission teachers and sustain scholarly ties. A port administration that protected foreigners did not by that fact produce Muslim conviction; it furnished conditions in which a community could reproduce itself. Islam's place in Malay literature, legal traditions and court ceremony must be studied through actual texts and manuscripts, not inferred solely from the label 'Muslim sultanate.' The transition from an Arabic book to Jawi instruction was likewise mediated by language, translators, scribes and patrons. Local rulers, merchants, scribes and teachers shaped how Islamic practice entered Malay institutions and vernacular texts.

Melaka's syahbandar officers, customs regulations and arrangements for visiting merchants make an excellent causal case. They helped reduce uncertainty about lodging, weighing, protection and market transactions for temporary residents. Some of these residents formed more durable households and communities in which religious learning could continue between trading seasons. But the chain from one harbour protection to one conversion is not automatic; it must be reconstructed from dated local testimonies, inscriptions, legal texts and later chronicles. Peacock's Arabic and Persian material locates Melaka within broader Muslim geographical knowledge, while Malay and Portuguese accounts supply different perspectives. Holding these kinds of evidence apart prevents commerce from being treated either as proof of spiritual transmission or as an explanation that makes spiritual teaching unnecessary.

Bengal: fields, settlements and changing religious communities

In eastern Bengal the expansion of Muslim communities did not follow the map of early military conquest. Much of the lower delta was wooded or marshy, its waterways shifting and its agricultural settlements growing at different times. As political authorities claimed territory, cultivators cleared land and organized villages; religious teachers and patrons sometimes participated in these efforts by establishing shrines, places of prayer and local charitable ties. A family newly settled in a district might become attached to a saintly household through landholding, work and inherited practice rather than through a single dramatic conversion. Muslim courts and Persianate administration operated beside Bengali language and local custom. Over generations, devotional practices, agricultural communities and political boundaries changed at different rates. These intertwined processes help explain why large Muslim populations came to inhabit eastern Bengal, including districts far from the first centres of Turkish military power. The religion acquired local continuities through settled households and remembered teachers while remaining connected to wider Muslim texts, pilgrimage and law.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Richard Eaton's The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier, 1204–1760 is the key test case. He uses inscriptions, monuments, administrative texts and local literary records to relate political, agrarian and religious frontiers without collapsing one into the others. A later essay clarifies four overlapping processes, adding the eastward movement of Sanskritic institutions to political, agrarian and religious change. These distinctions are historical hypotheses supported in particular places and periods, not a universal theory of conversion.

A rigorous microhistory would trace one named village or shrine, the associated land grant if preserved, the date and location of an inscription and subsequent changes in surrounding settlement. It would ask whether a saint's remembered career is independently dated, whether followers occupied adjacent fields, and whether a later text projects a more coherent religious community onto an earlier landscape. The spiritual question is inseparable from this context but not reducible to it: why did people regard particular teachers and places as bearers of blessing and trustworthy guidance? The archive may establish their social position without proving or disproving their inner spiritual realization. This is the sort of disciplined combination of material and religious explanation that the broader history requires.

A land grant associated with a religious figure in Bengal can carry several meanings at once: authorization to cultivate, a claim to revenue, support for a place of worship and later memory of a revered founder. Eaton's historical method asks which meanings can actually be dated from the document or its material setting. If cultivation moves into a forest frontier, the establishment of a village may alter the resources and social ties through which a religious community reproduces itself. Yet neither the clearing of land nor the grant proves a person's inward conversion. The question of conviction must be approached through additional evidence and through the limits of what such evidence can reveal. A genuine integrated history resists the equal and opposite temptations of reducing Islam to economics or treating agricultural conditions as irrelevant to its local life.

The Ottoman settlement: courts, colleges and a growing imperial household

Ottoman rulers grew from an Anatolian principality into an empire whose capital, after the conquest of Constantinople in 1453, linked the Balkans, Anatolia and the eastern Mediterranean. Military forces, taxation and palace households sustained rule over Muslim, Christian and Jewish populations. Mosques, courts, colleges and charitable foundations gave towns recognizable Islamic institutions, while local communities retained different forms of worship and internal authority. Judges applied learned Ḥanafī legal traditions alongside sultanic regulations addressing revenue, order and administration. Scholars moved through educational and judicial appointments, acquiring prospects for service that tied some of them closely to the state. The conquest of Arab provinces in the early sixteenth century added older centres of Muslim scholarship and the great pilgrimage routes to Ottoman rule. Yet the empire could not teach religious life by decree: provincial mosques, households, Sufi teachers and book circles sustained practices that sometimes supported and sometimes criticized official policy. Religious institutions both helped organize the empire and provided resources that outlived individual sultans.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The Ottoman ʿilmiyye offered a relatively structured hierarchy of appointments but cannot be equated with a single centralized clergy. Gilles Veinstein's chapter in The Cambridge History of Turkey documents how judges, mosques, Friday sermons, colleges and pious foundations became part of Ottoman governance from its early period, while scholars were originally recruited from other regions as well. Later institutions changed over time; do not project the sixteenth-century hierarchy back onto the fourteenth century.

A judge in an Ottoman town faced questions raised by actual families and markets, while sultanic qānūn regulated matters such as tax and administration. The relationship between juristic tradition and imperial rule varied by subject and province; a law code is not evidence that everyone obeyed it. Endowments supported services but also elite households. Sufi lodges and the pilgrimage connected people across frontiers, including Arab provinces with established scholarly traditions. Rulers patronized colleges and courts, but jurists, household teachers and spiritual lineages retained forms of learning that governments did not wholly control. Ordinary subjects encountered the empire through courts, markets, taxes, village administration and worship, not solely through the decrees of Constantinople.

An Ottoman court register provides a different view of the state than an imperial legal code. In a register, litigants present claims about inheritance, debts or property, and the qāḍī records settlements within a social world in which witnesses, custom and political order matter. The imperial law code reveals how the ruler intended to regulate some activities. These documents may agree or differ without either being irrelevant. A madrasa certificate, meanwhile, records a different kind of authority, that of learned transmission. The empire's durability required all three—imperial command, local adjudication and teaching—but their relations varied. Ottoman court records, the ʿilmiyye hierarchy, endowments and regional Sufi institutions document distinct and sometimes competing forms of authority.

Safavid Iran and Mughal India: rival confessional and imperial settlements

Across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, two other great states established different relationships between religious authority and government. In Iran, the Safavids made Twelver Shiʿism central to imperial identity and supported scholars able to teach its law and traditions; this process altered the religious institutions of towns and provinces over generations. In India, Mughal rulers governed populations with multiple languages, religious affiliations and local landholding arrangements. Persian served important administrative and literary functions, while legal scholars, Sufis, temples, shrines and regional elites occupied different positions within the court's world. Mughal policies varied from Akbar's experiments in courtly religious discussion and imperial inclusion to Aurangzeb's patronage of Ḥanafī legal compilation. In both empires, rulers required reliable revenue and armies, while religious teachers depended upon patrons and communities whose loyalties did not always match the state's priorities. The result was neither one universal early-modern Islamic order nor a simple division between spirituality and politics: different institutions formed through different bargains among scholarship, authority and the needs of diverse populations.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The Safavid adoption of Twelver Shiʿism was a state project with long and uneven social consequences, not an instant change in the convictions of all Iran's inhabitants in 1501. The arrival and support of scholars from regions with established Imāmī learning are part of that history. The relation of rulers to jurists also changed between Ismāʿīl I and later Safavid reigns. Mughal policies toward religious communities, revenue and scholarship differed among rulers and provinces, and changed across time. Akbar's courtly debates, revenue administration and imperial household were not interchangeable institutions; the Fatāwā-yi ʿĀlamgīrī is a major compilation whose practical reception demands separate research.

Safavid Isfahan supported philosophers and theologians including Mullā Ṣadrā, while Indian scholarly networks developed curricula in law, ḥadīth, logic and spiritual discipline. Philosophers, jurists and spiritual authors within each empire disputed one another's positions rather than expressing a uniform imperial doctrine. Shāh Walī Allāh's eighteenth-century work later connected South Asian hadith, law and spiritual concerns in a setting shaped by Mughal fragmentation. Comparative studies of the three empires should account for revenue systems, military organization, confessional policy and local communities before making large claims about decline or renewal. Safavid patronage of Twelver Shiʿi scholarship gave Iran institutions different from those sustained by Ottoman Sunni and Mughal scholarly networks.

Oceans, finance and the changing balance of power

While the great Muslim empires governed extensive territories, European states and commercial companies expanded their maritime activities. Portuguese fleets entered the Indian Ocean at the end of the fifteenth century; Dutch and English companies later established trading posts, fortified ports and systems of credit and coercion. They encountered a crowded commercial world already served by Arab, Indian, Persian, African, Armenian, Jewish and other merchants. Some Asian rulers cooperated with Europeans, others resisted them, and regional trade did not immediately become European property. Over subsequent centuries, however, Atlantic commerce, new financial institutions, industrial production and military changes altered the resources available to competing states. Ottoman, Iranian and Indian governments faced fiscal and administrative pressures that had different local histories. The problem was no longer simply that one dynasty had weakened while another rose; technologies of production, credit, warfare and communication were changing the relations among distant societies. Religious institutions in Muslim lands would meet these transformations through reforms of government, learning and the organization of law.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Hodgson's 'Great Western Transmutation' describes changes in technology, communication, production and social organization that accompanied and exceeded Europe's overseas conquests. Atlantic credit, maritime trade and continuing Asian commercial networks developed together, challenging claims of a sudden and universal European monopoly. The familiar claim that Europeans simply monopolized the Indian Ocean from 1500 is false; studies of Gujarat and the Gulf document cosmopolitan communities and continuing Asian participation into the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Nor should a single Ottoman defeat be made the beginning of a uniform Muslim 'decline.'

Material change mattered to intellectual and religious life through identifiable intermediaries. A state unable to finance armies might reform taxes, borrow money or reorganize offices. A trading company that acquired territorial power could alter courts and property claims. Printed books and eventually telegraphy changed the speed and scale at which arguments moved. These were uneven transformations: an endowment-supported teacher in Cairo, a village jurist in India and a merchant family on the Swahili coast experienced them differently. Guénon's account of modernity as a change in the understanding of knowledge may be placed alongside this history as an intellectual and metaphysical critique, but it does not replace the evidence for economic and administrative changes.

Surat's maritime trade demonstrates why European arrival did not produce instant European control. Merchants of different religions and regions financed vessels, exchanged credit and relied on commercial partnerships; European companies entered these existing networks while later acquiring territorial power in different ports. Political control over a harbour, a monopoly over a commodity and control of an inland agrarian region are three different achievements. In Europe itself, changing relations among taxation, debt and military expenditure affected how states competed. Hodgson's 'transmutation' and traditionalist critiques of modern civilization address different questions: the former seeks historical mechanisms of change; the latter evaluates the intellectual and spiritual meaning of modern modes of life. Their conclusions can be compared only after the subject of each has been specified.

Colonial courts and the transformation of Islamic law

In parts of South Asia, the British East India Company acquired the power to collect revenue and administer justice in territories where Mughal legal and fiscal traditions had previously operated. Colonial officials consulted Muslim jurists and translated authoritative books, but gradually established courts that classified cases through new procedures and state-defined categories. The scope of Islamic law was reduced and reorganized, especially as penal and commercial law came under new codes and Muslim family matters were assigned to a distinct personal-law field. Elsewhere, French rule in Algeria and British occupation of Egypt rearranged property, education and judicial authority through different institutions. A local scholar could still teach jurisprudence, but a colonial court might decide what counted as applicable 'Muslim law' through manuals, precedents and officials trained in another legal system. The changes affected the social meaning of legal expertise as well as the content of particular rulings. They also encouraged efforts to preserve teaching networks and to reform curricula, since inherited ways of educating jurists no longer guaranteed influence over public institutions.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Colonial authorities reorganized courts and legal procedures differently in India, North Africa and other Muslim regions. British Indian 'Anglo-Muhammadan law' applied selected doctrines under colonial procedures and later concentrated much Muslim law in personal status; the University of Exeter's Privy Council Papers historical guide gives an accessible account of the changing scope. Wael Hallaq argues that colonial state structures transformed juristic authority; local court records and studies of codification document how these changes operated in particular jurisdictions. Sohaira Siddiqui examines changes in legal theory and authority under colonial rule alongside the translation and codification of particular legal manuals.

Colonial courts transferred parts of legal classification and procedure from older juristic networks to officials working within new state institutions. A juristic ruling had once emerged in a field populated by teachers, muftis, judges, litigants, rulers and local practice; colonial government could retain the vocabulary of Muslim law while relocating final authority over procedure and legal classification. That shift did not erase all juristic agency, and different courts followed different trajectories. It did, however, produce new educational problems. Madrasas might strengthen instruction in the inherited texts, while new colleges trained students for changed administrative professions. The divergence between moral formation, scholarly qualification and public jurisdiction became acute. Al-Attas later argued that education should order knowledge according to its proper purposes, while colonial officials and modern colleges changed the institutional settings in which law and learning acquired authority.

The Bengal judicial reforms associated with Warren Hastings in 1772 provide a dated institutional point of entry. Officials attempted to retain some Hindu and Muslim legal doctrines while placing their application inside a new hierarchy of Company courts. Over the nineteenth century, codes, appeals and professional practices altered the role of classical juristic reasoning in public judgments. The effect cannot be summarized as the abolition of the Sharīʿah on one day. In other places, such as Egypt and Malaya, colonial and local arrangements developed differently. The source method here is to choose one class of cases—for example inheritance—and compare its doctrinal treatment in a juristic text, the translated rules used by officials and a particular court judgment. Only then can one assess which parts of interpretation and jurisdiction changed.

Renewal through teaching, reform and print

Scholars and teachers responded to political and educational change by returning to inherited texts, developing new institutions and seeking wider audiences. The printing press allowed Qurʾānic commentaries, legal manuals, devotional books and polemics to reach readers who might never have entered a major teaching circle. In India, Deoband developed a madrasa founded in 1866 to preserve and renew learned disciplines under colonial conditions; Aligarh pursued a different educational path through English-language and modern instruction. In Cairo and other centres, reformers debated legal reasoning, religious schooling and the relationship between inherited disciplines and new sciences. Sufi communities, independent teachers and local scholars also continued older forms of transmission while adapting to railways, steamships and expanded pilgrimage. Such changes brought more readers into religious argument but sometimes weakened the authority of personal explanation: a printed excerpt could circulate without the teacher who had supplied its context. Schools and teachers now had to explain inherited doctrines to audiences formed by print, modern examinations and new divisions of professional and religious study.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Modern reform should not be reduced to a binary of 'traditional' and 'modern'. Deoband, Aligarh, reformist writers in Cairo, Ottoman educational institutions and West African learned networks pursued different objectives. Some expanded hadith study; some sought a reorganization of legal authority; some defended established Sufi instruction; some integrated administrative or scientific subjects. Printed editions also altered textual practice: a student's stable printed page differed from a manuscript exemplar annotated through personal reading, but neither medium guaranteed correct understanding.

Al-Attas connects education to adab and the ordering of knowledge; Guénon criticizes the modern priority given to quantity. Their arguments interpret modernity at a different level from the institutional histories of particular schools. Their theses must be named and examined; they cannot stand for the motives of every nineteenth-century Muslim teacher. The historical evidence involves school registers, syllabi, printed editions, biographies, correspondence and institutional records. The better question is which students each new arrangement served, what it enabled them to do, and what forms of knowledge became easier or harder to transmit. The answer will vary across classes, languages, genders and regions. Print, mass schooling and institutional examinations changed how teachers explained texts, corrected mistakes and assessed students' understanding.

Deoband and Aligarh represented distinct institutional responses among many forms of South Asian Muslim education. Deoband emerged through a specific scholarly and financial arrangement, while the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College developed its own residential and institutional aspirations. Women, merchant communities, village teachers, Sufi networks and other regional schools also shaped religious learning. Print made a legal opinion or devotional text more widely available, yet public circulation could intensify disputes over wording, authority and context. Examinations, lessons and student writings reveal differences between memorizing formulas, explaining a text and applying its reasoning to unfamiliar cases. This links the formation of malakāt to actual instructional practice without pretending that one curriculum was uniformly successful.

Nation-states, migration and the new scale of Muslim life

The twentieth century saw the Ottoman sultanate and caliphate abolished, colonial governments challenged, and many Muslim-majority states established or transformed through independence, partition and revolution. Borders divided communities that had long travelled through imperial, commercial and scholarly routes; migration then formed new congregations in Europe, the Americas and elsewhere. Mass schooling brought religious instruction to large populations while altering the places where qualified teachers were trained. Radio, television and digital media gave preachers and readers an audience far beyond their own towns. Pilgrimage by air connected Muslims who spoke different languages and lived under different legal systems, but shared recognisable rites and texts. Governments variously administered mosques, family law and universities, while private associations, scholars, families and spiritual lineages sustained additional kinds of authority. A person now might learn prayer from family, jurisprudence from a university course, religious guidance from an online lecture and spiritual discipline from a local teacher. The recurring question of the Sīrah—how knowledge becomes trustworthy action and enduring community—had acquired new conditions, not a single new answer.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Muslim-majority states, minority communities and religious institutions experienced distinct political and educational developments; no single pattern describes them all. Independence in India and Pakistan in 1947, Indonesian independence during 1945–49, the dissolution of Ottoman political institutions and distinct African decolonizations took place under different conditions. State ministries, traditional schools, Sufi networks, universities and migrant associations cannot be assigned one universal effect. The transformation of broadcast and digital communication also has contrary possibilities: access to large textual corpora expands, while the authority of a circulating quotation can become detached from the circumstances of its transmission.

In Madinah, believers developed arrangements for shared obligations and adjudication; later institutions addressed related problems through different legal and communicative structures. In seventh-century Madinah, a new community needed ways to place trust in reports, resolve disputes and act together; later hadith, legal and spiritual traditions elaborated forms of authority that could travel beyond their first setting. Today the material conditions of access and repetition have changed again. A transmitted text, its juristic interpretation, its practice in daily life and later spiritual explanations remain distinct even when one person engages all four. Digital circulation expands access to religious material without guaranteeing correct interpretation or lasting habits. It yields concrete research questions: what does a student know after repeated short videos, what can they independently verify, who corrects their misunderstanding, and how does learning change conduct over time? A student's ability to identify sources, explain their meaning and apply what they learn supplies concrete evidence of understanding.

Online archives lower the cost of finding religious texts, but understanding them still requires linguistic, historical and interpretive skills. A scanned manuscript, a search result from a hadith database, a short devotional clip and a university lecture each give different access to evidence and guidance. Digital texts and short lectures often circulate without their original context; checking the source, language and commentary remains necessary for accurate interpretation. Teachers still identify errors that access to a document alone cannot correct. Throughout the history, the ability to receive information has been easier to establish than the formation of a person able to understand and live by it. Digital archives broaden access to sources while placing greater demands on independent verification and sustained study.
