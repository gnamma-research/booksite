Book I — The world into which the mission came · 01 / 46

Prophetic beginnings: guidance addressed to human life

The Infinite excludes no possibility, and creation unfolds the possibilities of manifestation without adding to God's perfection. The cosmos discloses the divine Names through distinct beings and relations; the complete human gathers their meanings in knowledge and character. God forms and teaches Ādam for this comprehensive vocation. As His khalīfa, the human being recognizes creation's dependence upon its Source and enacts the rightful relations among its goods.

The Garden presents human life in its original harmony, with created things open to their higher meanings. The loss of that harmony begins when a sign no longer directs the soul beyond itself. Satan promises Ādam and his spouse immortality through the forbidden tree, attaching a longing for the Eternal to a finite means against God's command. They eat and acknowledge their wrong; God accepts Ādam's repentance and gives guidance. The aspiration for permanence receives its true direction through remembrance and obedience. The descent fulfils the earthly vocation God had announced before the lapse.

Iblīs makes his limited perception sovereign. He judges Ādam by clay, claims superiority through fire and persists against the command. His judgment encloses the human being within one outward feature and defends the image of a superior servant. Even devotion becomes a veil when the lover makes his chosen relation to the Beloved the condition of obedience. Divine wisdom encompasses this rebellion and brings opportunities for resistance, discernment and repentance through the trial. The tempter seeks another's ruin while the penitent receives the grace of returning to God.

Earthly life requires provision through successive causes, cooperation among persons and the formation of capacities through repeated action. Labour, craftsmanship and the diversity of human desires supply the means of responsible earthly life. Disorder arises when a particular good displaces its proper end: remembrance becomes unquestioning allegiance, provision becomes hoarding, authority becomes domination, and knowledge becomes self-exaltation. Revelation restores judgment; worship and virtue join understanding, desire and action. Return restores human integrity and opens beyond separate individuality toward God.

The prophets address these possibilities under different conditions. Nūḥ builds the Ark under revelation; at the flood he calls his son aboard and receives divine instruction concerning the promised household. Hūd challenges the pretence of permanence in monumental power, and Shuʿayb restores exchange to truthful measure. Ibrāhīm rejects the divinization of created forms and establishes a sanctuary where household, pilgrimage, security and livelihood serve worship. Yūsuf interprets concealed meanings, preserves fidelity through temptation and confinement, and turns foresight into provision across years of abundance and scarcity. Mūsā confronts oppressive sovereignty, leads Israel from bondage and forms outward freedom through covenant and law.

Dāwūd joins praise, craftsmanship and just judgment; Sulaymān exercises wide dominion as a received trust. Their authority orders earthly life through knowledge of its ultimate Source. ʿĪsā's extraordinary signs of life disclose divine agency through a created messenger and direct the witnesses toward their Lord. These missions reveal different dimensions of human perfection within the common summons to God.

Muḥammad ﷺ completes their succession through the final revelation, whose address reaches people and generations beyond its original recipients. The Qurʾān confirms and governs the reception of earlier guidance; the Prophet's life joins knowledge, worship, inward purification, household relationships and public responsibility. Ibrāhīm's House becomes the common orientation of believers across the earth. The final dispensation gathers the forms of good under a guidance enacted among different persons and circumstances. Its enduring principles form the capacities of the individual, order household and communal relationships, and orient public judgment. Fiqh gives determinate normative expression to aspects of these acts; ethical and spiritual formation cultivate the responsible agent; practical governance applies knowledge to changing forms of common life. Prophecy ends; humanity inherits the work of understanding, embodying and transmitting its guidance.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Divine plenitude and the human vocation

The Infinite excludes no possibility; manifestation unfolds the possibilities whose nature admits manifestation. It adds nothing to God’s knowledge or perfection. Creation is neither a second absolute beside God nor an illusion devoid of reality: its reality is received, and its dependence is complete. The divine Names express the relations through which one Source sustains diverse beings. Life, knowledge, power and beauty appear in creatures according to their capacities without dividing their Source into parts.1

The world distributes these possibilities; human nature gathers them. The body belongs to the material order, sensation encounters particular things, imagination presents forms beyond immediate perception, intellect apprehends meanings, and will directs action. The heart can recognize the divine significance of the whole. Teaching Ādam the names joins this receptive constitution to knowledge of what it can apprehend. Humanity does not merely stand among the things it names: it can understand their relations and its responsibilities toward them.2

Comprehensiveness orders knowledge, faculties and action toward their appropriate ends. Knowledge without humility can serve self-exaltation; mercy without judgment can protect injustice; judgment without mercy can become severity for its own sake. Perfection gives each capacity its proper scope. Its perfection preserves the rightful distinction and cooperation of the faculties.

Khilāfa turns this comprehensiveness into responsibility. A vicegerent receives the goods he must protect and the authority by which he acts. Provision, teaching, craftsmanship and judgment serve different needs within one vocation. Knowing their Source prevents delegated power from becoming a claim of independent sovereignty. Such knowledge does not replace technical competence: the entrusted servant must also learn what his particular responsibility requires.3

The Garden and the loss of inward vision

Paradisal integrity joins the enjoyment of creatures to recognition of their Source. The world nourishes the body while its appearances disclose spiritual meanings. Human perfection orders the faculties around spiritual knowledge; transcendent realization passes beyond confinement within separate individuality. These are distinct depths of attainment, not greater and lesser quantities of worldly information.4

Harmony can nevertheless persist as an inheritance after its inward principle is no longer directly realized. Conduct, custom and received forms preserve an order whose possessors do not fully understand its grounds. Their goodness remains real, but a plausible alternative can displace them because recognition no longer sustains conformity from within. The inward history of the Fall passes from direct vision through inherited harmony to disordered desire. The primordial human couple act and return together: their temptation addresses a shared longing, both acknowledge wrongdoing, and the promise of divine guidance opens their earthly vocation. It describes humanity’s spiritual conditions, not a demonstrated sequence in which the historical Ādam repeatedly lost completed realization.4

Satan addresses the longing for permanence, promising immortality through a forbidden tree. The aspiration exceeds ordinary appetite: the soul seeks a good that decay cannot remove. The deception substitutes possession of a created object for dependence upon the Eternal. A sign becomes a veil when its appearance absorbs the attention it should direct beyond itself. The supposed power of the tree belongs to the tempter's promise, which directs the longing for permanence toward created possession.

Ādam and his spouse already dwell together when they receive the prohibition. Both eat and acknowledge wrongdoing; God accepts Ādam’s repentance and grants guidance. The return preserves the distinction between a good aspiration and its wrongful pursuit. The earthly vocation precedes the lapse, while the descent changes the conditions under which humanity fulfils it. Repentance restores the relation to God without cancelling bodily dependence or the work that earthly life requires.5

The restricted vision of Iblīs arises when a faculty suited to perceiving particular forms claims authority over a comprehensive human reality. Estimation (wahm) grasps individual significances; intellect and heart can relate them to more universal meanings. The comparison of fire and clay operates within a limited field of perception. When that comparison governs obedience, the restriction becomes a moral act. The body presents Ādam in a perceptible form while the human spiritual constitution gathers faculties whose unified reality becomes intelligible through divine teaching. Even considerable intellectual ability can coexist with an incapacity to receive correction when the will defends its preferred image of the self.24

Iblīs: a partial relation made absolute

Finite faculties receive a particular goodness through the realities they are constituted to apprehend. A creature realizes a particular good; privation arises where a good proper to it is absent, and moral disorder arises when its powers oppose their rightful ends. Deception therefore borrows attraction from something good—life, knowledge, security, devotion—while concealing the conditions of its right pursuit. A truthful proposition or worthy action can mislead when severed from its occasion, obligations or purpose.6

Iblīs makes a partial apprehension authoritative. He compares fire with clay, then treats that comparison as sufficient to reject God’s command. His error lies in granting his limited perception authority over the divine command. Ādam acknowledges a fault against the command; Iblīs makes his own judgment the standard by which the command must justify itself.

Even devotion can assume this form. Love may relinquish reward yet retain the identity of the exceptional lover. Rejection then confirms that identity, while correction threatens it. Fidelity becomes obstinacy when the servant will accept the Beloved only through a relation of his own choosing. The corruption occurs when the lover requires a particular relation to the Beloved in order to preserve his own chosen spiritual identity. Repentance relinquishes both the fault and the self-interpretation that protects it.7

The inward fixation also discloses the breadth of providence. A suggestion intended to ruin someone may occasion resistance, humility or repentance. The tempter’s consuming intention can prevent him from grasping this wider result. Divine wisdom does not compete with his agency on the same level: it encompasses consequences that exceed his knowledge and purpose. His own intention remains directed toward temptation while divine wisdom governs consequences beyond his knowledge.8

Concealment participates in a manifested order as a dependent mode through which discernment and disclosure become apparent. Beauty can organize contrast; providence can overcome an adversarial intention. The moral character of the tempter's own intention remains determined by his rejection of the divine command. The perfected servant recognizes opposition’s place without making opposition his vocation. The whole includes what his conduct must reject.9

Labour, habit and return

Earthly life separates desire from fulfilment through distance, time and intermediate causes. Hunger does not produce food; an intention to judge fairly does not establish the facts. Cultivation requires seasons, tools and cooperation; judgment requires evidence, discernment and enforcement. Labour bridges a practical gap between a capacity and its achieved good. Its difficulty reflects dependence, not inherent moral corruption.10

Work also forms the worker. Repeated acts train perception, restrain impulse and stabilize skill. They can equally establish carelessness, domination or entitlement. A malaka is an acquired disposition, not necessarily a virtue. It becomes virtuous when it makes right action stable and responsive to circumstance. A rigid habit can preserve yesterday’s answer while losing the judgment that made it appropriate.

Desires conflict because finite persons pursue several goods through limited time and resources. Rest, nourishment, learning, companionship and service are not enemies by nature. Judgment orders their claims. Disorder begins when one demand acquires an unconditional priority: present appetite excludes future need, personal advantage excludes another’s right, or institutional survival excludes the purpose for which the institution exists.

Worship draws the faculties repeatedly toward their common end. Recitation addresses understanding; prayer joins attention to bodily action; fasting restrains appetite; giving subjects possession to responsibility. Their revealed authority and spiritual significance exceed their psychological effects, but those effects help explain how remembrance becomes a lived disposition. Repetition restores what distraction repeatedly disperses.11

Return therefore has two connected depths. Primordial integrity reunites knowledge, desire, will and action within the human state. Transcendent realization passes beyond attachment to the separate self. The first is not mere respectable behaviour; the second is not the ego’s acquisition of unlimited power. Neither abolishes the distinction between spiritual knowledge and particular expertise. A person can recognize his ultimate end without knowing every material means required by every occupation.43

Continuity and preservation: Nūḥ

A community inherits both goods and habits of interpreting them. Ancestry preserves language, affection and knowledge, but succession does not establish truth. When inherited practice becomes immune to judgment, the vehicle of transmission claims the authority of what it should transmit. Historical continuity then imitates eternity: what has lasted appears entitled to last, and belonging replaces recognition.

Nūḥ’s narrative distinguishes these forms of continuity. His preaching challenges inherited worship; the Ark preserves life through a judgment that dissolves the existing order. He builds under divine instruction. Revelation supplies the direction, craftsmanship supplies the vessel, and trust refuses to mistake the vessel for an independent source of safety.

His son relies on a mountain. Elevation ordinarily protects against water, but an ordinarily effective means cannot guarantee escape from divine judgment. Nor can biological descent from a prophet replace fidelity. The story separates natural support from ultimate refuge and kinship from the promise kinship cannot command.

The Ark also signifies an integration capable of surviving dissolution. Body and spirit, sensible form and meaning, revealed law and spiritual realization remain joined rather than abandoned as incompatible. Preservation carries a living order across a rupture; it does not require every feature of the former society to survive. The Flood joins judgment to renewed continuity without turning destruction itself into spiritual attainment.12

Covenant and the sacred centre: Ibrāhīm

Ibrāhīm distinguishes the Creator from the visible forms through which people seek ultimate power. Celestial brilliance does not confer divinity; inherited worship does not establish truth. His trials move recognition into action: allegiance must survive separation from customary protection and the surrender of what he loves.

The covenant gives this allegiance a durable communal form while withholding its authority from unjust descendants. The House then joins place, family, prayer, pilgrimage, security and provision. Its purpose precedes the prosperity it attracts: Ibrāhīm settles his household so that they may establish worship and asks for the livelihood that sustains it. Custodianship remains service to that end, not ownership of the holiness associated with the site.13

A sacred centre concentrates an orientation that must extend beyond the centre. Its fixed place gathers dispersed journeys; its recurring rites order time; its direction enters the worshipper’s posture. Divine appointment makes these acts religiously authoritative. Their embodied form lets an invisible end govern visible conduct. The stone does not contain God; worship through the appointed sign prevents either matter or private imagination from becoming the final authority.

The centre also creates obligations. Pilgrims need protection and provision; residents depend upon people who come from elsewhere; sacred privilege demands service. Worship and livelihood therefore become reciprocal without becoming identical. The later corruption of sanctuary privilege concerns this relationship: benefits that sustain worship can become ends defended against worship’s demands. The Muhammadan restoration returns the House to its purpose and extends its orientation beyond the lineage that guards it.14

Interpretation becomes provision: Yūsuf

Yūsuf’s story joins symbolic knowledge, moral fidelity and administration. The opening dream presents a destiny not yet visible in events. His brothers judge through rivalry; betrayal, enslavement and prison then conceal the larger course from ordinary expectation. The narrative’s end discloses connections that its participants could not perceive at the beginning.

Interpretation crosses from a presented form to its meaning without treating every appearance as self-explanatory. Ethical discernment makes the same crossing in action. Immediate pleasure may conceal injustice; apparent loss may preserve integrity. Yūsuf’s preference for prison over wrongdoing distinguishes the good an object promises from the consequences of obtaining it unlawfully.

In prison, interpretation accompanies the call from divided lordships to the one God. Before the king, it becomes foresight. Images of abundance and scarcity disclose successive conditions; Yūsuf converts that knowledge into cultivation, restrained consumption and storage. Present abundance is only part of the relevant whole: future hunger has a claim upon present action.

Knowledge must therefore govern means as well as identify ends. Grain must remain usable, labour must continue, and consumption must leave reserves. Trustworthiness protects the beneficiaries; competence makes protection effective. Either alone is insufficient. His eventual reconciliation with his brothers completes the same movement personally: power that could exact revenge instead repairs the household. The interpreter does not merely explain providence; he acts responsibly within it.15

Freedom, judgment and delegated power

Mūsā confronts a king who turns entrusted power into a claim of supremacy. Pharaoh invokes his care for the child while concealing the oppression of the people. A genuine benefit becomes a false justification when detached from the wider relations in which the benefactor acts. Personal favour cannot establish the justice of an entire rule.

Liberation removes an external master; it does not automatically free desire, judgment or collective life from disorder. Covenant and law direct the freedom won at the sea. A people can escape a tyrant yet remain vulnerable to fear, appetite and inherited habits of submission. Religious formation makes freedom answerable to a good beyond immediate preference.

The encounter with al-Khiḍr adds a distinction within knowledge. An event’s visible sequence may not disclose the circumstances that govern its meaning. Recognizing that limitation does not authorize ordinary claims of secret knowledge against public obligations. It distinguishes an extraordinary disclosure from the evidence on which others must act.16

Dāwūd joins worship, craft and adjudication. Praise acknowledges the Source of power; workmanship directs material capacities toward protection; judgment subjects conflict to truth rather than the ruler’s appetite. Sulaymān extends rule across a wider range of creatures while acknowledging its gift. Their kingdoms disclose the positive vocation of authority: coordinating differentiated powers toward goods no isolated agent could secure. Breadth of dominion does not remove mortality or confer knowledge of everything concealed.17

Their stories distinguish strength from sovereignty in the absolute sense. A ruler may command without creating the truth that legitimizes command. His authority remains accountable to the rights and ends it serves. Prophetic kingship integrates these relations in a person; subsequent government must preserve them without claiming the ruler possesses prophecy.

Life and its divine Source: ʿĪsā

ʿĪsā’s conception, speech and healing make the gift of life exceptionally conspicuous. His signs exceed ordinary human powers, yet the repeated qualification “by God’s permission” identifies their agency. The messenger’s greatness lies in the extraordinary disclosure through him, not in independence from the One who grants it.

The greater the sign, the greater the possibility of mistaking its received perfection for self-sufficiency. Diminishing the sign would also miss its meaning. Right recognition affirms both its excellence and its dependence. The same distinction governs spiritual authority more generally: a teacher’s effectiveness does not make him the source of the truth he communicates.

Confirmation of the Torah and the alteration of certain prohibitions join this disclosure to an actual religious community. Spiritual renewal neither abolishes inherited guidance simply because it is inherited nor preserves every existing restriction as ultimate. The life-giving sign and the correction of communal practice belong to one mission: restoring relations through which human beings receive and obey God.18

The Muhammadan gathering

The prophetic missions share one divine end but differ in audience, law, setting and characteristic disclosure. No prophet possesses only one virtue, and no community needs only one kind of correction. Their differences reveal distinct relations within human life: preservation through judgment, orientation through a sanctuary, interpretation through images, freedom through covenant, and authority through entrusted knowledge. Their unity lies in the right ordering of these relations toward God, not in identical historical arrangements.

Muhammadan comprehensiveness gathers these perfections under one revealed orientation. In its metaphysical sense, the comprehensive divine Name embraces the differentiated Names manifested through the prophets. The Muhammadan reality has principial priority; the historical Prophet completes the succession. This order of principle and manifestation is not an ordinary chronology placing his earthly life before Ādam.19

In the dispensation, comprehensiveness has definite consequences. Its address extends beyond the first people who receive it. Its finality excludes a later prophetic commission to supersede it. The Qurʾān confirms earlier revelation while governing its reception; it does not combine every earlier law indiscriminately. The Prophet’s example relates worship, purification, household obligation, livelihood and justice without reducing them to a single occupation.20

Universal guidance also requires accessible means. The earth provides places of prayer and means of purification; one orientation unites worshippers without requiring everyone to reside at the sanctuary. Stable obligations can operate across different environments. Suitability therefore includes mercy and practicability, not merely a greater number of regulations.20

The enduring recitation carries the sign beyond its first witnesses. Its meanings address new listeners, but access alone does not produce understanding or virtue. Teaching must make meaning intelligible; practice must make it operative; correction must prevent partial understanding from claiming the authority of the whole. Where direct spiritual vision is uncommon, preserved words, rites and disciplines provide means of recovery rather than substitutes for realization.21

Comprehensiveness is thus an ordered capacity to guide, not an inventory of every future event. It preserves the relationships through which new circumstances become answerable to enduring ends. Earlier missions fulfil their entrusted purposes; the final mission establishes a common criterion for the remaining history of prophetic inheritance.

The practical consequences of this metaphysical order belong to three interrelated scales. The formation of the individual (tahdhīb al-akhlāq, tadbīr al-nafs) concerns the governance of knowledge, desire, intention and will. Household governance (tadbīr al-manzil) concerns the ordered relationships of spouses, children, dependants, guests, possessions and livelihood. Political governance (tadbīr al-madīna, al-siyāsa al-madaniyya) concerns the coordination of rights, offices, common resources, protection and public judgment. These are simultaneous orders of practical intelligence exercised by complete human subjects.25

The Prophet's enacted practice gathers these orders. A prayer shortened for a crying child brings worship together with a mother's distress and the responsibility of a congregation's leader. A confession involving pregnancy and a dependent infant draws upon repentance, judicial evidence, public authority and the care owed by other people. Each encounter bears a determinate configuration of goods. Fiqh articulates the normative duties and limits governing these acts. The formation of character concerns the one who acts, the household communicates trust and obligations across generations, and political institutions sustain public responsibilities among strangers. Their appropriate integration is learned through rightful conduct, sound interpretation and the development of practical capacities.26

The final revelation supplies lasting ends, prescribed acts, binding rights and authoritative methods of judgment. The variable conditions of commerce, government, education and common life also require acquaintance with material circumstances, institutional means and the people affected. Sound practical discernment judges these particulars in light of enduring principles, using the legitimate interpretive methods appropriate to each field.27

From prophetic integration to historical inheritance

Finality changes the form of continuity. Prophets no longer succeed prophets, but teaching, judgment, public authority and spiritual formation must continue. These functions pass through different people, practices and institutions. No one office inherits the whole prophetic vocation.22

A text preserves wording; a teacher communicates understanding; repeated practice forms dispositions; a judge settles a claim; a household sustains mutual obligations; government coordinates and protects. Their contributions depend upon one another without becoming identical. Learning cannot replace justice, political power cannot confer spiritual realization, and personal sincerity cannot correct a false argument. Integration requires both cooperation and limits.

Institutions make commitments effective beyond a single moment. Stored grain gives future hunger a claim upon present abundance. A teaching tradition allows later learners to receive knowledge from people they never meet. An endowment can sustain a purpose beyond its founder’s life. Each arrangement preserves something that individual memory, lifespan or resources cannot secure alone. Each can also mistake its own survival, prestige or possessions for the good it exists to serve.

The relation between soul and society is reciprocal. Persons establish practices through judgment and desire; established practices then train the attention, habits and expectations of later persons. A private distortion can acquire public durability, while a well-ordered practice can support people who do not yet understand its full purpose. Neither structural reform nor inward counsel therefore suffices in every case. Renewal restores the relation between persons, practices and ends.

Ādam receives a comprehensive vocation; the prophets disclose its realization under differing conditions; Muḥammad ﷺ establishes its final revealed guidance; later communities must preserve that guidance through differentiated forms of life. Their responsibility is not to reproduce every former arrangement, but to keep means answerable to ends and inherited authority open to truth.23

Land, settlement and the political worlds of the Near East

Humanity's earthly vocation unfolds through dependence upon created means. Water, soil, animals, plants and the capacities of the human body make provision possible, but their benefits are obtained through knowledge, labour and cooperation. Cultivation requires attention to seasons; storage makes the harvest of one year available to another; craft transforms natural materials into instruments; and exchange carries goods beyond the places where they arise. These activities give practical expression to the Adamic capacity to understand the natures, properties and relations of things. They establish obligations because each person's livelihood depends upon the work, knowledge and restraint of others.

Along the Nile, the annual flood sustained cultivation whose rhythms joined households, villages and successive political authorities. In southern Mesopotamia, channels of the Tigris–Euphrates system supported fields and settlements requiring continuing maintenance and decisions about water. Each landscape supported several possible arrangements of cooperation, temple and palace administration, royal power and local independence. Local cooperation preceded and persisted alongside the enlargement of temples, palaces and royal administrations. The concentration of harvests supported artisans, soldiers, scribes, worship and public works; it also made possible heavier exactions, compulsory labour and the accumulation of power. The same administrative knowledge that secured a worker's ration could determine what was taken from the farmer.

Writing and accounting made obligations durable beyond personal recollection. Records distinguished quantities, recipients, stores and transfers; contracts and correspondence extended cooperation across distance and time. The truth of the written account and the justice of the obligation it records require distinct judgments. Kingship similarly brought powers of protection, adjudication and coordination that could be exercised for the common good or converted into domination. The growth of civilization therefore enlarged both the possibilities of human service and the consequences of moral disorder.

Other communities lived through mobility rather than continuous settlement. Herding joined access to pasture and water with seasonal movement, while merchants connected places whose inhabitants could not produce all that they required. Caravan journeys depended upon animals, guides, credit, hosts and agreements governing passage. Town and countryside, cultivator and pastoralist, merchant and producer participated through different forms of livelihood and in interdependent economic networks. Their relations could be cooperative, unequal or openly hostile according to circumstances and conduct.

Between Egypt, Mesopotamia and Anatolia, the Levant contained coastal ports, cultivated plains, upland settlements and routes into Arabia. Its rulers negotiated with stronger kingdoms while households continued the recurrent work of farming, herding and exchange. During the second millennium BCE, Egyptian authority extended through networks of dependent city rulers, soldiers and tribute. The disruption of several Late Bronze Age political and commercial systems around 1200 BCE did not destroy every settlement or practice. Older centres survived, others declined, and new political configurations arose, including the Phoenician coastal cities, Aramaean kingdoms and the emerging communities of Israel and Judah. Political forms changed unevenly while the obligations of provision, protection and judgment remained.

Arabia presented further variations. Oases sustained cultivation through the protection and management of scarce water, while movement across arid country required knowledge of routes and reliable arrangements with those controlling places of rest. Settled and mobile lives interacted without one being reducible to the other. The later sanctuary-centred order of Makkah would possess its own history and obligations; its significance cannot be deduced from the political institutions of Egypt or Mesopotamia.

The prophets addressed people who worked, cultivated, traded, formed households and exercised authority. Food, land, wealth, rank, kinship and political authority are genuine goods, but none determines its own proper limits. The obligation to worship God orders the use of property, the conduct of exchange, the treatment of dependants and the exercise of judgment. A community may possess powerful institutions without realizing their rightful ends, while a religious teaching may outlast the political arrangement that once supported it. In Israel and Judah, the loss of royal power and sanctuary would test this distinction: the covenant could not be identified simply with the survival of a king, a territory or a building.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Provision, embodied action and the purpose of earthly knowledge.

The human body lives through a world received from God and through relationships with other living beings. Rain precedes harvest, fertile soil precedes cultivation, animals and plants possess forms and capacities independent of human choice, and no one person's abilities suffice for every stage between seed and meal. The Qurʾān directs the human being to consider food and then names rain, the splitting of the earth, grain, fruit and pasture (80:24–32). Food becomes available through rain, fertile soil, cultivation, harvest, transport, preparation and the human capacity to employ these created means. Cultivation and cooking require work upon things whose existence and efficacy remain received. Al-Rāzī distinguishes the conditions through which food comes into being from the processes through which it nourishes the body: attention to provision must trace actual intermediate causes rather than halt at the consumed object.

The command to inhabit and cultivate the earth places human work within received responsibilities toward land, its inhabitants and its Lord. In the address of Ṣāliḥ, God has brought human beings forth from the earth and istaʿmarakum fīhā (11:61). Al-Ṭabarī explains the latter as causing people to dwell in or inhabit it. The verse joins the created setting of human life with worship and repentance, not with a particular constitution for the state or an unconditional mandate to appropriate whatever land can be seized. Human labour becomes an exercise of knowledge when it takes account of the properties of things, the claims of other people and the dependence of every result upon more than the agent's will.

Spiritual detachment can mature through the faithful performance of practical work. The farmer who understands how a seed germinates and the builder who knows the strength of a material exercise genuine faculties. Their acts become morally excellent only when the end, the means and the claims arising from their use are rightly related. Need does not make extraction innocent; physical success does not make a purpose legitimate. The Adamic capacity to know a creature's nature is distinct from the practical virtue of responding to that knowledge justly. A society can acquire increasingly sophisticated skills while failing to form the dispositions through which those skills are used well.

Interdependence is neither equality of functions nor a warrant for domination.

Households require many unlike activities: some cultivate, some preserve, carry, grind, cook, record, heal, construct or judge. Specialization can extend the effectiveness of each activity because it permits learning through repetition and cooperation across persons. It also creates asymmetries of information and power. The person who controls a store may determine access to food; the one who assesses a harvest may misrepresent its yield; the person whose labour is indispensable may nevertheless lack the power to negotiate its terms. Each advantage needs an ordering principle beyond the mere fact of being advantageous.

Qurʾān 43:32 speaks of differentiated livelihoods and some people making use of the services of others. The immediate argument rejects the attempt by powerful Meccans to nominate the kind of person upon whom revelation ought to descend. Al-Rāzī observes that the opponents mistook wealth and public eminence for the relevant measure of prophetic excellence. The verse recognizes dependence within worldly life while simultaneously denying that possession of worldly advantage authorizes a claim to divine election. Its description of economic disparity is not a moral endorsement of every hierarchy, contract or regime of labour that happens to exist.

An administration is therefore answerable to a dual test. It must know enough to preserve the material process for which it is responsible, and it must exercise its knowledge according to the obligations actually owed. A ruler unable to anticipate scarcity may fail through incompetence; a ruler who anticipates scarcity and withholds stores to coerce dependants may fail through injustice. Practical intelligence and right disposition belong together, but one does not substitute for the other. This conjunction will later appear in Yūsuf's claim to be both trustworthy and knowledgeable concerning the storehouses (12:55).

The Nile: the flood's rhythm and the limits of centralization.

Egyptian agriculture depended on the Nile's annual inundation and the recession that left fields ready for sowing. The level and timing of the flood varied; cultivators needed to adjust seed, labour and the protection of stores to the land actually available. Much ancient cultivation used basins that received floodwater and were then drained or allowed to recede. Coordination occurred at the scale of adjacent fields, communities and local districts. It did not require every farmer's irrigation decision to be issued from the royal court. Extensive perennial irrigation, in the later sense of year-round regulated water, must not be projected without qualification into the early pharaonic periods.

A successful village could maintain embankments and organize work without owning the entire river. Royal authority could nevertheless tax the yield, mobilize workers and command transport over distances exceeding the reach of a single village. These are different kinds of power. A central government might survey fields, supervise levies or maintain strategic works while local groups controlled the immediate distribution of labour and water. The possibility of taxation does not prove central origination of irrigation, and the existence of local cooperation does not eliminate royal coercion.

Storage separates the time of production from the time of consumption. A granary can sustain workers who are building, travelling or unable to cultivate; it can preserve grain against an uncertain harvest and support people whose crafts serve others. It also creates a point at which access becomes conditional upon an office, a seal, a command or an imposed debt. The farmer bears the first risk of a failed crop while an authority may possess the means to transfer the burden to another district. The actual outcome depends on assessment, collection, transport and distribution. A general statement that the state either 'provided prosperity' or 'extracted surplus' conceals the work by which either result occurs.

The Nile was thus neither an automatic engine of despotism nor a sufficient explanation of Egyptian kingship. Collective discipline, local expertise, courtly religion, military power and established social relations combined in historically changing ways. Since prosperity and extraction could employ the same channels and stores, judging an institution requires knowledge of the persons protected, the burdens imposed and the purpose for which resources were directed.

Mesopotamia: channels, city growth and the changing scale of obligation.

The lower Tigris–Euphrates plain presented another combination of possibilities and constraints. Rivers and distributaries shifted their courses; fields were supplied through natural levee openings, distributary systems and, increasingly, artificial channels. Early arrangements could be maintained by relatively small communities. More developed canal networks involved agreements about excavation, water timing, land access and maintenance. By the third millennium BCE, cities and royal or temple administrations could coordinate works extending beyond the immediate settlement, but the resulting power did not extinguish every smaller authority.

An upstream diversion illustrates the difference between a technical capacity and a legitimate claim. A channel may improve the crops of one district while reducing the share or raising the maintenance burden of another. Its proper construction requires knowledge of gradients, soils and water flow; its rightful administration requires determining whose fields are served, who performs the work, and who bears the cost. Technical improvement and justice cannot be read off the same fact. Neither can every dispute be attributed to a uniquely evil intention: scarcity can produce competing genuine needs that remain to be judged.

Urban life intensified the effects of decisions made at such junctions. By the late fourth millennium BCE, Uruk had become one of the principal large cities of southern Mesopotamia. Concentrated populations could support scribes, potters, metalworkers and administrators who did not directly cultivate the fields supplying them. The arrangement was not a single indivisible 'temple economy'. Temples and palaces possessed estates and administrative offices, while households, local authorities and private economic actors retained roles that differed by place and century. Surviving institutional archives are disproportionately rich evidence for official activity; they cannot by themselves establish that all production and exchange took place within institutions whose documents survived.

The city made recurrent obligations both more numerous and more durable. A worker could receive rations rather than harvest grain personally; an official could be accountable for stores he had never grown; a ruler could command resources drawn from people he might never meet. Such distance enlarges the demand for reliable measures and recognized duties. It also increases the opportunities for a powerful office to conceal the persons whose labour makes its decisions effective.

The account: what the earliest writing could—and could not—secure.

A clay tablet from southern Mesopotamia dating to approximately 3300–3100 BCE records the allocation of rations. A further Uruk-period tablet of the late fourth or early third millennium represents an administrative account of products such as barley and malt. These objects preserve signs for quantities, commodities and transfers from a time before fully developed cuneiform became suitable for extended literary composition. The first documents were not primarily abstract statements of law or theology. They grew amid procedures through which goods were received, allocated and remembered.

A memory held outside an individual gives other persons access to a decision after the decision-maker has departed. A ration list can show which worker is owed food, reveal a discrepancy between receipts and stores or preserve the terms of a transaction across an interval. It enables greater precision of coordination and supplies evidence with which to question an officer. Its use also presupposes persons able to make and interpret the marks. Those who lack such access may find their own entitlement represented by an authority who controls the document.

The distinction between a record and a just account is exact. A scribe may enter the amount ordered rather than the amount fairly due. The storage register may be accurate while the allocation it records is oppressive. Conversely, the ability to reconstruct a lost obligation can protect a weaker claimant against the forgetfulness or denial of a stronger one. Written evidence thus enlarges human power without specifying its moral direction. The record is an instrument of truth when answerable to the reality it represents, not merely because it is written.

Writing later served letters, contracts, school exercises, ritual, law and literary remembrance. Some systems of verbal transmission also preserved matters that an archive did not contain. No simple contrast between 'written administration' and 'oral religion' describes these societies. Priests and scribes belonged to religious institutions; families handed on obligations through practice and speech; and the same document could have legal, economic and sacred implications. Yet a fiscal register anchored to one estate and a teaching deliberately reproduced across households face different conditions of survival. Their distinction becomes especially consequential when a kingdom or temple ceases to function.

Pastoral mobility, households and trading corridors.

Seasonal mobility answers to the spatial distribution of pasture, water and risk. A flock cannot always graze the same ground without depletion; an animal may make use of land unsuitable for cultivation; a household may maintain relationships with settlements in which grain or tools can be obtained. Mobile groups therefore neither simply precede cities nor exist wholly outside their economies. Herding, farming and trade can be joined within a single family network, across successive seasons or through exchanges between different communities. Political authority may follow a group of people rather than apply uniformly to a bounded tract of land.

A journey introduces requirements that a local harvest cannot meet. The caravan needs animals, fodder, repair, secure halts, trustworthy information, guides and agreements with persons capable of obstructing passage. Credit permits goods to move before their final sale, but spreads vulnerability across partners who may be separated for months. In the Old Assyrian trading networks of the early second millennium BCE, merchants from Ashur carried tin and textiles to Anatolia and obtained metals in return. The Kültepe/Kanesh tablets record consignments, loans, household correspondence, disputes and transaction costs. They attest a commercial organization that depended on both settled scribal practice and repeated travel, and on households as well as itinerant merchants.

A route is therefore not a physical line alone. Its viability depends upon continuing assurances: the donkey and its load need food and water, the merchant needs safe passage and a forum to settle a debt, and the buyer needs confidence that goods and information are what they claim to be. Local hospitality and protection become economic and moral relations rather than optional sentiment. A powerful group can profit by guaranteeing passage; it can also turn control of a well or narrow crossing into extortion. In neither case does the desert determine the morality of the arrangement.

The contrast between a canal and a caravan is consequently relative, not absolute. Both involve material infrastructures, accumulated knowledge, specialized labour and enforceable understandings. A canal directs water along a constrained course; a caravan moves commodities between separated centres. They differ in the kind of territory and personnel through which decisions take effect. Rivers could carry boats, and desert towns could possess settled archives. No division between 'bureaucratic peoples' and 'tribal peoples' follows from the geographical distinction.

The Levant under competing powers.

The Levant linked northern Syrian cities and Anatolia with the Nile region, the Mediterranean and the approaches to Arabia. Coastal ports had access to shipping and imported materials; valleys and uplands supported different combinations of cultivation, herding and settlement. A kingdom controlling one prosperous city did not thereby administer the countryside continuously or command every route used by neighbouring communities. Its effective authority depended on surrounding villages, local chiefs, armed personnel and the willingness or inability of greater rulers to intervene.

The fourteenth-century BCE Amarna correspondence makes the structure unusually tangible. Vassal rulers in Canaan wrote to the Egyptian court about rival rulers, security, service, trade and tribute. Their professions of submission did not eliminate their local conflicts; subordination to Pharaoh could coexist with attempts to attract his support against a rival. A demand imposed through the imperial system became a burden carried by particular inhabitants, even when the written exchange records only the negotiations of their rulers. The letters are invaluable for political relations, but their elite perspective cannot be treated as a complete account of every village or household.

An imperial change altered the practical conditions of local life without always replacing its people. A new overlord might demand tribute, station soldiers, appoint or confirm a local ruler, interfere with disputes or direct goods along a different road. Traders might gain protected access to a market while cultivators faced increased assessment; a town might be spared destruction but lose the autonomy of its decisions. The effects of empire must be followed through these mechanisms rather than expressed solely as a succession of dynastic names.

Destruction and renewal at the end of the Bronze Age.

Around 1200 BCE several interconnected palace-centred regimes of the eastern Mediterranean were disrupted. Some sites experienced destruction or abandonment; the Hittite imperial system and Ugarit's palace order ceased in their earlier forms; trading patterns and political alliances were reorganized. Yet the processes differed sharply by region. Coastal communities, especially those later identified as Phoenician, preserved substantial continuities, and other settlements adapted or developed under changed conditions. There was no single uniform day of collapse and no secure explanation assigning every regional change to one climate event, invading people or political failure.

This unevenness matters because institutions survive in different pieces. A royal court may disappear while a harbour continues to operate; a long-range political alliance may end while village fields remain cultivated; a temple may lose an endowment while households continue older rites. Destruction can interrupt knowledge, but it may also redistribute the persons who carry it. Neither outcome is determined solely by the former power of the institution. The later Iron Age involved the formation of new regional configurations, including the Aramaean kingdoms and the small kingdoms of Israel and Judah, alongside enduring cities of the coast. These developments must be dated and reconstructed from their own sources rather than made consequences of one generalized civilizational catastrophe.

Political plurality also changed the settings in which allegiance could be expressed. A community dependent upon a patron king may face obligations to him and to an external empire; a local religious law may command conduct that the existing government does not reward. Such conditions do not establish that one religious community was uniformly more faithful than another. They give concrete form to the distinction between the validity of an obligation and the coercive capacity of the institution ordinarily associated with it.

Arabia's oases and the coexistence of different forms of life.

Arid lands concentrate settlement at places where groundwater, springs or episodic flows permit dependable cultivation. Protection of a well or cultivated enclosure can be as important to a household as possession of a field. Agreements over water affect planting, animal use, hospitality and the possibility of passage. Oases are not merely temporary stopping places for mobile groups: they can support durable households, specialized production, written communication and fortifications.

Archaeological work in northwestern Arabia identifies long-standing walled oasis settlements, including Taymāʾ and Qurayyah, with roots in the Bronze Age; the chronology and function of individual walls remain under research. Excavations at Dadan in the al-ʿUlā valley provide safely dated evidence of local date-palm cultivation in the twelfth–eleventh centuries BCE and greater oasis development in the first millennium BCE. These finds support distinct, long-lived forms of Arabian settlement rather than a picture of an undifferentiated desert inhabited only by nomads.

Oases did not make mobile exchange unnecessary. The cultivator needed tools or goods absent from the locality; moving groups needed water, provisions and sometimes markets; towns and travellers depended upon arrangements able to withstand disputes. The resulting interdependence could be stabilizing or exploitative. A fortified wall may protect crops and stores or mark the coercive reach of whoever controls them. The evidence does not permit an unbroken institutional genealogy from Bronze Age Taymāʾ or Dadan to the Quraysh of sixth-century Makkah. Makkah's specific sanctity, local power and seasonal trading practices require their own historically dated reconstruction.

Sacred places and political places: related but not identical.

Palace, sanctuary, market and household may share a locality while exercising different claims upon people. A temple may possess an estate or receive goods, but its rites do not reduce to the management of property. A king may protect a sanctuary and still be answerable to obligations that his protection does not create. A market can operate within political guarantees without receiving its standards of fairness from the mere will of the guarantor. A holy centre may draw people who owe political obedience elsewhere. Its religious role does not require it to function as the capital of the largest neighbouring state.

Each institution has both a proper service and a characteristic deformation. Storage becomes hoarding when goods needed for a legitimate purpose are withheld without right. Protection becomes monopoly when a guardian treats those protected as possessions. Sacred stewardship becomes appropriation when custodians present access to worship as an exclusive entitlement of their lineage or office. Kingship becomes tyranny when coordination and judgment are subordinated to the ruler's independent appetite for power. These are differences of action and relation, not conclusions drawn from the size or outward splendour of an institution.

A religious centre can unite people whose territories, languages and trades remain diverse. The Abrahamic House will give this possibility a particular revealed form. Its role cannot be deduced from the shapes of earlier Near Eastern temples, nor does its sacredness erase the material acts through which visitors are fed, defended and received. The contrast between service to a sanctuary and possession of its privileges later becomes acute in the Meccan Sīrah.

Prophetic judgment enters the actual arrangements of provision.

The prophetic stories name several misdirections of genuine capacities rather than condemning the capacities themselves. Hūd's people use construction and strength; the reproach falls upon imagined permanence and oppressive force (26:128–130). Ṣāliḥ's people cultivate, build and use a common water source; their test concerns the specified share owed to the divine sign (26:146–158). Shuʿayb confronts the use of commercial ingenuity to falsify measure and diminish the claims of others (11:84–88). Yūsuf's interpretation becomes a public discipline of cultivation, preservation and distribution (12:47–49). Mūsā confronts a ruler who has made the ordering of a population into a means of humiliation and violence (28:4). The Qurʾān's particular acts prevent 'civilization' from becoming an empty praise or an equally empty accusation.

The material conditions must be specified before the moral judgment can be understood. An accurate measure has a physical relation to the goods measured; withholding part of an agreed quantity injures the recipient rather than merely displaying an unattractive disposition. A water schedule is implemented at a place and time; destroying the protected claimant changes what others can actually receive. A grain reserve sustains life only when protected against deterioration and allocated when needed. A ruler's instruction becomes oppression through officials, categories, exactions and acts imposed upon bodies. Right worship reaches these transactions because they involve the rights of persons and creatures whose reality does not originate in the powerful agent's desire.

Providence does not absolve an unjust actor by bringing an unexpected good from his action. A great city can preserve art and knowledge while committing wrongs against those who build it. Nor does material success establish that a people have accepted guidance. The proper relation between inward recognition and outward discipline must be enacted through practices and institutions capable of responding to actual claims.

Knowledge and obligations beyond the loss of sovereignty.

Material records and religious transmissions have different vulnerabilities. A field survey may retain its precision only while the same boundaries and units remain in force; a temple archive may become obsolete when its estate is confiscated. An injunction, remembered account, prayer or legal teaching may be repeated in a different place, although language, instruction and stable communal practice are still necessary for its survival. It would be mistaken to oppose a territorially fixed state to a wholly immaterial religion. Teaching depends on teachers and households, texts on copying and preservation, rites on places and times, and justice on some means of applying judgments.

The distinction is between the obligation itself and the political apparatus that can protect or enforce it. A community may preserve an inherited command after losing its king; it may also preserve a formula while forgetting the disposition or understanding through which that formula is rightly lived. Conversely, the survival of a crown or building is not evidence that the duty it once served remains effective. The forms of continuity are therefore multiple: textual, pedagogical, familial, liturgical, judicial and political. Each can support the others without being identical to them.

The histories of Israel and Judah embody both the strength and the difficulty of this differentiation. Kings, territory and the Jerusalem sanctuary became powerful supports for communal life. Defeat and displacement transformed those supports without simply abolishing the possibility of teaching, worship or covenantal identity. The historical question is not whether a people can dispense with every institution, but which institutions can carry an obligation when its former political setting no longer exists and which practices prevent that obligation from becoming only a name.

Israel and Judah: kingship, destruction and life beyond a kingdom

In the Israelite remembrance, Dāwūd and Sulaymān ruled a people responsible for worship and justice; prophets later recalled both rulers and subjects to the covenant. In the Iron Age Levant, the kingdoms of Israel and Judah occupied lands contested by stronger neighbours. Assyria conquered Israel's capital, Samaria, in 722/721 BCE. A century later Babylon imposed its power on Judah, took Jerusalem in 597 and destroyed its temple in 587/586 BCE, deporting members of the population while others remained. People who had associated worship with land, king and sanctuary now had to maintain their identity in dispersed communities. Persian conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE enabled a restored temple at Jerusalem, but did not bring all Judeans home. Priestly service, teaching of the Torah, festivals and household observances sustained Jewish communities under rulers of different languages and religions. During subsequent centuries Jewish communities would live from Mesopotamia to the Mediterranean and eventually in several Arabian settlements. By the Prophet's lifetime, Jews in Yathrib participated in local alliances and cultivation as well as scriptural traditions. Their presence belonged to a long history of Jewish life outside independent Jewish kingship, though the origins of particular Arabian groups were diverse and are not securely reconstructed.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The transition from monarchy to dispersion changes the central historical problem. When the temple could no longer function and the king no longer secured the territory, continuity depended more heavily on scribal teaching, kin relations, calendar, prayer, law and ritual practice. The return under Persian supremacy restored a centre but not a self-sufficient sovereign state. This helps explain how identifiable Jewish communities could persist through Greek, Roman and Sasanian changes of rule. Jewish communities maintained religious institutions in Arabia, but the surviving evidence does not trace every family's ancestry to the Babylonian exile.

The changing medium of religious continuity is the decisive bridge. Royal patronage can maintain a central sanctuary; a dispersed community must teach children, preserve forms of worship, settle questions internally and recognize people of the same religious tradition elsewhere. Some of these activities developed over long periods, and their exact early chronology is contested. The conceptual connection to Islam's later history is nevertheless concrete: after the caliphate fragmented, Muslim communities too relied upon learning, law and travel beyond the reach of one political centre. Judean communities transmitted scripture through priests and scribes; Islamic madrasas formed much later under different political and educational conditions.

The Arabian connection must remain deliberately modest. Evidence that Jews were established in Yathrib in the Prophet's lifetime is not evidence identifying the specific route by which each family reached it. Jewish communities established roots in Arabia over different periods, but surviving sources do not establish one ancestry or migration date for all of them. The stronger line of explanation is how religious observance and distinctive communal law could exist under governments not controlled by those communities.

The Qurʾān recounts the missions of Mūsā, Dāwūd and Sulaymān (2:47–61; 21:78–82; 34:10–14); Kings, Chronicles and Ezra–Nehemiah preserve related but distinct histories, while Near Eastern evidence helps date the conquests of 722, 597 and 587/586 BCE. The Qurʾān and the biblical books recount some shared prophetic figures but differ in emphases, narrative structure and theological claims. Late-antique Jewish communities could be connected by learning and ritual despite local variations in language, social hierarchy and political status. This will later matter in Madinah, where the Prophet did not encounter an abstract “Judaism” but households and groups with land, alliances and particular obligations. Before that, the Persian and Greek worlds changed the languages and intellectual settings in which such communities lived.

Religious disciplines and the changing world of the first millennium BCE

After the destruction of Jerusalem, Jewish communities had to sustain worship and teaching without the independence of their former kingdom. Meanwhile the Persian kings linked the Levant, Mesopotamia and Iran through imperial government, and Greek cities developed their own modes of philosophical inquiry. Farther east, teachers in northern India debated ritual and liberation, while Chinese thinkers disputed the moral obligations of rulers and families. These developments unfolded across different centuries; they were not the work of a single generation or one demonstrable worldwide upheaval. In the western Near East, however, the lines of contact can be followed. Persian administration brought diverse communities under common rule; Alexander's conquests replaced it with Greek-speaking dynasties; and Greek became a language through which Jews, Egyptians, Syrians and others could explain their traditions to wider audiences. Jewish scripture was translated and interpreted in Greek, and schools of argument developed in cities where different religious communities met. The Roman world would inherit much of this linguistic and urban setting. It was within Jewish life under Roman authority—not in an undefined 'age of religions'—that the movement associated with ʿĪsā began. Elsewhere in Eurasia, religious and philosophical communities developed distinctive teachings, but the evidence does not establish direct transmission between all these movements.

Jesus and the communities that carried his message

In first-century Judaea, Roman governors, local rulers, priestly families and teachers of the Law operated within a population whose hopes and disagreements were shaped by worship at Jerusalem and memories of earlier prophets. Yaḥyā summoned people to repentance, and ʿĪsā ibn Maryam called Israel to sincere worship, mercy and righteousness. His disciples gathered around his teaching; after his earthly mission, communities devoted to him arose in Judaea and spread into Greek-speaking cities. Their members had to decide how Jews and non-Jews could share worship and discipline, what authority the apostles' teaching carried, and how the life of Jesus should be told. Letters, narrative accounts, readings and liturgies travelled with these communities. Jerusalem's destruction in 70 CE transformed Jewish religious life; Christian assemblies, meanwhile, multiplied around the Mediterranean and eastward. When imperial patronage followed Constantine in the fourth century, debates already underway concerning Christ's status acquired new councils, offices and political consequences. Greek, Syriac, Coptic and other churches did not interpret those debates identically. Monasteries became places of prayer, learning and hospitality, and missionaries and traders carried Christian life beyond the imperial frontier. By Muḥammad's lifetime the Christian people encountered by Arabs were neither a single doctrinal party nor a distant abstraction: they were neighbours, traders, rulers, monks and communities bound to different churches.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The Islamic account of ʿĪsā and Maryam belongs to revealed history (notably Qurʾān 3:33–55; 5:110–120; 19:16–36). The earliest recoverable Christian histories rest on a different kind of evidence: first-century letters, Gospel narratives, later patristic writings and documents of councils. Christian creeds developed through councils and theological disputes; their later formulations cannot automatically be attributed to the earliest disciples. The controversy surrounding Nicaea (325), Ephesus (431) and Chalcedon (451) needs to be understood as sustained argument among Christian communities, not as a single moment when Christianity became homogeneous.

Christianity also changed the geography of learning. In Greek-speaking eastern cities, philosophical and theological argument shared a vocabulary capable of crossing political boundaries. Syriac translators and teachers moved between theological, medical and philosophical work, sometimes under rulers of different religions. This made them potential mediators when Muslim patrons later sought Greek learning in Arabic. The intellectual bridge is therefore not a vague statement that Islam was 'influenced by Christianity'; it is the documented existence of bilingual scholars, texts and schools available within the regions that Muslim armies and merchants later encountered. In the Sīrah itself the immediate issues were worship, prophecy and protection, not the ninth-century translation of Aristotle.

A concrete mechanism in Christian expansion is linguistic and urban. Letters to congregations could circulate along roads and shipping routes connecting Greek-speaking cities; local assemblies could read them, dispute their meaning and transmit instruction even without a common local bishop. Later councils and imperial patronage made certain disputes politically consequential. At the Arabian frontier, however, Greek, Syriac, Coptic and Geʿez Christian lives did not form one uniform audience for Islam. Each source needs to identify the community it actually describes.

The causal thread is institutional as well as doctrinal. Greek and Syriac writing made teachings travel; churches offered regular worship and communal leadership; monasticism provided spaces for disciplined life, copying and teaching. Imperial patronage offered resources and created new occasions for controversy, since bishops and emperors could have different interests. Aksum's Christianity and Syriac-speaking communities in Persian territory meant that the Prophet's contemporaries encountered Christian practices beyond Constantinople's control. Muslims encountered Christian neighbours, envoys, protectors, clergy and monks as well as arguments about the person of Christ. Their routes of movement traversed empires and Arabian borderlands where religion, commerce and military protection intersected.

The sixth-century crossroads of Rome, Persia, Ethiopia and Arabia

The world bordering sixth-century Arabia was divided between powerful courts that also depended upon local allies. From Constantinople the Romans administered Syria and Egypt; Sasanian kings governed Iran and Iraq; Arab federates guarded approaches, mediated trade and sometimes fought one another along the imperial frontier. To the south, conflict between Ḥimyar, Aksum and Persian interests drew Yemen into Red Sea diplomacy. The persecution of Najrān's Christians in the 520s and Aksumite intervention showed how Arabian religious disputes could involve neighbouring kings. Yet the Ḥijāz lay outside direct Roman or Sasanian administration. Its towns depended more immediately on local sanctuaries, families, oases and alliances, while travellers carried news from the wider world. Jews and Christians lived in different Arabian regions; local shrines and tribal devotions continued alongside them. In 602 the Romans and Sasanians entered a devastating war. Syria and Egypt fell under Persian occupation before Roman recovery in the 620s. Around the beginning of that war Muḥammad was approaching maturity in Makkah; around 610 his public mission began to take form. Neither empire appointed the chiefs of Quraysh or the alliances of Yathrib, yet the conflict would soon alter the political setting into which the Muslim community expanded. The Prophet's earliest hearers lived among households governed by Quraysh's sanctuary customs and clan protections rather than direct imperial administration.

The sanctuary, the clans and the obligations of protection

Makkah was organized around a sanctuary that drew people whose clans might otherwise have been rivals. Quraysh families maintained the Kaʿbah and its services, arranged trade, and relied upon agreements that made travel and exchange possible across tribal boundaries. A person's safety ordinarily depended upon kinship or a protector willing to defend him; those with few allies or enslaved status had much less room to refuse a powerful household's demands. The sanctuary could restrain violence at particular places and times without abolishing wider inequalities or feuds. Among Quraysh, Banū Hāshim held an established place, and Abū Ṭālib would protect his nephew Muḥammad even when he did not join his religious summons. Beyond Makkah, Yathrib was a settlement of orchards and fortified neighbourhoods where the Aws, Khazraj and several Jewish groups were linked by cultivation, alliances and longstanding disputes. These two towns offered different possibilities. In Makkah the first call would be heard under the protection and pressures of established clans; in Yathrib rival groups would eventually seek a leader capable of mediating and joining them. The Hijrah thus became possible through relationships developing long before the emigrants departed.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Kinship and patronage determined who could challenge prevailing religious customs without immediately losing protection. A free man with an influential guardian, an enslaved person and a visitor without an alliance did not face the same risks when accepting a new religious allegiance. This social difference helps explain why early conversions followed ties of trust and household contact, and why some believers could resist pressure while others suffered violence. It would be anachronistic to describe Quraysh's sanctuary as a modern government. Tribal obligation, ritual privilege, negotiated protection and exchange could overlap without creating one centralized state. The Sīrah tradition's accounts of the Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl and the Prophet's protection by Abū Ṭālib are valuable evidence for how contemporaries remembered justice and patronage; the wording and sequence of those accounts need comparison across transmitters.

The protection system helps explain both the survival of the Prophet's mission and the injustice suffered by vulnerable converts. Abū Ṭālib's defence of his nephew was a recognizable kin obligation, even while religious disagreement persisted. A client or enslaved convert could lack a similarly effective protector. Accepting the new call was therefore not one uniform act of social defiance. The religion's claim of common accountability sat within highly unequal capacities to resist. Later migration did not remove the importance of kinship immediately; it placed inherited loyalties into new relations with people whose commitment rested upon worship rather than birth.

Mecca's exact commercial scale is disputed. Accounts of Quraysh's seasonal journeys record remembered patterns of commerce, whereas claims that particular Meccan families dominated long-distance luxury trade remain disputed. The protection system is more immediately relevant to the first believers: clan standing could shield a dissenting member while enslaved or client households faced coercion. That difference explains the uneven distribution of suffering without implying that the first converts belonged to one social class.

Quraysh organized commercial journeys, but historians disagree about the volume and geographical reach of Meccan trade; evidence does not establish that its clans controlled a vast trading empire. Qurʾān 106 emphasizes journeys, security and provision, while the institutional details come through later Sīrah and genealogy. A proclamation that challenged sacred objects and inherited status also threatened the loyalties through which clans maintained protection and authority. In Yathrib, meanwhile, the aftermath of conflicts such as Buʿāth provided a different political setting. The earliest instruction would begin in Muḥammad's household, where personal confidence and protection mattered before public authority existed.

Ḥirāʾ, Khadījah and the beginnings of a teaching community

Muḥammad grew up in the clan of Banū Hāshim, without his father, and after his mother's death under the care of his grandfather and then his uncle Abū Ṭālib. He married Khadījah, whose household joined affection, economic security and social standing. In middle life he withdrew at intervals to Ḥirāʾ, away from the crowded obligations of Makkah. There the first revelation confronted him with a command to recite and the knowledge of a Creator who teaches. He returned shaken to Khadījah, who listened, comforted him and sought counsel from Waraqah ibn Nawfal. Khadījah’s assurance drew on her long knowledge of his conduct and established the first intimate setting in which the experience was received and discussed. Revelation continued, and the new summons took shape through shared prayer, recitation, learning and the practical support of a small circle. ʿAlī, Zayd, Abū Bakr and others entered that circle through differing relationships to the household. In those gatherings believers learned to pray, give to others and govern their conduct in expectation of judgment. When the message moved into public hearing, the circle would be exposed to the protections and pressures of Quraysh's clans. Its beginnings in a household helped it survive those pressures.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

ʿĀʾishah's report in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (no. 3) recounts the first revelation, Khadījah's reassurance and Waraqah's recognition of the prophetic encounter. Khadījah's response is more than a gesture of reassurance: she names traits of conduct that can be tested in ordinary social relationships—kinship, hospitality, truthfulness and aid to the burdened. The report connects an extraordinary encounter to recognizable moral character without making a private experience into a publicly verifiable event of the same kind as a dated treaty. The initial call in Qurʾān 96:1–5 brings recitation, creation and knowledge together; passages such as 74:1–7 connect warning with purification and patience. Different reports arrange the earliest revelations and conversions differently. The surviving reports do not establish one unambiguous chronological order among the first converts.

There is an intellectual distinction between the event of revelation and the social process by which people learned to trust its messenger. The former, within Muslim belief, is God's address to the Prophet; the latter involves repeated teaching, memory, recognition of moral conduct and decisions taken by particular persons. Khadījah's role links those orders without merging them. Her reassurance addresses the Prophet personally, but the qualities she cites later become intelligible to others as grounds for trust. Similarly, an early follower's prayer is an act of worship and at the same time a repeated practice through which a shared form of life becomes recognizable.

Khadījah appealed to Muḥammad's established conduct, while Waraqah connected the encounter with earlier prophecy. In Bukhārī's opening revelation report, Khadījah's reassurance appeals to observable qualities of Muḥammad's conduct, while Waraqah places the encounter within remembered prophecy. That is not the same as independent archival corroboration of the private experience at Ḥirāʾ. In the transmitted account, Khadījah first reassured Muḥammad through her knowledge of his character, and Waraqah subsequently related his encounter to earlier prophecy. Khadījah, members of the household and other early believers heard the recitations, learned practices of worship and formed the first circle of instruction.

The transition from a revealed word to a growing community required human processes: someone had to hear it, learn its recitation, recognize the teacher's trustworthiness, practise prayer and make a judgment about allegiance. Khadījah's home, Abū Bakr's friendships and other networks of instruction each helped that process. Yet the new allegiance would eventually alter the meaning of those older ties. A believer might remain a son, client or merchant while refusing a form of worship expected by his household. That friction grew as teaching moved beyond familiar households into a public proclamation demanding changes in conduct.

The Meccan proclamation: worship, reckoning and new loyalties

As recitation reached people beyond the first household, it summoned them to worship one God and to answer for their lives after death. Wealth and ancestry could not secure a person's standing before God; powerful households now faced obligations toward slaves, orphans, debtors and hungry neighbours. Such teaching challenged ordinary habits of speech, possession, giving and judgment as well as established sanctuary rites. Quraysh's response was uneven. Some men and women of leading families accepted the call; others feared dishonouring ancestors, losing authority or unsettling inherited relationships. Those without a protective clan suffered most directly: Bilāl, the family of Yāsir and other vulnerable believers became known through reports of coercion. The new community learned endurance through circumstances that did not equally threaten all its members. Prayer, recitation, gifts and emancipation offered ways to practise new loyalties before the believers possessed collective political power. They could help and teach one another, but could not yet guarantee the security of everyone who joined them. When persecution made such vulnerability intolerable, some sought protection across the Red Sea. The movement's first institutional problem was therefore not how to govern an empire but how a community of conscience could preserve worship and people when its opponents controlled local protection.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Meccan revelation joins resurrection and judgment to demands for generosity, truthfulness and care for the vulnerable. Passages such as Qurʾān 81:1–14; 90:11–18; 92:5–21 and 107:1–7 join eschatological accountability to care for the vulnerable and critique ostentatious worship. The Meccan proclamation did not prohibit wealth or commerce as such; it demanded truthfulness, generosity and accountability before God in their pursuit and use. Recurring oaths, warnings, questions and scenes of the Last Day gave believers memorable language for examining daily conduct.

Giving, emancipation, prayer and restraint affected both relations among households and the intentions and habits of the believers who practised them. A person asked to free a slave must relinquish property; a person asked to give food must resist retaining it; a person commanded to endure mockery must discipline anger. The practices do not prove that every participant attained the virtue intended, but they identify occasions in which character was tested and cultivated. When later authors discuss malakah, intention or purification, they supply analytical language for such processes. Later writers developed theories of habit and virtue around practices that early believers already undertook: giving, prayer, restraint and endurance.

When the proclamation made generosity and accountability obligatory, its claim reached the actual power relationships of a city. The master who owned a slave, the guardian handling an orphan's property and the rich merchant dispensing or withholding support faced newly articulated duties. The proclamation challenged exploitation, but Meccan masters and patrons continued to wield coercive power. The historical effect in Makkah was first the formation of a community that judged such conduct by different standards, often without the power to enforce them.

Later ethical writers such as al-Ghazālī describe the formation of stable dispositions through repeated action, examination of intention and correction of appetite. This offers a useful account of how practices may form character, but it is a later analytical reconstruction. Al-Ghazālī developed his technical account of moral psychology centuries after the earliest converts practised prayer, giving and patient endurance. Historically, the relevant evidence for persecution comes largely from transmitted reports about Bilāl, the family of Yāsir and other early believers; severity, chronology and the details of speeches need individual examination. Their different social protections made a common religious commitment costly in unequal ways. As persecution intensified and clan protection failed some believers, migration across the Red Sea offered an alternative source of safety.

Aksum: faith before political safety

The believers responded to persecution according to the very different forms of protection available to them. Some were shielded by respected relatives; others were subjected to pressure, confiscation or violence. Groups therefore crossed the Red Sea to Aksum, where a Christian ruler was remembered as giving them refuge despite Meccan efforts to secure their return. The migrants left their homes and received security through a sovereign whose court belonged to the Christian kingdom of Aksum. They lived under the justice and authority of another ruler, preserving worship and fellowship while explaining their beliefs to people of a different religious tradition. In Makkah the Prophet continued to teach despite increasing pressure on Banū Hāshim and his supporters. Separated from the believers in Makkah, the emigrants maintained allegiance to the Prophet through trust and reports carried across the Red Sea. Aksum demonstrated that believers could survive beyond the reach of Meccan clan sanctions, but it did not solve the problem of how the Prophet's mission might become public and secure within Arabia. That problem would return with force when the Prophet lost two of his closest protectors.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The Aksum episode is usually narrated through Ibn Isḥāq as preserved by Ibn Hishām and other Sīrah collections. The religious setting is nonetheless plausible and important: Aksum was a Christian kingdom with Red Sea connections, and Arabia's western shore participated in a network wider than Quraysh.

The Negus's later standing within the Islamic tradition belongs to the fuller account. In al-Bukhārī 3877, the Prophet ﷺ announces Aṣḥamah's death, calls him a righteous brother and instructs the Companions to perform the funeral prayer. Aksum also clarifies a difference between spiritual steadfastness and political capability. Perseverance may enable an individual to withstand loss, but it does not create a lawful jurisdiction under which his children can live. The migrants needed a ruler willing to refuse extradition and a place where worship could continue; those conditions were supplied by a polity not organized around their own community. The emigrants remained loyal to the Prophet while living under the Negus's protection across the Red Sea. Yathrib later supplied a different combination: local believers not only offered protection but undertook responsibilities of a common public order. Confusing these two forms of security obscures the development of the Hijrah.

The emigrants in Aksum could worship under the protection of a Christian sovereign without possessing jurisdiction over their hosts. That institutional difference matters more than anachronistic claims about modern citizenship or interfaith policy. The transmitted exchange before the Negus places Jesus and Mary at the centre of the emigrants' explanation of their faith; later Sīrah accounts preserve differing details of the speeches. Yathrib's pledges would introduce a new kind of protection—shared responsibility for defence and settlement within Arabia.

The institution of asylum mattered because it shifted the conditions of survival. Makkah's protection was commonly personal and tribal; in Aksum, the migrants depended upon a foreign sovereign who was not part of their kinship system. They did not have to become citizens of a modern pluralist state for the episode to show a real form of interreligious coexistence. From loss of protection to an alliance at al-ʿAqabah

Khadījah's death deprived the Prophet of the companion whose home had sustained the first circle; Abū Ṭālib's death removed an influential protector within Quraysh. Without that protection, he sought a hearing beyond Makkah, but his visit to Ṭāʾif ended in rejection. The Qurʾān recounts the Prophet's Night Journey from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque. In the transmitted accounts of the Miʿrāj, he ascends through the heavens, encounters earlier prophets and returns with the obligation of the five daily prayers after the counsel of Mūsā. This extraordinary journey brings divine nearness into recurring bodily worship. Muslim spiritual commentators, especially Ibn ʿArabī and his interpreters, later explored the significance of the return from ascent to ordinary duties. The dating and sequence of individual reports remain subjects of investigation; the prophetic mission continued among households and pilgrims in Makkah as the people of Yathrib offered protection.

During pilgrimage seasons he continued speaking to visitors who came to the sanctuary. Among them were people from Yathrib, where rival groups had suffered years of conflict and where some were prepared to recognize a mediator and teacher from outside their local divisions. Several accepted his message and returned home; Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr then taught recitation and prayer among them. The first pledge at al-ʿAqabah concerned allegiance in worship and conduct; the second bound a larger group to protect the Prophet if he came to Yathrib. The difference was decisive. Believers had already found refuge in Aksum, but now a group within Arabia offered protection tied to a growing local community. Emigrants began departing in stages, leaving property and familiar alliances. The Prophet and Abū Bakr followed, travelling under concealment until they reached Yathrib. A religious movement formed under Meccan pressure was entering a town where it would have to organize public life.

Sīrah and maghāzī reports recount the rejection at Ṭāʾif and the subsequent pledges at al-ʿAqabah, although they vary in reported speeches and participants. The first pledge concerned belief and moral conduct; the second also committed Yathrib's supporters to protect the Prophet against hostile forces. The instruction by Muṣʿab was crucial between the two because the new allegiance required neighbours able to pray and learn together before their prospective leader arrived.

The Night Journey and Ascension provide the chapter's vertical depth. Qurʾān 17:1 states the journey of God's servant; accounts such as Muslim 162a relate the heavenly encounters, Mūsā's counsel and the five daily prayers. The Qurʾānic passages concerning vision (53:1–18) and the reports transmitted by ʿĀʾishah and Ibn ʿAbbās require differentiated interpretation. The primary accounts establish revealed worship and narrate the ascent; their literary variations call for comparison.

In Ibn ʿArabī's analysis of spiritual ascent, interpreted by James Morris through Futūḥāt chapter 367, the return to created life is a completion of realization. The prophetic servant resumes teaching, prayer and the right ordering of human relationships after an extraordinary experience of nearness. The five prayers bring each day under an orientation whose source exceeds the temporal order in which they are performed. Their bodily actions, congregation, repetition and reception by later generations give the spiritual event an enduring historical form.

Al-Qūnawī's analysis of prophetic correspondences and Farghānī's understanding of Muhammadan comprehensiveness offer further reflection on the many heavenly stations gathered in the Prophet's experience. A living person receives a distinctive divine commission and acts among particular people. The spiritual and historical orders meet in the Prophet's life without supplying one another's missing historical evidence.

The second pledge committed Yathrib's supporters to protect the Prophet, extending their obligations beyond personal acceptance of the message. An instructed circle in Yathrib could receive the Prophet as a teacher, but a binding commitment to defend him entailed risks for its people and their allies. The supporters of Yathrib accepted the risk of armed conflict with Quraysh when they promised to protect the Prophet. Muṣʿab's period of instruction connects private adherence to public capability: people who had prayed together and learned the recitation could recognize a common cause when asked to make a demanding pledge. The Hijrah then gave that promise a real object, placing vulnerable newcomers and the Prophet himself under the hosts' protection.

Yathrib did not invite an unknown stranger into an empty political space. The Aws, Khazraj and Jewish groups had their own property, alliances and memories of violence. The Prophet's standing as a religious teacher and an outside arbiter could appeal to people seeking a settlement, but the motives of each participant cannot be reduced to a single desire for peace. The Hijrah also required courage, secrecy and trust under risk, themes central to later spiritual reading. The immediate historical consequence was more demanding: once the newcomers arrived, housing, food, disputes and protection could no longer be managed as private matters among a few Meccan believers. Those problems became urgent as soon as the emigrants arrived.

The stages of al-ʿAqabah matter because instruction preceded armed protection. The migrants reached Yathrib with their religious fellowship intact but without much of the property, kin protection or livelihood that had supported them in Makkah. Worship takes shape in the routines of a town

In Madinah worship entered the shared life of households, neighbours and public authority. Congregational prayer gave the day a recurring orientation toward God. Families organised care, livelihood and obligations to dependants; disputes concerning property, promises and injury required judgments binding people beyond one household. The Prophet ﷺ addressed these relations through enacted guidance, instruction and decisions concerning particular persons.

During congregational prayer he heard a child crying and shortened the prayer because he knew the distress caused to its mother. One act joined worship to the recognition of a mother's circumstance and the responsibility of leading others. The child's need became relevant to the measure of a valid public rite. The same attentive conduct appeared in the Prophet's service to his household, his playfully affectionate speech to a young child, and his correction of deceit in the market.

Another situation concerned a woman from Ghāmid who repeatedly sought judgment concerning adultery. The Prophet directed her to seek forgiveness and repent. Her pregnancy then required consideration of the child she carried; after delivery, the child's need for nursing required a further arrangement of care. The reported process concluded with a severe legal punishment, while repentance and the treatment of the woman remained matters of public moral instruction. Judicial authority, maternal dependence, personal contrition and communal responsibility thus entered the same encounter.

These actions reveal three dimensions of practical guidance. The individual's inward formation orders desire, conscience and obedience to God. Household governance orders care, dependence, provision and close relationships. Public governance orders common worship, evidence, protection and binding judgments. Each situation brings different obligations into prominence, and the Prophet's comprehensive guidance answers them within their actual relations. The later sciences preserve distinguishable aspects of this practical unity. Jurisprudence determines legal duties and conditions; ethical and spiritual instruction cultivates the subject who performs them; education and public administration sustain the practices through which a community carries them into new circumstances.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The individual, the household and the city are three orders of practical reason, each with a distinct object. The formation of character (tahdhīb al-akhlāq) concerns how knowledge, appetite and will acquire a stable orientation toward the good. Household governance (tadbīr al-manzil) concerns persons bound by ongoing relations of dependence, care, cooperation and property. Public governance (al-siyāsa al-madaniyya) concerns justice and the coordinated obligations of a wider association. An individual participates in all three, often in a single action. These divisions help identify the formal structure of what the prophetic encounters make visible.1

The account of the crying child relates four determinations: the leader's intention to prolong prayer, the sound of a child, his awareness of the mother's distress and his decision to shorten the prayer. A rite directed toward God acquires an appropriate duration through attention to a person participating in it. The governing excellence is the capacity to know what is presently due: the end of worship remains fixed, and its permitted performance takes account of embodied dependence. Juristic analysis establishes the applicable ritual rules; practical and spiritual understanding considers the leader's perception, intention and act of attention.2

The case of the Ghāmidī woman requires more discriminating analysis. Her repeated request for judgment concerns the public treatment of a grave wrong. Her pregnancy and the subsequent need for nursing place an infant's welfare within the judge's responsibility. The narratives include the death penalty, the recognition of repentance and the correction of communal speech. Personal contrition, household responsibility toward a dependent child and public judicial authority have different formal objects. They meet in a decision involving the same persons and the same unfolding case. The jurist examines the requirements of proof and legal competence; the ethical inquiry considers what kind of person is formed by repentance and judgment; household discernment concerns the child and those capable of caring for the child; politics concerns the authority and procedure governing a public punishment. The differing reports preserve details that require individual comparison, especially nursing, timing and the care arrangement.3

The encompassing spiritual principle is the proper manifestation of guidance in the particular conditions of action. Comprehensiveness is realized through knowledge that discerns these conditions and gives each rightful claim its appropriate weight. In the Muhammadan Reality, the differentiated perfections belong to a higher formal unity; in the Prophet's historical life, guidance is enacted among concrete persons whose circumstances are known through attention, speech and action. The transmitted report makes the act available to later generations, while the completeness of living realization concerns the human subject from whom the act proceeds.4

A legal science must isolate the normative determination: the validity and duration of congregational worship, the admissibility of a confession, the conditions of punishment and the protection due to a dependent child. The normative determination is indispensable to the justice of public action. Ethical instruction addresses the capacities that make judgments perceptive and trustworthy; spiritual discipline forms the quality of the agent's relation to God; household practices sustain continuing relationships; political organization supplies courts, witnesses, resources and protection. These differentiated sciences and institutions allow a community to address analogous new situations under changing conditions, with binding norms and practical discernment operating together.5

Badr: conflict tests allegiance and public judgment

Leaving Makkah had not ended the confrontation with Quraysh. The emigrants had lost property and protection, Meccan trading parties still passed through routes near Madinah, and both sides saw the other's growing alliances as a threat. In the second year after the Hijrah, a confrontation connected with a Quraysh caravan led to battle near Badr. A smaller Muslim force defeated an army sent from Makkah, killing important Quraysh leaders and taking captives. Victory gave the new community standing it had not possessed before, but also brought decisions that a circle of persecuted believers had scarcely faced: how to deal with prisoners, what to do with goods taken in war, and how to distribute the benefits and burdens of fighting. The victory strengthened Madinah's position while intensifying Quraysh's resolve to retaliate. Participation in common defence required believers to accept command and share risks with people outside their immediate kin. Those demands could not be settled by enthusiasm alone; they called for rules, judgment and forms of trust strong enough to survive the reversals that followed.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The Qurʾān discusses Badr in passages including 3:123–127 and Sūrat al-Anfāl, which addresses fear, victory, captives, spoils and cohesion. Detailed troop movements, numbers and dialogues belong mainly to the later maghāzī tradition; they require comparison of versions, especially Ibn Isḥāq's material and relevant hadith. A battle's broad occurrence does not license treating each remembered speech as independently verified. Badr's importance as an institution-forming event is clearer when we ask who now bore responsibilities that had previously been limited to kin groups: commanders coordinated armed men from several backgrounds; the community faced captives and property disputes; alliances elsewhere in Arabia were affected by the perceived result.

Captivity offers a sharp case of institutional change. Before a public community could hold prisoners, conflicts of capture or ransom might be managed primarily through family or tribal retaliation. After Badr, the Prophet and the believers confronted prisoners from powerful Meccan households, whose treatment affected diplomacy and future exchange. Spoils raised related disputes among fighters who had taken different risks. These were not abstract problems added by later jurists: the community was learning to assign claims when common military action produced consequences beyond any one family's control. Later law systematized matters already encountered here, while the Qurʾānic address contested pride and entitlement arising from victory.

In Qurʾān 8 the aftermath of Badr is not celebrated merely as successful fighting. Disputes over spoils and the treatment of captives bring human desires into the immediate interpretation of events. That permits one to connect personal formation to public judgment without claiming that believers were uniformly free of rivalry. The Qurʾānic passages address fear, victory, captives and spoils; later maghāzī reports supply fuller speeches and troop counts whose transmission differs.

Victory confronted the fighters with decisions about spoils and captives, testing their restraint, loyalties and judgment. The participants faced fear, conflicting expectations and the temptation to read success as personal entitlement. Qurʾān 8 addresses disputes over spoils and requires obedience and collective responsibility after victory. Quraysh returned the next year with an army seeking revenge. Near Uḥud the Muslims initially held their ground, but the departure of some archers from an assigned position exposed them to attack; the Prophet was wounded and Ḥamzah was killed. The loss forced the community to confront how desire for gain and uncertainty about command could undo its common effort. A larger coalition later threatened Madinah. Defenders excavated a trench to deny the attackers an easy approach and endured a siege in which supplies, labour and the reliability of allies mattered as much as courage. The war also sharpened tensions among groups bound by different agreements in and around the town. Banū Qaynuqāʿ and Banū al-Naḍīr had been expelled in earlier conflicts. After the Trench, Saʿd ibn Muʿādh pronounced judgment concerning Banū Qurayẓah, with executions and captivity described in the transmitted accounts. By the end of these campaigns, Madinah had learned to defend itself through planning, common labour and coordinated action. It had also become clear that agreements, religious allegiance and ordinary kinship could pull individuals and groups in different directions under danger.

Fighting between the two centres ceased for a time, movement and contact became less dangerous, and neighbouring tribes reassessed their alliances. The treaty did not remove every grievance. Khaybar, the deferred pilgrimage and changing relations beyond Madinah

The truce with Quraysh opened space for action elsewhere. A campaign against Khaybar ended with Muslim control over its fortified settlements. Its Jewish cultivators continued working the land under an arrangement giving them half the produce, enabling agricultural life to continue after the change of authority. The settlement required decisions about land, labour and income that were more complicated than distributing movable spoils after battle. The following year the Muslims performed the pilgrimage visit previously denied to them, entering Makkah for a limited period under the negotiated agreement. The journey reunited the emigrants with the sanctuary and demonstrated that the treaty's terms could be observed. Other envoys and delegations entered the Prophet's growing circle of relations, and people outside Madinah had to decide whether to form alliances, resist, or acknowledge his authority while maintaining aspects of local life. The community was no longer concerned only with sheltering its own emigrants. It increasingly needed to govern relationships with towns and agricultural communities whose wealth, commitments and religious identities differed from its own. Those arrangements would become more extensive once Quraysh's treaty network broke down.

The return to Makkah and the incorporation of Arabia

When a conflict between allied tribes broke the arrangements of al-Ḥudaybiyyah, the Prophet marched toward Makkah in 630 with a force large enough to limit organized resistance. He entered the city, the idols in the Kaʿbah were removed, and the sanctuary's worship was redirected to the one God. Meccan families who had opposed the mission now faced a ruler who was also their kinsman; accounts of the entry emphasize grants of security and acts of reconciliation, though particular exceptions and incidents require careful reading. The new position did not make every Arabian allegiance secure. A coalition including Hawāzin confronted the Muslims at Ḥunayn, and campaigns, settlements and distribution of property followed. Delegations came to Madinah from different regions, while teachers, collectors and appointed officials carried instructions beyond the Prophet's immediate presence. The challenge changed with the scale of allegiance: people needed to know how to pray, contribute, settle claims and maintain obligations after an envoy departed. The mission that had begun in a single household now involved a network of communities and local leaders, whose integration depended upon teaching and administration as well as military strength.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The conquest is discussed in Qurʾān 110 and in hadith and Sīrah accounts; the Ḥunayn reversals in 9:25–27. The exact numbers, speeches and scope of amnesty are not all equally secure. The principal Muslim accounts describe Muḥammad's entry into Makkah and removal of idols; reports about individual exceptions differ and require separate evaluation. The Meccan sanctuary was not discarded: it was redefined within the worship taught by the Prophet. That mattered because pilgrimage, kin memory and the practical gathering of tribes were already rooted in it.

Integration also depended on the limits of central presence. A new Muslim household in a distant region could not consult the Prophet about every prayer, marriage or commercial dispute. Envoys and local teachers therefore had to communicate a core of practice while learning what questions the community actually faced. Tax collection brought another kind of encounter: people had to know the amount, purpose and authority of payment, and collectors had to be trusted not to abuse their power. Religious identity could expand rapidly after military victory, but the competence to live that identity required sustained instruction. After the Farewell Pilgrimage, the Companions carried the Prophet's instructions into communities that had not witnessed his conduct directly.

Incorporation is not an event completed by the removal of idols. After entry into Makkah, teachers, delegates and collectors had to make ritual competence and recurrent payments possible in regions distant from the Prophet. An envoy could convey belief and prayer, but regular contributions and disputes called for local collectors, teachers and judgments. The Prophet's dealings with delegations thus show the beginnings of a transmission problem: guidance could not depend indefinitely upon every person travelling to Madinah. Religious language, social custom and economic arrangements varied across Arabia. The Farewell Pilgrimage and the responsibilities that survived the Prophet

At the final pilgrimage the Prophet returned publicly to the rites of the sanctuary that had once been closed to his followers. Believers from different districts saw him perform the pilgrimage and heard instructions that joined worship to obligations toward other people. The rites ordered movement between Makkah, ʿArafāt, Muzdalifah and Minā; the address emphasized the inviolability of life and property, duties within families and the ending of inherited exploitative practices. Delegates and teachers carried these teachings to places whose inhabitants could not all attend a gathering in Madinah. The Prophet's household transmitted much that public assemblies could not preserve, including private worship, speech, conduct with children and the treatment of dependants. In his final illness, prayer and concern for the community continued while companions faced the prospect of his absence. When he died in 632, the revelation would not be renewed through another prophet. Worship, property obligations, local judgments and the communication of his teaching nevertheless continued. The community had to distinguish tasks previously united in one life: governing, preserving recitation and reports, issuing judgments and learning how to act. Different arrangements for those tasks became major features of subsequent Islamic civilization.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The pilgrimage's actions are preserved through differing Companions, notably the extended report of Jābir in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Book of Pilgrimage, no. 1218 in common numbering. Qurʾān 5:3 is linked in the received tradition to the completion of religion, while legal details arise through multiple reports and juristic comparisons. Versions of the Farewell Sermon differ in wording, while repeatedly addressing the sanctity of life and property, inherited abuses and obligations between members of the community. The historical significance of embodied instruction is strong: when an act is publicly demonstrated before witnesses, its later transmission can draw upon what people saw as well as what they heard. But witnesses differ in attention and memory, explaining why later scholars examined reports and reconciled apparent discrepancies.

After the Prophet's death, legal adjudication, report transmission and spiritual instruction increasingly developed through different scholars and institutions. What did the Prophet teach or do? Who witnessed it? Which of those actions binds others, and under what conditions? Who has authority to judge a dispute now? What dispositions make the instruction inwardly fruitful? Hadith criticism addresses the reliability of transmission; jurisprudence its normative application; political theory the government of the community; spiritual instruction its realization in character. In the Prophet's lifetime these functions met in a single authoritative person, though many companions already served as teachers or agents. His death therefore did not create knowledge out of nothing, but changed how different kinds of authority were accessed and coordinated.

The Prophet's death made an analytical distinction unavoidable. His authority included receiving revelation, teaching its meaning, judging disputes, leading worship and directing communal affairs. The end of prophecy did not end any of the community's practical needs, yet the functions could not all be inherited in the same manner. Sunni accounts emphasize the formation of the caliphate and the wider transmission of knowledge by the Companions; Imāmī accounts place particular interpretive authority in the divinely designated Imams. Sunni and Shiʿi accounts of the Prophet's final instructions later informed distinct understandings of political leadership and religious authority. Al-Ghazālī, Shādhilī authors and Akbarian thinkers later explored prophetic inheritance as the formation of character or realization of spiritual wisdom. These are serious retrospective interpretations, not evidence that the first assembly at Saqīfah already used their technical distinctions. The demands of leadership and expansion made these distinctions urgent long before jurists and spiritual authors gave them their later technical forms.

The Farewell Pilgrimage provides a public model of worship, but a great deal of the Sunnah reached later generations through domestic life and instruction outside large gatherings. Reports through ʿĀʾishah, Umm Salamah and others matter particularly for private conduct and interpretation of what the Prophet intended. Later scholars' distinctions between common practice, judicial rulings and prophetic particularities are analytical responses to the differing settings of those reports; they are not a ready-made classification uttered at the final pilgrimage.

The later question of succession arises because different functions performed by the Prophet could be transmitted in different ways without implying that revelation continued. A transmitted report could preserve his practice; a judge could apply it; a teacher could cultivate virtues exemplified in it; political leadership required a decision about public allegiance and coercive power. Sunni and Shiʿi traditions developed different doctrines of legitimate succession, while later spiritual authors elaborated additional accounts of prophetic inheritance. Ibn ʿArabī, Qūnawī and Farghānī developed distinct technical accounts of walāyah through their own arguments and writings (Dossier 7).

Abū Bakr insisted that the public obligations established during the Prophet's life were not dissolved by his death. The campaigns of the Ridda, including the fighting against Musaylimah at al-Yamāmah, restored control over much of Arabia at considerable cost. The same period stimulated concern for preserving the recited text and for maintaining officials and commanders across distant communities. Abū Bakr's caliphate lasted only two years, but it settled an immediate question of government in practice: a common religious life would require leadership capable of acting after the Messenger's death. Conquest and the problems inherited from older empires

Abū Bakr had struggled to maintain the obligations of communities already associated with Madinah. Under ʿUmar, armies moving beyond Arabia confronted the established powers of Syria, Iraq and Egypt. The battles at al-Yarmūk and al-Qādisiyyah brought military victory, but governing the conquered lands demanded skills unlike those needed to win them. Towns still required grain; fields still needed irrigation; churches, monasteries and village communities had their own leadership; tax officials understood accounts in Greek, Coptic or Persian. Soldiers expected payment and the people who remained on the land expected to cultivate it. The new rulers could not make all these arrangements anew. They negotiated submissions, retained many local officials and gathered revenue through modified versions of older systems. Garrison settlements, especially al-Baṣrah, al-Kūfah and al-Fusṭāṭ, kept substantial bodies of troops together without dispersing them among every village. Victory thus brought the Muslims into daily responsibility for populations whose religious affiliations, languages and economic practices had not changed with their governors. These responsibilities created the setting in which later debates about land, taxation, public justice and the rights of non-Muslim communities became unavoidable.

A landholder and a soldier encountered power differently: the soldier needed regular provisions; the cultivator needed a predictable demand and protection from arbitrary seizure. The garrison, the treasury and the provincial community

In the new garrison towns, soldiers lived with their families, received stipends and gradually made places for merchants, freed persons, jurists and teachers. Their quarters often followed tribal affiliations; common prayer and military command therefore existed beside familiar bonds of kin and patronage. ʿUmar's registers of payment recognized differences of service and standing, and the distribution of revenue required information about households, campaigns and rights of recipients. In Iraq, keeping extensive cultivated land under continuing taxation, rather than dividing it all permanently among victorious soldiers, sustained payments beyond a single generation. Arab tribal groups had become provincial communities, but the money that sustained them came increasingly from settled agricultural populations. At the same time, new Muslims entered these communities through conversion, marriage, alliance and clientage. Their claims to social equality and fiscal treatment did not always fit the privileges of the earliest military settlers. The government's efforts to maintain cohesion created problems that accompanied the Umayyad period: who counted as a member of the ruling community, how service was rewarded, and whether the treasury belonged to an army, a dynasty or the wider body of believers.

Civil war and the limits of loyalty

Expansion increased the powers of provincial commanders and the resources at stake in their appointments. Complaints against ʿUthmān's government sharpened until rebels killed him in Madinah in 656. ʿAlī received allegiance while others demanded that the murderers first be punished; in the disorder no agreement existed about who could enforce that punishment or command the armies needed to do so. Fighting followed near al-Baṣrah and later at Ṣiffīn against Muʿāwiyah's Syrian forces. Arbitration, proposed to halt the conflict, divided ʿAlī's supporters and led to further violence with the Khārijites. ʿAlī was killed in 661. Al-Ḥasan then made a settlement with Muʿāwiyah, bringing an end to that phase of warfare. Supporters divided over allegiance to a caliph, responsibility for ʿUthmān's killing, obedience to commanders and the Prophet's family's claims to leadership. Those disputes survived the immediate fighting and later shaped accounts of legitimate authority, grave sin and rebellion.

Sunni and Shiʿi arguments about legitimate authority developed amid disputes over retaliation, military allegiance and the duties of government.

The civil war also had geographical and material mechanisms. The garrison army of Iraq and the Syrian command were organized forces with their own leadership, pay and attachments. Muʿāwiyah could rely upon a developed provincial military apparatus; ʿAlī had to govern amid divided coalitions and disputes about the killing of ʿUthmān. Damascus, Karbalāʾ and the making of an imperial administration

Muʿāwiyah ruled from Damascus with Syrian military support and an administration able to draw on older provincial practices. His nomination of Yazīd placed hereditary succession at the centre of a dispute that the settlement with al-Ḥasan had not finally resolved. The event gave the claims of the Prophet's household an enduring place in Muslim religious memory. Civil war continued until ʿAbd al-Malik consolidated authority. Under him and his successors, the state issued new coinage, enlarged the role of Arabic in administration and invested in prominent buildings, including the Dome of the Rock. Governors and armies extended authority toward the Iberian Peninsula, Central Asia and Sind. The resulting empire was increasingly visible as a single political order, but its provinces differed in language, law and social composition. The government could regulate revenue and command troops more readily than it could determine what every believer should accept as authoritative religious teaching.

The Abbasid revolution and a change of ruling centre

By the middle of the eighth century, the Umayyad empire possessed formidable fiscal and military resources but faced opposition among groups dissatisfied with provincial rule, status and the distribution of authority. In Khurāsān, networks of Arab settlers and others were mobilized in a movement promising rule associated with the Prophet's family. The revolution did not redistribute authority equally among its supporters: the new rulers built a dynasty with its own claims, commanders and administrators, while ʿAlid expectations persisted. Al-Manṣūr chose a new site on the Tigris for Baghdad, bringing the court closer to Iraqi agricultural revenues, eastern communication routes and the skills of Persianate secretarial households. What had begun as a contest over legitimate rule became the foundation of a metropolis where political power, commerce and scholarship would meet. The earlier garrison cities remained significant, but the new capital drew specialists from several regions and languages into a shared urban economy.

Baghdad and the work of a learned city

Baghdad drew court officials, traders, physicians, craftsmen and students to a place where goods and ideas arrived by river, road and caravan. The treasury paid armies and officials, but private households and patrons also supported books, teachers and specialized work. Paper and book markets made it easier to reproduce, annotate and circulate substantial texts. Yet the city's different enquiries did not form one institution: some occurred in courts, others in mosques, private houses, bookshops or medical circles. The caliphs could sponsor scholars and also coerce them. As the city expanded, its intellectual authority depended increasingly on the quality of transmission and argument within communities of learning as well as the favour of those who ruled it.

Legal schools emerge from the need to decide recurrent cases

Merchants still sold on credit, spouses disputed entitlements, heirs divided estates and judges faced disagreements between neighbours. New places brought new customs and documentary practices, while transmitted guidance had to be applied to circumstances not described in identical terms by earlier reports. Teachers in Iraq, the Ḥijāz, Syria and Egypt developed methods for reaching and defending judgments. Abū Ḥanīfah's students preserved and extended Iraqi legal discussions; Mālik gathered reports and the practice of Madinan scholars; al-Shāfiʿī examined the authority of different kinds of proof; Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal became a prominent model of reliance on transmitted evidence. Over generations, their successors compared opinions, taught precedents and produced manuals. The resulting schools preserved characteristic procedures without freezing every judgment at the founder's death. They allowed communities far from the first centres to ask similar questions in a shared vocabulary, even while arriving at different rulings. Lawyers and judges thereby acquired influence independent of any single dynasty, although appointments, patronage and local social hierarchies continued to shape their work.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Disputes over sales, agency and liability required jurists to apply transmitted rulings and broader principles to new transactions. Suppose a merchant gives goods to an agent who sells to a third party, then disputes the price or liability for loss. A jurist needs to know the contractual terms, local usage, rules of agency and the relation of evidence to general principle. The legal concepts are formed precisely to make difficult cases teachable and repeatable. Al-Shāfiʿī's al-Risālah, Mālik's Muwaṭṭaʾ, works transmitted from Muḥammad al-Shaybānī and early Ḥanbalī collections arose in different settings; later school manuals reorganized their arguments and rulings.

His response to the woman of Ghāmid in Muslim 1695 includes a legal decision and also an encounter shaped by confession, pregnancy, care for a child and repentance. A jurist must identify the kind of evidence that can establish an offense, the qualifications governing a sanction and the authority that may apply it. The discipline is a genuine preservation of the revealed and transmitted normative order.

The same original can be read through ethical and spiritual questions concerning repentance and the disposition of those who witness another person's wrongdoing. Endowments, colleges and the social life of scholarship

A teacher could attract students through learning and reputation, but lessons required rooms, books, food and sometimes long years free from earning a living. Rulers, merchants and wealthy families established endowed mosques, hostels and colleges to support teachers, students, Qurʾān readers, jurists and visitors. Endowments could secure a scholar's work beyond a patron's lifetime, yet appointments to teaching chairs also became prizes in contests among households and officials. Learning thus acquired durable places while remaining dependent on personal instruction and recognition. A student still needed a teacher who could explain difficult passages and authorize their transmission; a building did not automatically certify understanding. The college, household library, mosque and Sufi lodge therefore became connected, sometimes competing settings in which law, grammar, recitation, theology and spiritual conduct were taught.

Translation, theological controversy and the uses of reason

Muslims confronted disagreements not only over actions but over what could be known about God, the world and human responsibility. Earlier debates about free choice, divine justice, attributes and the status of the Qurʾān developed among theologians who used argument to defend positions drawn from revelation and inherited instruction. Greek works on logic, natural philosophy and metaphysics became available in Arabic through translators working with Syriac and Greek learning. Thinkers such as al-Kindī and al-Fārābī fashioned new accounts of knowledge and its demonstration; physicians and mathematicians pursued different kinds of inquiry under the same broad literary language. Theologians and philosophers met on questions of causation, the soul and the beginning of the world, but did not necessarily mean the same thing by knowledge or proof. Al-Ashʿarī and al-Māturīdī emerged from different disputational settings and gave enduring Sunni theological vocabularies to these controversies. The encounter made the disciplines more technically precise while leaving unsettled which claims demonstration could establish and where revelation decisively guided judgment.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Philosophers and theologians disputed whether created causes act with inviolable necessity and how such causation relates to miracles and divine agency. Different answers presuppose different accounts of nature, regularity and possibility. It would be a mistake to summarize all Ashʿarīs as holding a single identical philosophy of causation or to equate the whole of falsafah with denial of miracles. The problem is debated in al-Ghazālī's seventeenth discussion of the Tahāfut, but it had a longer prehistory in kalām and philosophical accounts of nature. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's 'Occasionalism' entry gives a useful map of disagreement even about how to read al-Ghazālī's ultimate position.

Arguments about God's attributes, moral obligation and human action also arise from different sources. Early Muʿtazilī positions are not interchangeable, and Sunni Ashʿarī and Māturīdī schools later developed their own internal debates. Ibn Sīnā's distinction between essence and existence and his proof of the necessary existent entered many later theological treatises, despite strong criticism of other Avicennan doctrines. The crucial historical change is not that 'Greek reason defeated' or 'Islam rejected reason.' Methods of inquiry moved across disciplines, were adapted to particular problems, and became objects of criticism by new authors. Primary controls include al-Ashʿarī's Maqālāt, al-Māturīdī's Kitāb al-Tawḥīd, al-Fārābī's works on the sciences, Ibn Sīnā's Shifāʾ and al-Ghazālī's Tahāfut. Differences in authorship, date and formulation can change the meaning of a philosophical proposition and the objections later commentators raise against it.

The miḥnah offers an early case of theological language becoming an instrument of public authority. During the miḥnah, caliphal officials demanded declarations about the Qurʾān's createdness, making conformity to a theological position a condition of public service. Muʿtazilī theologians developed arguments about divine justice and unity beyond the court's coercive policies; later Sunni theologians also employed rigorous dialectical argument. Later Sunni theologians used intricate logical methods while rejecting the imposition of a doctrine by a caliph. The controversy involved distinct claims about divine speech, legitimate proof and the ruler's authority to enforce a doctrinal declaration. These are distinct but interacting processes.

Al-Ghazālī, al-Rāzī and the remaking of philosophical theology

As knowledge became more specialized, the scholar who mastered one discipline could no longer assume that its methods answered every question. Al-Ghazālī studied legal reasoning, theological controversy and philosophy, then examined both the power and the limits of their claims. His later teaching joined the discipline of outward action to the examination of intention and character. Ibn Rushd, writing in al-Andalus, defended particular philosophical demonstrations against objections raised in al-Ghazālī's critique. In the eastern lands, Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī interrogated Ibn Sīnā's arguments about existence, definition, knowledge and the soul; Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī later answered and reorganized parts of that critical legacy. Their works entered new teaching chains through commentary and gloss, often in the same institutions that taught grammar, law and theology. A student could therefore meet rival accounts of the human intellect and causation within a shared curriculum of religious learning. Commentators preserved rival arguments, formulated objections and replies, and taught students to test the force of a demonstration rather than assume agreement.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

These are two interacting but distinct intellectual histories. In Tahāfut al-falāsifah, al-Ghazālī objects to identifiable claims associated with the philosophers, including an argument about causation and miracle; Ibn Rushd's Tahāfut al-tahāfut responds to the critique. The disagreement cannot be reconstructed adequately by the bare sentence 'reason and revelation were reconciled' or by assigning one universal causal theory to al-Ghazālī. His Iḥyāʾ is another undertaking, concerned with the ordering of worship, everyday action, destructive motives and saving virtues. Al-Ghazālī's critique of philosophical theses and his treatment of worship and character pursued related concerns within different genres and arguments.

A second history develops through the criticism of Ibn Sīnā's al-Ishārāt. Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī uses detailed commentary to expose problems in essence, existence, definition and demonstration; al-Ṭūsī's replies help establish a continuing debate. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on al-Rāzī documents particular positions and the care needed in inferring his considered conclusions from dialectical writings. Their commentaries preserved technical disputes about essence, existence and demonstration through successive generations of study. Commentaries became intellectual instruments: by setting out a lemma, objection, response and further objection, they required students to inhabit the logic of a question before accepting a resolution. That is relevant to the larger history of formation because the discipline of judgment can be cultivated through argument as well as through repeated moral actions. The achievements and failures of those pedagogic practices must still be judged from actual texts and institutions, not an idealization of scholasticism.

The dispute over essence and existence turned on what definitions establish about things outside the mind. Ibn Sīnā argues that a thing's essence can be understood without thereby affirming its existence; al-Rāzī questions what such a distinction establishes about real beings and whether a definition yields knowledge of an external essence. The reply cannot be assessed by saying merely that the later thinker 'synthesized' theology and philosophy. Al-Rāzī's dialectical writings sometimes test a thesis without endorsing it, so particular lemmas and objections determine which position he actually adopts. The later classroom practice of commenting line by line preserved these difficulties for students. It also introduced a risk of treating subtle distinctions as verbal contests detached from demonstration; whether that happened is a question about particular curricula and commentaries, not a verdict on late scholasticism as a whole.

The work of the heart becomes a teachable discipline

A believer could fulfil an outward obligation and still struggle with vanity, anger, envy or attachment to reputation. The first generations had learned to examine intention through prayer, almsgiving, fasting, companionship and restraint. As Muslim communities grew, teachers gathered such questions into more systematic instruction. Al-Muḥāsibī asked how a person could recognize motives that concealed themselves from self-examination; al-Junayd was remembered for disciplined sobriety in spiritual life; al-Qushayrī preserved teachings, terms and biographical examples for later students. Al-Ghazālī arranged the ordinary life of worship, eating, work, marriage and fellowship alongside destructive and saving dispositions. Prayer, trade, speech, anger and hope all engaged the same person's intentions and character. Thus the concern for the inward life did not arise as a substitute for law or communal obligation. Teachers developed particular methods for addressing what correct outward action might leave unformed. Their techniques varied, and the later organized paths were still taking shape; what endured was the question of how knowledge and repeated action become a stable character.

The practice of self-examination takes place under conditions that can foster or frustrate it. Al-Ghazālī's discussion of blameworthy traits becomes relevant to such people precisely because their ordinary roles carry moral risks. Al-Ghazālī treated prayer, trade, social duties and concealed motives as connected aspects of moral formation. His interpreters, including al-Qūnawī and al-Farghānī, elaborated technical accounts of disclosure and Muhammadan perfection. These traditions overlapped in persons and settings without collapsing into one doctrine, and their spread depended upon teachers able to transmit both practices and ways of understanding them.

Cairo, Damascus and Jerusalem: government, learning and the Crusades

While scholars and spiritual teachers increasingly travelled across regions, political authority was divided among rulers whose territories and religious allegiances differed. The Fāṭimids founded Cairo in 969 and established Ismāʿīlī institutions of rule and learning beside the older centres of Iraq and Syria. Seljuk military rulers later entered Baghdad and governed extensive territories while acknowledging Abbasid caliphal prestige. Rival courts recruited soldiers, built hospitals and colleges, endowed mosques and sought scholarly support. In 1099 a crusading army captured Jerusalem, establishing Latin Christian principalities among Muslim and Eastern Christian populations. Recovery demanded more than an isolated battlefield victory. Nūr al-Dīn worked to consolidate Syria, while Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn took power in Egypt, ended the Fāṭimid caliphate there, and assembled forces able to recover Jerusalem in 1187. Trade, truces, pilgrimage and argument continued across the frontiers throughout the wars. The cities of Cairo and Damascus became important homes for jurists, scholars, craftsmen and Sufis, whose teaching relationships were not exhausted by the campaigns. The long conflict joined military resources, religious legitimacy and urban institutions without making the history of all those institutions reducible to crusading warfare.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Muslim and Christian rulers fought, negotiated truces, exchanged envoys and traded with one another; neither population acted as one undifferentiated political bloc. The Latin principalities interacted with diverse eastern Christian communities and with Muslim dynasties that themselves fought rivals. Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn's military success depended on prior Syrian consolidation, control of Egypt's resources and treaties with local powers. Ibn al-Athīr, Ibn Shaddād and Latin chroniclers preserve accounts of the campaigns from differing political and religious positions. Persianate culture did not replace Arabic learning; the two frequently occupied different but related roles in the education, administration and devotion of the same persons.

Mongol conquest, conversion and the survival of scholarly worlds

In the thirteenth century Mongol armies destroyed cities, displaced populations and dismantled established regimes across Central and Western Asia. Baghdad was taken in 1258, ending the ruling Abbasid court there. Yet other Muslim centres survived, and new rulers needed scribes, merchants, physicians and administrators to govern the territories they had conquered. Mamluk armies stopped a Mongol force at ʿAyn Jālūt in 1260, while Cairo and Damascus continued to support judges, schools and the movement of books. In Iran, Mongol rulers first patronized several religious communities; Ghāzān's conversion to Islam in 1295 marked a major development in Ilkhanid political life. Rebuilding, new endowments and courtly histories followed among continuing wars and fiscal pressures. Older scholarly practices could thus be adopted by conquerors who had initially stood outside them. Teachers and merchants also moved along Mongol-controlled routes, even as warfare made many local livelihoods precarious. The loss of a capital did not extinguish Islamic learning, but the recovery of learning depended on surviving people, books, towns and resources, rather than on an abstract continuity immune to violence.

The difference between a dynasty's conversion and a population's religious transformation is essential. Aḥmad Tegüder and Muslim Mongol soldiers preceded Ghāzān's accession and therefore demonstrate earlier Islamic affiliations within Ilkhanid society. Later Mongol rulers' patronage of scholars, foundations and monuments also served political purposes, but religious conviction cannot be disproved merely by identifying those interests. The contrasting Mamluk and Ilkhanid reports should therefore be compared as partisan accounts of a contested transformation. Courts, colleges, manuscripts and commercial routes carried learned and devotional practices through changing dynastic conditions. Mongol campaigns devastated cities and scholarly households, while surviving people and institutions later rebuilt teaching and textual circulation under new rulers.

The Middle Niger: markets, kings and independent scholars

South of the Sahara, Muslim merchants had long crossed deserts to exchange salt, metals, textiles and other goods. Their routes linked North African towns with settlements and courts along the Niger. Rulers sometimes welcomed Muslim traders and employed literate specialists before most of their subjects adopted Islamic practices. Mali became a powerful kingdom, and Mansa Mūsā's pilgrimage in 1324 brought its wealth and connections to wider notice. Under later Songhay rulers, towns such as Timbuktu and Jenne supported jurists, merchants, teachers and book copying. Scholars acquired standing through knowledge, family networks and their ability to mediate disputes; some sought autonomy from rulers while relying on the same commercial communities for resources. Rural devotional life, local languages and courts did not always develop at the same pace. West African Muslim scholarship therefore emerged through local institutions and debates, not merely through the arrival of books from the north. Its authors participated in wider Mālikī and Sufi conversations while writing about the needs of their own societies.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The Tārīkh al-Sūdān, learned biographies, manuscripts, archaeological evidence and reports by travellers such as Ibn Baṭṭūṭah preserve different aspects of the Middle Niger's history. Zachary Wright traces West African scholarly authority through teaching communities and inherited learned relationships, which did not depend wholly on appointment by royal courts. Northwestern University's West African Arabic manuscript collections and catalogues also make local textual production accessible.

Timbuktu supported learned families, libraries and teaching circles whose organization differed from modern universities; surviving manuscript inventories vary greatly in age and provenance. The age, provenance and content of particular libraries need separate examination. A study of 31 private libraries found variation in the age and provenance of collections, limiting claims of uninterrupted scholarly transmission across every household. The surviving libraries preserve evidence of local scholarship but differ in the periods and networks they document. Trade supported towns and literacy; rulers patronized institutions; scholars organized teaching and defended authority within those changing conditions. West African scholars used Arabic and Mālikī learning within local commercial, political and educational arrangements, adapting inherited disciplines to their own institutions.

Aḥmad Bābā (d. 1627) wrote on Mālikī learning and scholarly biographies, and his career was disrupted by Moroccan intervention in Timbuktu. His works on Mālikī scholarship and learned biographies belong to a world that maintained ties with North Africa; his career was also affected by Moroccan intervention in Songhay and the removal of scholars from Timbuktu. Aḥmad Bābā's scholarly career and forced removal after Moroccan intervention connected the independence of learned households with struggles over political power. The surviving works document learned circles and libraries in particular towns; they do not establish Cairo-style colleges everywhere or a uniform date for the manuscript collections. Their ownership and contents vary. Across the monsoon seas: ports, households and the Malay world

Muslim merchants travelled the Indian Ocean with sailors and traders of many religions, following seasonal winds between the Red Sea, Persian Gulf, East Africa, western India and Southeast Asia. A merchant entering a port needed lodging, partners, trusted weights and a means of settling disputes; resident communities built mosques, married locally and taught their children alongside commercial work. In the fifteenth century Melaka became a major harbour where rulers protected visiting merchants, organized customs and governed a population whose residents changed with the monsoon. The ruling court's Islamic identity connected it to other Muslim sovereigns, but the port's prosperity also depended on Chinese, South Asian and island Southeast Asian exchanges. Malay-language instruction and texts in Arabic-derived scripts developed within these changing communities. Islam moved through merchants, families, teachers and courts, with different sequences in different places. The spread of shared religious practice therefore depended on practical relations that made sustained teaching and settlement possible, while local languages and institutions gave it forms unfamiliar to early Arabia.

Melaka's syahbandar officers, customs regulations and arrangements for visiting merchants make an excellent causal case. They helped reduce uncertainty about lodging, weighing, protection and market transactions for temporary residents. Some of these residents formed more durable households and communities in which religious learning could continue between trading seasons. Harbour privileges supported trade and settlement, but dated inscriptions, legal texts and local chronicles give differing evidence for the conversion of particular rulers and communities. Peacock's Arabic and Persian material locates Melaka within broader Muslim geographical knowledge, while Malay and Portuguese accounts supply different perspectives. Holding these kinds of evidence apart prevents commerce from being treated either as proof of spiritual transmission or as an explanation that makes spiritual teaching unnecessary.

Bengal: fields, settlements and changing religious communities

In eastern Bengal the expansion of Muslim communities did not follow the map of early military conquest. Much of the lower delta was wooded or marshy, its waterways shifting and its agricultural settlements growing at different times. As political authorities claimed territory, cultivators cleared land and organized villages; religious teachers and patrons sometimes participated in these efforts by establishing shrines, places of prayer and local charitable ties. A family newly settled in a district might become attached to a saintly household through landholding, work and inherited practice rather than through a single dramatic conversion. Muslim courts and Persianate administration operated beside Bengali language and local custom. Over generations, devotional practices, agricultural communities and political boundaries changed at different rates. These intertwined processes help explain why large Muslim populations came to inhabit eastern Bengal, including districts far from the first centres of Turkish military power. The religion acquired local continuities through settled households and remembered teachers while remaining connected to wider Muslim texts, pilgrimage and law.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Richard Eaton's The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier, 1204–1760 is the key test case. He uses inscriptions, monuments, administrative texts and local literary records to relate political, agrarian and religious frontiers without collapsing one into the others. A later essay clarifies four overlapping processes, adding the eastward movement of Sanskritic institutions to political, agrarian and religious change. These distinctions are historical hypotheses supported in particular places and periods, not a universal theory of conversion.

A rigorous microhistory would trace one named village or shrine, the associated land grant if preserved, the date and location of an inscription and subsequent changes in surrounding settlement. It would ask whether a saint's remembered career is independently dated, whether followers occupied adjacent fields, and whether a later text projects a more coherent religious community onto an earlier landscape. The spiritual question is inseparable from this context but not reducible to it: why did people regard particular teachers and places as bearers of blessing and trustworthy guidance? The archive may establish their social position without proving or disproving their inner spiritual realization. In parts of Bengal, agriculture, land grants, shrines and religious instruction developed through overlapping local settlements rather than one uniform sequence.

A land grant associated with a religious figure in Bengal can carry several meanings at once: authorization to cultivate, a claim to revenue, support for a place of worship and later memory of a revered founder. Eaton's historical method asks which meanings can actually be dated from the document or its material setting. If cultivation moves into a forest frontier, the establishment of a village may alter the resources and social ties through which a religious community reproduces itself. Yet neither the clearing of land nor the grant proves a person's inward conversion. Land grants and mosque foundations document settlement and patronage but seldom disclose the private convictions of every household involved. A genuine integrated history resists the equal and opposite temptations of reducing Islam to economics or treating agricultural conditions as irrelevant to its local life.

The Ottoman settlement: courts, colleges and a growing imperial household

Ottoman rulers grew from an Anatolian principality into an empire whose capital, after the conquest of Constantinople in 1453, linked the Balkans, Anatolia and the eastern Mediterranean. Military forces, taxation and palace households sustained rule over Muslim, Christian and Jewish populations. Mosques, courts, colleges and charitable foundations gave towns recognizable Islamic institutions, while local communities retained different forms of worship and internal authority. Judges applied learned Ḥanafī legal traditions alongside sultanic regulations addressing revenue, order and administration. Scholars moved through educational and judicial appointments, acquiring prospects for service that tied some of them closely to the state. The conquest of Arab provinces in the early sixteenth century added older centres of Muslim scholarship and the great pilgrimage routes to Ottoman rule. Yet the empire could not teach religious life by decree: provincial mosques, households, Sufi teachers and book circles sustained practices that sometimes supported and sometimes criticized official policy. Religious institutions both helped organize the empire and provided resources that outlived individual sultans.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The Ottoman ʿilmiyye offered a relatively structured hierarchy of appointments but cannot be equated with a single centralized clergy. Gilles Veinstein's chapter in The Cambridge History of Turkey documents how judges, mosques, Friday sermons, colleges and pious foundations became part of Ottoman governance from its early period, while scholars were originally recruited from other regions as well. Later institutions changed over time; do not project the sixteenth-century hierarchy back onto the fourteenth century.

A judge in an Ottoman town faced questions raised by actual families and markets, while sultanic qānūn regulated matters such as tax and administration. The relationship between juristic tradition and imperial rule varied by subject and province; a law code is not evidence that everyone obeyed it. Endowments supported services but also elite households. Sufi lodges and the pilgrimage connected people across frontiers, including Arab provinces with established scholarly traditions. Rulers patronized colleges and courts, but jurists, household teachers and spiritual lineages retained forms of learning that governments did not wholly control. Ordinary subjects encountered the empire through courts, markets, taxes, village administration and worship, not solely through the decrees of Constantinople.

An Ottoman court register provides a different view of the state than an imperial legal code. In a register, litigants present claims about inheritance, debts or property, and the qāḍī records settlements within a social world in which witnesses, custom and political order matter. The imperial law code reveals how the ruler intended to regulate some activities. These documents may agree or differ without either being irrelevant. A madrasa certificate, meanwhile, records a different kind of authority, that of learned transmission. The empire's durability required all three—imperial command, local adjudication and teaching—but their relations varied. Ottoman court records, the ʿilmiyye hierarchy, endowments and regional Sufi institutions document distinct and sometimes competing forms of authority.

Safavid Iran and Mughal India: rival confessional and imperial settlements

Across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, two other great states established different relationships between religious authority and government. In Iran, the Safavids made Twelver Shiʿism central to imperial identity and supported scholars able to teach its law and traditions; this process altered the religious institutions of towns and provinces over generations. In India, Mughal rulers governed populations with multiple languages, religious affiliations and local landholding arrangements. Persian served important administrative and literary functions, while legal scholars, Sufis, temples, shrines and regional elites occupied different positions within the court's world. Mughal policies varied from Akbar's experiments in courtly religious discussion and imperial inclusion to Aurangzeb's patronage of Ḥanafī legal compilation. In both empires, rulers required reliable revenue and armies, while religious teachers depended upon patrons and communities whose loyalties did not always match the state's priorities. The result was neither one universal early-modern Islamic order nor a simple division between spirituality and politics: different institutions formed through different bargains among scholarship, authority and the needs of diverse populations.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The Safavid adoption of Twelver Shiʿism was a state project with long and uneven social consequences, not an instant change in the convictions of all Iran's inhabitants in 1501. The arrival and support of scholars from regions with established Imāmī learning are part of that history. The relation of rulers to jurists also changed between Ismāʿīl I and later Safavid reigns. Mughal policies toward religious communities, revenue and scholarship differed among rulers and provinces, and changed across time. Akbar's courtly debates, revenue administration and imperial household were not interchangeable institutions; the Fatāwā-yi ʿĀlamgīrī is a major compilation whose practical reception demands separate research.

Safavid Isfahan supported philosophers and theologians including Mullā Ṣadrā, while Indian scholarly networks developed curricula in law, ḥadīth, logic and spiritual discipline. Philosophers, jurists and spiritual authors within each empire disputed one another's positions rather than expressing a uniform imperial doctrine. Shāh Walī Allāh's eighteenth-century work later connected South Asian hadith, law and spiritual concerns in a setting shaped by Mughal fragmentation. Comparative studies of the three empires should account for revenue systems, military organization, confessional policy and local communities before making large claims about decline or renewal. Safavid patronage of Twelver Shiʿi scholarship gave Iran institutions different from those sustained by Ottoman Sunni and Mughal scholarly networks.

Oceans, finance and the changing balance of power

While the great Muslim empires governed extensive territories, European states and commercial companies expanded their maritime activities. Portuguese fleets entered the Indian Ocean at the end of the fifteenth century; Dutch and English companies later established trading posts, fortified ports and systems of credit and coercion. They encountered a crowded commercial world already served by Arab, Indian, Persian, African, Armenian, Jewish and other merchants. Some Asian rulers cooperated with Europeans, others resisted them, and regional trade did not immediately become European property. Over subsequent centuries, however, Atlantic commerce, new financial institutions, industrial production and military changes altered the resources available to competing states. Ottoman, Iranian and Indian governments faced fiscal and administrative pressures that had different local histories. The problem was no longer simply that one dynasty had weakened while another rose; technologies of production, credit, warfare and communication were changing the relations among distant societies. Religious institutions in Muslim lands would meet these transformations through reforms of government, learning and the organization of law.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Hodgson's 'Great Western Transmutation' describes changes in technology, communication, production and social organization that accompanied and exceeded Europe's overseas conquests. Atlantic credit, maritime trade and continuing Asian commercial networks developed together, challenging claims of a sudden and universal European monopoly. The familiar claim that Europeans simply monopolized the Indian Ocean from 1500 is false; studies of Gujarat and the Gulf document cosmopolitan communities and continuing Asian participation into the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Nor should a single Ottoman defeat be made the beginning of a uniform Muslim 'decline.'

Material change mattered to intellectual and religious life through identifiable intermediaries. A state unable to finance armies might reform taxes, borrow money or reorganize offices. A trading company that acquired territorial power could alter courts and property claims. Printed books and eventually telegraphy changed the speed and scale at which arguments moved. These were uneven transformations: an endowment-supported teacher in Cairo, a village jurist in India and a merchant family on the Swahili coast experienced them differently. Guénon criticized modern conceptions of knowledge and quantity, a metaphysical interpretation distinct from documentary evidence about finance, commerce and administration.

Surat's maritime trade demonstrates why European arrival did not produce instant European control. Merchants of different religions and regions financed vessels, exchanged credit and relied on commercial partnerships; European companies entered these existing networks while later acquiring territorial power in different ports. Political control over a harbour, a monopoly over a commodity and control of an inland agrarian region are three different achievements. In Europe itself, changing relations among taxation, debt and military expenditure affected how states competed. Hodgson's 'transmutation' and traditionalist critiques of modern civilization address different questions: the former seeks historical mechanisms of change; the latter evaluates the intellectual and spiritual meaning of modern modes of life. Their conclusions can be compared only after the subject of each has been specified.

Colonial courts and the transformation of Islamic law

At Khaybar, ʿUmar obtained valuable land and sought the Prophet's ﷺ guidance concerning its use. He was advised to preserve the underlying property and devote its yield to charitable purposes. ʿUmar established conditions for its continuing preservation and benefit, naming relatives, the poor, travellers, guests and other recipients. He permitted a responsible administrator appropriate use of its produce. One person's valuable acquisition thereby became a continuing source of provision for people whose needs and relationships differed.

The arrangement offered a model of property ordered toward purposes extending beyond its owner's life. Later jurists formulated the conditions through which an endowment could be constituted, interpreted and protected. Questions arose concerning the founder's intention, ownership, the beneficiaries' rights, the administrator's competence and the court's authority to enforce its provisions. Endowed property came to support households, instruction, places of worship and other public goods within different local forms of governance.

In Bengal, a family settlement executed in 1868 brought similar questions before a changed legal order. The deed provided benefits to descendants, with an ultimate charitable destination after the family's line ended. Litigation carried the arrangement through provincial courts to an imperial appellate tribunal. In 1894 the final judgment rejected the endowment's validity on the ground that its eventual charitable purpose was too remote. New legislation in 1913 then recognized qualifying family endowments, and a further enactment in 1930 gave that recognition qualified retrospective force.

The encounter at Khaybar and the later case concern the ordering of property, kinship, enduring benefit and public judgment. Their historical settings introduce different arrangements of authority and evidence. The moral end of stewardship persists as a guiding principle; legal interpretation determines the validity of specific instruments; public institutions give those determinations effective force. The resulting history records the passage of a prophetic example through differentiated legal reasoning into the changing political structures of a much wider civilization.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

The original question concerns the proper direction of a valuable asset. ʿUmar has acquired land that could provide personal wealth. His request for guidance draws the possession into an order extending toward God and other people. The answer distinguishes the continuing substance of the property from the distributed yield; the founder's conditions identify recipients and govern the manager. The same decision operates in the owner's conscience, the network of dependants and beneficiaries, and the wider community sustained by the asset.1

This encounter admits an analysis through the three practical orders. Personal ethics concerns detachment from absolute possession, generosity and the honest exercise of responsibility. Household governance concerns the legitimate claims of kin, inherited property and the arrangements through which future members receive provision. Political governance concerns recognition of the charitable foundation, public protection of its terms and the judicial powers available when parties disagree. The dimensions interact through one legal instrument. Their unity consists in the effective ordering of property toward an enduring good.2

Fiqh subsequently determines the normative form of this relationship. A jurist must decide what constitutes an effective dedication, what remains tied to its original purpose, how conditions are interpreted, which beneficiaries can claim distributions and what constitutes misconduct by the administrator. The legal determination protects both the founder's intention and the position of future claimants. Historical juristic schools developed different formulations of ownership, bindingness and family endowments. Their technical distinctions became teachable through cases, manuals, judgments and the routines of courts.3

The Sylhet dispute shows a later transformation in the relations through which such determinations acquire public force. Two brothers executed a deed in December 1868 favouring descendants and providing a final charitable purpose. The provincial courts and the imperial appellate tribunal had distinct capacities to interpret the deed and declare its legal effect. In December 1894 the Privy Council concluded that the postponed charitable benefit was too remote to validate the settlement. Legislation in 1913 then authorized qualifying family endowments; the statute in 1930 extended its operation to earlier instruments subject to rights previously acquired. The controversy reveals a relocation of decisive interpretive authority within a colonial state, followed by political and legislative revision.4

The underlying metaphysical relation is the human power to order created goods through knowledge and right intention. Property has material properties and yields; families have continuing needs; courts possess enforceable procedures; states exercise public power. The comprehensive prophetic orientation gathers these distinct objects under a moral end. Its historical inheritance is distributed among jurists, judges, beneficiaries, administrators and law-makers. Each must know the facts and claims appropriate to that office. The history of ʿumrān explains how those offices arise, how they compete over authority and how their actions transform the common order.5

Renewal through teaching, reform and print

Scholars and teachers responded to political and educational change by returning to inherited texts, developing new institutions and seeking wider audiences. The printing press allowed Qurʾānic commentaries, legal manuals, devotional books and polemics to reach readers who might never have entered a major teaching circle. In India, Deoband developed a madrasa during the 1860s to preserve and renew learned disciplines under colonial conditions; Aligarh pursued a different educational path through English-language and modern instruction. In Cairo and other centres, reformers debated legal reasoning, religious schooling and the relationship between inherited disciplines and new sciences. Sufi communities, independent teachers and local scholars also continued older forms of transmission while adapting to railways, steamships and expanded pilgrimage. Such changes brought more readers into religious argument but sometimes weakened the authority of personal explanation: a printed excerpt could circulate without the teacher who had supplied its context. Schools and teachers now had to explain inherited doctrines to audiences formed by print, modern examinations and new divisions of professional and religious study.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Reformers disagreed over inherited curricula, state schooling, legal authority and new sciences; their programmes crossed any simple traditional–modern division. Deoband, Aligarh, reformist writers in Cairo, Ottoman educational institutions and West African learned networks pursued different objectives. Some expanded hadith study; some sought a reorganization of legal authority; some defended established Sufi instruction; some integrated administrative or scientific subjects. Printed editions also altered textual practice: a student's stable printed page differed from a manuscript exemplar annotated through personal reading, but neither medium guaranteed correct understanding.

The founding chronology of Deoband is reported as 1866 in institutional histories and 1867 in several scholarly reference works. The exact Hijrī/Gregorian conversion and the distinction between foundation and formal opening need direct comparison. Aligarh's initial school opened in 1875 and the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College followed in 1877. These were actual institutional experiments supported through different donors, teachers, languages, curricula and aspirations. Their success is best assessed through the competencies students acquired, the work they subsequently performed and the forms of religious life each helped sustain.

Al-Attas connects education to adab and the ordering of knowledge; Guénon criticizes the modern priority given to quantity. Their arguments interpret modernity at a different level from the institutional histories of particular schools. Al-Attas and traditionalist authors later offered distinct critiques of knowledge and modernity; neither expressed the intentions of every nineteenth-century Muslim teacher. The historical evidence involves school registers, syllabi, printed editions, biographies, correspondence and institutional records. The better question is which students each new arrangement served, what it enabled them to do, and what forms of knowledge became easier or harder to transmit. The answer will vary across classes, languages, genders and regions. Print, mass schooling and institutional examinations changed how teachers explained texts, corrected mistakes and assessed students' understanding.

Deoband and Aligarh represented distinct institutional responses among many forms of South Asian Muslim education. Deoband emerged through a specific scholarly and financial arrangement, while the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College developed its own residential and institutional aspirations. Women, merchant communities, village teachers, Sufi networks and other regional schools also shaped religious learning. Print made a legal opinion or devotional text more widely available, yet public circulation could intensify disputes over wording, authority and context. Examinations, lessons and student writings reveal differences between memorizing formulas, explaining a text and applying its reasoning to unfamiliar cases. This links the formation of malakāt to actual instructional practice without pretending that one curriculum was uniformly successful.

Nation-states, migration and the new scale of Muslim life

The twentieth century saw the Ottoman sultanate and caliphate abolished, colonial governments challenged, and many Muslim-majority states established or transformed through independence, partition and revolution. Borders divided communities that had long travelled through imperial, commercial and scholarly routes; migration then formed new congregations in Europe, the Americas and elsewhere. Mass schooling brought religious instruction to large populations while altering the places where qualified teachers were trained. Radio, television and digital media gave preachers and readers an audience far beyond their own towns. Pilgrimage by air connected Muslims who spoke different languages and lived under different legal systems, but shared recognisable rites and texts. Governments variously administered mosques, family law and universities, while private associations, scholars, families and spiritual lineages sustained additional kinds of authority. A person now might learn prayer from family, jurisprudence from a university course, religious guidance from an online lecture and spiritual discipline from a local teacher. The recurring question of the Sīrah—how knowledge becomes trustworthy action and enduring community—had acquired new conditions, not a single new answer.

Sharḥ · evidence and interpretation

Muslim-majority states, minority communities and religious institutions experienced distinct political and educational developments; no single pattern describes them all. Independence in India and Pakistan in 1947, Indonesian independence during 1945–49, the dissolution of Ottoman political institutions and distinct African decolonizations took place under different conditions. State ministries, traditional schools, Sufi networks, universities and migrant associations cannot be assigned one universal effect. The transformation of broadcast and digital communication also has contrary possibilities: access to large textual corpora expands, while the authority of a circulating quotation can become detached from the circumstances of its transmission.

In Madinah, believers developed arrangements for shared obligations and adjudication; later institutions addressed related problems through different legal and communicative structures. In seventh-century Madinah, a new community needed ways to place trust in reports, resolve disputes and act together; later hadith, legal and spiritual traditions elaborated forms of authority that could travel beyond their first setting. Today the material conditions of access and repetition have changed again. A transmitted text, its juristic interpretation, its practice in daily life and later spiritual explanations remain distinct even when one person engages all four. Digital circulation expands access to religious material without guaranteeing correct interpretation or lasting habits. It yields concrete research questions: what does a student know after repeated short videos, what can they independently verify, who corrects their misunderstanding, and how does learning change conduct over time? A student's ability to identify sources, explain their meaning and apply what they learn supplies concrete evidence of understanding.

Online archives lower the cost of finding religious texts, but understanding them still requires linguistic, historical and interpretive skills. A scanned manuscript, a search result from a hadith database, a short devotional clip and a university lecture each give different access to evidence and guidance. Digital texts and short lectures often circulate without their original context; checking the source, language and commentary remains necessary for accurate interpretation. Teachers still identify errors that access to a document alone cannot correct. Mass media and searchable digital texts expanded access to religious information, while interpretation and durable habits still required sustained practice and correction. Digital archives broaden access to sources while placing greater demands on independent verification and sustained study.
