VI · An international civilization with many centres

Chapters 34–42. Each case asks which persons did what, how their actions affected durable practices, and what the cited evidence does not establish.

34 · Cairo, Damascus, JerusalemAl-Azhar was established under the Fāṭimids (970–972); later rulers and scholars made the same setting part of different teaching worlds.Political patronage supplies buildings and endowments; teachers, pupils, doctrinal appointments and ordinary worship determine what actually continues. The building's survival does not prove a single uninterrupted curriculum.The shift from Ismāʿīlī patronage to Sunni teaching requires period-by-period study, not a single institutional origin story. al-Azhar historical account
35 · Granada and the MaghribThe November 1491 surrender terms promised protections; after the January 1492 surrender, later policies undermined Muslim religious and communal life.Property, courts, language, teachers and households were affected as authorities changed what earlier guarantees allowed. Loss of state power does not imply identical loss of teaching for all persons.Separate the original capitulations from events in 1499–1502 and subsequent Morisco histories. 36 · Persianate and multilingual worldsDārā Shukoh sponsored a Persian Upaniṣad translation, completed in 1657 as the Sirr-i akbar, through a collective scholarly process.Translators, Sanskrit specialists, scribes and patrons moved technical ideas across languages. Across the wider Persianate world, colophons, licenses and ownership notes may document actual routes of a book.Dārā's claim about hidden scripture is his interpretation, not an established Qurʾānic identification or an Akbarian consensus. Munis Faruqui on Dārā
37 · Mongol IranGhāzān's 1295 conversion took place amid Ilkhanid political conflict; Nawrūz and allied military networks were important.A ruler's religious commitment altered patronage, court symbols and some institutional arrangements through administration and military power; local adoption varied.Courtly narratives and later Mamluk reports need comparison. Conversion of a sovereign is not proof of every subject's belief or the ruler's inward motives. Encyclopaedia Iranica: Ghāzān
38 · Timbuktu and the Middle NigerAfter the Moroccan invasion of 1591, jurist Aḥmad Bābā was displaced to Morocco; scholarship continued despite upheaval and losses.Teachers, pupils, family collections, copies and trans-Saharan learned relations allowed juristic teaching to extend beyond a single ruler, while conquest destroyed some of its material supports.Use dated manuscripts and biographies cautiously. Scholarly continuity cannot erase the losses or establish every reported detail of exile. Tombouctou Manuscripts Project
39 · Melaka and the monsoon portsAccounts associated with Tomé Pires describe merchant-facing harbour administration divided among four syahbandars.Security, translators, lodging, trade duties and dispute settlement enabled many language and merchant groups to participate in one port without identical customs.The number and jurisdictions of harbour masters need verification against the early witnesses. Commercial administration cannot establish the inward faith of its participants. Maritime scholarly reconstruction
40 · Bagerhat and the Bengal deltaKhān Jahān's tomb inscription attests his death in 1459; later histories associate him with wetland settlement, waterworks, roads and mosques.Local irrigation, land grants, cultivation, religious foundations and teaching could coincide in new villages; none automatically entailed private conversion.Date the inscription separately from later popular traditions. Agrarian, political and religious movements require distinct evidence. Richard Eaton: Bengal Frontier
41 · Ottoman AintabIn July 1541, Haciye Sabah's classes for women became the subject of a court prosecution, followed by public punishment and banishment.Household teaching, gendered expectations and a judge's authority intersected in a single documented dispute. A court record does not equate the power to punish with justice.The surviving recorded allegations must not be treated as transparent access to every participant's intention. The punishment is described, not endorsed. Leslie Peirce: Haciye Sabah
42 · Safavid Iran and Mughal IndiaUnder Aurangzeb, Ḥanafī jurists led by Niẓām al-Dīn of Burhānpur compiled the Fatāwā-yi ʿĀlamgīrī.Imperial funding, juristic comparison, scribes and existing Ḥanafī books produced a durable compilation that could travel through teaching, copying and later courts.The existence of a compendium does not show that every Mughal case followed it. Safavid confessional change was a distinct, regionally uneven political process. National Museum, New Delhi manuscript

From particular people to a distributed inheritance

Al-Azhar, Granada, Persianate translation circles, Mongol Iran, Timbuktu, Melaka, Bengal, Ottoman towns and Mughal India did not form one political order. Teachers, jurists, families, merchants, rulers, patrons and translators transmitted distinct parts of inherited religious learning under changing circumstances. A patron can fund a building without making every pupil knowledgeable; a ruler may convert without changing every subject's faith; a scholar may continue to teach while dispossessed of books and freedom.

The Akbarian account of comprehensive Muhammadan inheritance can guide a later theological interpretation of the legitimate relations among worship, law, intellectual learning and spiritual formation. It cannot establish a lost manuscript route or the unreported thoughts of an early actor.

VII · Modern transformation

Chapters 43–46. Modern institutions changed the means of exercising authority, educating people and communicating inherited texts; they did not do so identically in every region.

43 · Indian Ocean trade and financePortuguese arrival by the Cape route in 1498 preceded the armed seizure of Goa in 1510; East India Company revenue authority expanded in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa in 1765.Local merchants, brokers and officials at Surat and Swally negotiated foreign access. 44 · Colonial law and family waqfAn 1868 family waqf deed was rejected in the Privy Council's 1894 judgment; Acts of 1913 and 1930 changed the applicable legal framework.Family records, litigants, translators, legal advisers, colonial courts and parliament reshaped how a known legal institution was interpreted, decided and enforced.The dispute documents one important institutional shift, not one settled view of all Ḥanafī law. Distinguish the 1913 prospective Act from 1930's qualified retrospective extension. Privy Council judgment, 1894
45 · Deoband, Aligarh and printDeoband's founding is dated 1866 in some institutional histories and 1867 in scholarly references. Aligarh's school began in 1875 and its college in 1877.Donors, classrooms, vernacular instruction, manuscripts and printed editions created different ways of sustaining inherited scholarship and training for new occupations.Do not use an unresolved year to claim a precise anniversary; distinguish Deoband's and Aligarh's actual courses, financial models and aims. Metcalf, Encyclopaedia Iranica
46 · States, migration and digital learningThe Ottoman sultanate ended in 1922 and its caliphate in 1924; the 1947 partition and Indonesian independence during 1945–49 had different local consequences.Ministries, courts, universities, families, diaspora mosques and digital archives mediate religious instruction under newly differentiated jurisdictions. The digital Fatāwā-yi ʿĀlamgīrī project remains ongoing.Searchable texts are not verified manuscript editions or juristic competence. Different nation-states and migrant communities cannot be assigned one uniform fate. Van Bruinessen: Abolition of the Caliphate

From legal documents and schools to searchable archives

Colonial decisions changed who could judge an inherited waqf claim and with what binding effect. Deoband and Aligarh organized learning through different financial, linguistic and curricular decisions. States, ministries and migrant associations inherited different public responsibilities. Digital search expands access to learning while still requiring authentic witnesses, sound interpretation, practice and correction.

Evidence status. These are a source-oriented comparative index. Some cases rest on published reconstructions of primary materials, not direct new manuscript collation; the 138-claim ledger, the Miʿrāj witness matrix, external scholarly review and rendered browser QA remain open.
