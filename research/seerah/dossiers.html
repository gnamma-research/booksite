Critical Sources and Dossiers

These dossiers expand the sources and causal explanations introduced in the sharḥ. They are not a third running commentary and do not claim comprehensive collation of original manuscripts. Each is linked back to the reading chapters that it illuminates.

The reports and arguments reproduced or summarized here have different evidentiary status. Comparison of published recensions is not the same as independent collation of their manuscript witnesses.

Critical dossier 1

Meccan protection, public recitation, and the Hijrah: one report read closely (chapters 8–11)

The early Muslim community cannot be understood by simply saying that its members were persecuted and then migrated. They belonged to households whose members enjoyed unequal degrees of protection. Some could withdraw into a powerful clan, others were dependent clients, and some had no effective protector. Religious instruction therefore travelled through existing households even as it called into question the inherited basis of standing within them. The clearest way of seeing this interaction is through the long report transmitted from ʿĀʾishah by ʿUrwah b. al-Zubayr, from Ibn Shihāb al-Zuhrī, in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, no. 3905, chapter on migration to Madinah. The chain matters: it is a report from an early family and scholarly network, not a series of contemporaneously dated depositions for every episode it narrates.

The report describes Abū Bakr setting out toward Aksum when opposition made life in Makkah difficult. At Bark al-Ghimād he met Ibn al-Daghinah, a tribal leader who offered him jiwār—public protection. Ibn al-Daghinah justified the offer by referring to Abū Bakr's care for relatives, dependants, guests and people in distress. Protection was thus negotiated through a reputation for social obligation recognizable even to people who did not share his belief. The Meccan notables accepted the arrangement on the condition that Abū Bakr worship quietly within his house. When he began reciting in a prayer-space in his courtyard, women and children gathered to listen; his public recitation unsettled the notables, who pressed the guarantor to restrict him. Abū Bakr refused to confine worship in the demanded manner and returned the guarantee. The passage joins religious emotion, domestic space, public attention, kinship prestige and the enforceable conditions of protection in one narrated sequence. It gives a much better explanation of the social stakes of preaching than a generic statement that the old elite feared Islam.

The crucial difference between private and public recitation was not a change of doctrine. A man reading aloud in view of other households created an audience not fully controlled by patriarchs or clan elders. The report presents women and children attending to the recitation; it does not give demographic figures for conversion or license a claim that all women or children adopted the message. It shows how a practice that was legally tolerable within the home became politically contentious at its threshold. In this setting, sincerity, steadfastness and courage were not abstract virtues added by later writers: they were manifested in a decision whether to surrender protection in order to continue a practice. Yet the subsequent precise interior state of Abū Bakr is known only insofar as the report describes it; later accounts of spiritual stations should not be made to stand in for historical testimony.

The same report proceeds to the prospect of a new destination, describes the Prophet's permission to migrate, Abū Bakr's preparation of mounts, the flight and cave, and arrival in the oasis. Its story of preparation is not evidence that every migrant undertook identical measures. Its value for the narrative lies in the sequence: protection at Makkah had limits; Aksum offered refuge under a Christian ruler but not a Muslim public order; Yathrib, through instruction and pledges, promised a place in which believers could defend and administer collective obligations. The Hijrah was therefore at once flight from danger, obedience to guidance, and the beginning of new burdens upon hosts and newcomers.

Textual control. The report is al-Bukhārī 3905; the Arabic chain and extended narrative can also be checked in the numbered Arabic transcript. The matn of chapters 10–11 should tell the episode. The sharḥ must distinguish the narrated facts, the inferred social mechanism (control over audiences and protection), and the spiritual reading (the testing of constancy). Open tasks: compare parallel reports of jiwār and the ʿAqabah pledges in Ibn Isḥāq/Ibn Hishām and al-Ṭabarī before treating speeches and precise sequences as independently confirmed.

Critical dossier 2

The Medinan *kitāb*: obligations before constitutional theory (chapter 12)

When the emigrants arrived in Yathrib, they entered an already inhabited and politically divided oasis. Households owned date gardens and had to manage labour, water, blood-compensation, captivity and military alliances. The survival of the new fellowship demanded a working answer to practical questions. Who pays compensation when a member of one group causes a death? Who helps ransom a captive? Who contributes to defence if a neighbouring coalition attacks? The transmitted kitāb attributed to the Prophet addresses precisely such problems. Its clauses are preserved in Ibn Isḥāq's material through Ibn Hishām and in another transmitted recension associated with Abū ʿUbayd's Kitāb al-Amwāl. The existence of recensions calls for comparison; it does not justify either dismissing the whole document or treating one modern clause-numbering system as an original feature of the text.

The opening describes participants as «أُمَّةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ مِنْ دُونِ النَّاسِ»—a distinct community or association. But subsequent clauses do not erase inherited group membership. They refer to the emigrants and named Yathrib clans retaining their arrangements for collective compensation and ransoming captives. The recurrent expression «يَتَعَاقَلُونَ مَعَاقِلَهُمُ الأُولَى» makes precisely that continuity visible. An injured family would not suddenly receive compensation from an anonymous modern treasury; actual groups still bore responsibilities while entering a wider system of obligation. For the newly arrived emigrants, arrangements of mutual support had to operate alongside these obligations. Religious allegiance did not automatically furnish food, land or enforceable claims. It created grounds on which people could undertake and judge those claims together.

The document also contains clauses about Jewish participants, their religion, expenditures and mutual aid. In one recension the brief wording «لِلْيَهُودِ دِينُهُمْ وَلِلْمُسْلِمِينَ دِينُهُمْ» appears. This is important evidence of specified religious differentiation, but it must not be taken to imply that every Jewish group in the oasis had signed the same instrument, or that its provisions amounted to a modern charter of equal individual citizenship. Michael Lecker's detailed study argues that participation was more restricted than popular presentations suppose and that some major groups had separate arrangements. The 2024 publication of his revised study contains separate chapters on Muslim and Jewish participants, the treaties, dating, unity, recensions and preservation. These are disagreements about which persons and obligations were included, not merely arguments over terminology.

This illuminates a transition that religious or political slogans conceal. The religious umma emerging around the Prophet included believers bound by worship and allegiance. But its public security could require arrangements with groups of different religion. Religious fellowship and political protection were therefore partly overlapping, partly distinct associations. The Prophet's adjudicating role operated at that intersection. The kitāb presents conditions for making cooperation predictable; it does not show that every clan ceased to function or that disputes ended. Nor does it establish by itself when each later Medinan command concerning marriage, property, fasting or inheritance was revealed. Those commands should be established separately in the relevant textual histories.

Source and limit. Base text: Ibn Hishām, section on the document, checked against a second published presentation of the attributed wording. Modern comparison: Michael Lecker, The “Constitution of Medina”: Muhammad's First Legal Document (2004), author's institutional summary, and the 2024 revised edition's chapter architecture. The present edition has checked the passages and scholarship but has not transcribed and line-by-line collated both complete recensions; clause numbering should therefore be treated as an editorial convenience, not definitive textual segmentation.

Critical dossier 3

Al-Ḥudaybiyyah: a difficult treaty as a test of communal judgment (chapter 16)

The Prophet's party approached Makkah with the purpose of pilgrimage rather than a declared attempt to take the city. Quraysh's obstruction forced a choice: withdraw without entering, fight, or seek an enforceable arrangement. The narrative in al-Bukhārī 2731–2732 gives political deliberation and spiritual trial as one event, not two unrelated lessons. The party manoeuvred to avoid cavalry, halted near al-Ḥudaybiyyah, and exchanged envoys. Negotiations culminated in Suhayl b. ʿAmr's arrival and a text whose disputed wording dramatized the difficulty of recognizing parties while each asserted different religious claims.

The terms deferred the intended pilgrimage, regulated a subsequent return visit, established a truce and addressed the treatment of fugitives from Makkah. The clause concerning a person arriving from Quraysh without a guardian's permission was especially painful when Abū Jandal, Suhayl's son, appeared in chains. It is insufficient to say that the treaty was a strategic success; that description leaves out what the participants thought they were surrendering. The report includes ʿUmar's distressed questions and the Prophet's insistence that his trust in the outcome did not exempt him from carrying out the agreed decision. The episode's immediate object was not trade expansion or later conversion statistics, but an encounter between fidelity to revelation and the difficulty of obeying a command whose effects were not yet visible.

A second scene explains the transition from agreement on paper to actual public conduct. After the terms had been made, the Prophet instructed the pilgrims to sacrifice and shave. The report describes their hesitation. Umm Salamah advised him to perform the rites himself without further exhortation; the Companions then followed. This is a rare sequence in which a political agreement, domestic counsel, ritual action and collective discipline are causally linked. The prior blockage of a religious goal generated grief and confusion; a visible act resolved the immediate practical impasse more effectively than repeating an order. The source allows us to say this. It does not license the stronger claim that we can identify every individual's motive for hesitation.

The familiar judgment that al-Ḥudaybiyyah proved an opening (fatḥ) belongs to the transmitted religious interpretation and to the subsequent sequence of events. The truce helped alter the terms in which groups could approach the Muslim community, and later alliance violations preceded the march on Makkah. Yet the exact growth of adherents between 628 and 630 cannot be calculated simply from this one account. A treaty opens opportunities; the inference that it mechanically caused every subsequent conversion would outrun the evidence. Nor should the restrictions on returning women be treated as permanently identical to the initial terms: the report relates subsequent revelation and a distinction in their treatment.

Limited comparison of two published narrative recensions. The version of Ibn Isḥāq preserved by Ibn Hishām (2:319, passages headed What troubled the people at the treaty and the arrival of Abū Jandal, and The Prophet sacrificed and shaved, and the people followed) describes Abū Jandal arriving in irons during the writing, the public distress at returning without entering Makkah, and the Prophet's sacrifice and shaving after the treaty. On seeing him, the Companions proceeded to sacrifice and shave. This part of Ibn Hishām's published presentation does not recount Umm Salamah's intervening counsel. Al-Bukhārī 2731–2732 describes the hesitation after three instructions and attributes the solution to Umm Salamah's advice that the Prophet act first without further speech. These accounts share the crucial sequence—disappointing treaty, sacrifice and shaving by the Prophet, communal imitation—but they give differently detailed explanations of the intervening action. The narrower comparison confirms the reported role of visible Prophetic example; it does not demonstrate that the earlier redaction denied Umm Salamah's role. Other transmissions linked to Ibn Isḥāq preserve it. The published texts have been compared here, not their complete manuscript witnesses or all chains. Primary passages: Ibn Hishām on Abū Jandal, Ibn Hishām on sacrifice and shaving, and al-Bukhārī 2731–2732. For an additional Ibn Isḥāq-associated line mentioning Umm Salamah, compare al-Dhahabī's presentation.

Source and limit. Al-Bukhārī 2731–2732, Kitāb al-Shurūṭ, chapter on treaties is the detailed base witness; compare the continuation at 2732. Qurʾān 48:1–29 and 60:10 belong in the sharḥ, where their theological and legal roles can be distinguished from what the narrator reports. A limited comparison with the published Ibn Hishām presentation has now been made; full manuscript comparison, systematic isnād-cum-matn analysis, and dating of subsequent tribal breaches remain outstanding. This dossier establishes a coherent, source-grounded explanation of a difficult event; it does not constitute a full isnād-cum-matn collation.

Critical dossier 4

Khaybar: cultivation, revenue and the limits of political simplification (chapter 17)

The military settlement at Khaybar created a problem familiar in agrarian conquests: an army could acquire claims to land without possessing the expertise or labour to cultivate its orchards immediately. Evicting all cultivators could remove the productive workforce and reduce the harvest; leaving production unchanged could sustain families whose relations to the new authority had altered. The transmitted arrangement answered that problem through terms of cultivation and a share of produce. This institutional detail matters because a victory in a chronicle does not explain how a garrison or community lives in the following season.

Ibn ʿUmar's report in al-Bukhārī 2331 states that the Prophet assigned the Khaybar lands to their Jewish cultivators so that they would work and cultivate them, taking half the yield. A related formulation appears at al-Bukhārī 4248. A short operative phrase in the Arabic is «وَلَهُمْ شَطْرُ مَا خَرَجَ مِنْهَا». This is concrete evidence of a crop-sharing arrangement. It tells us something about the distribution of produce, and by implication the need to maintain agricultural work after conquest. It does not tell us the complete land register, the duration of every household's tenure, the legal status of every parcel, or the motives of each party. Nor does it prove a general policy toward all religious minorities in all later Islamic states.

The two Bukhārī passages should not be called two wholly independent contemporary documentary witnesses. Both transmit the report through the Ibn ʿUmar–Nāfiʿ line with different later narrators and slight wording variation. The distinction matters because corroboration through related transmission lines differs from independent material corroboration. Later jurists debated sharecropping, labour, fiscal rights and the conditions of such arrangements. The chapter on jurisprudence should show what problem later legal reasoning needed to resolve instead of presenting Khaybar as the unqualified source of a single doctrine of tenancy.

The case also corrects a one-dimensional account of public authority. Worship gave believers a moral understanding of their obligations, but it did not automatically determine the harvest, the proportions of produce, the appointment of collectors or the settlement of disputes. Those activities required terms, agents and procedures. A legal-historical sharḥ can explain these without portraying the Prophet's decisions as mere economic necessity: the reported choice is both a practical judgment under particular circumstances and an action that later jurists studied within a broader religious framework.

Source and limit. Al-Bukhārī 2331 and 4248, both in the Ibn ʿUmar–Nāfiʿ transmission. The causal connection between cultivation and fiscal continuity is an inference from the agricultural and military setting, not a direct statement of the report. Further work: variants in al-Muwaṭṭaʾ and Muslim's collection, administrative reports and later juristic discussion of musāqāt and muzāraʿa. Those materials have not been comprehensively collated here.

Critical dossier 5

The *Muwaṭṭaʾ*: when teaching became a text without becoming motionless (chapters 27–29)

The transmitted works of early jurists were not published like modern single-edition books. A student heard a teacher, studied a written copy, sometimes read it back for approval, and passed on a certified recension. A teacher could continue to expand, omit or reorganize material in subsequent lessons. The resulting variants do not by themselves mean that the book had no author; nor do they mean that all surviving copies represent precisely one unchanged dictation. This is central to the emergence of Islamic scholarly authority: a ruling was preserved through human learning practices, and differences among those practices became material for further learned comparison.

Mālik b. Anas's Muwaṭṭaʾ is an unusually well-documented case. Ahmed El Shamsy's detailed study compares the surviving complete recensions of Yaḥyā b. Yaḥyā al-Laythī, Abū Muṣʿab al-Zuhrī and Ibn Bukayr with quotations of Mālik preserved in al-Shāfiʿī's Kitāb al-Umm. In one case about compensating an injury, the position remains almost identical while a verb describing earlier authorities' activity changes. In another, the vocabulary used in a reciprocal gift exchange varies by one word without apparently changing the legal reasoning. These are minor differences. But the examples concerning a rule's degree of scholarly acceptance matter more: phrases equivalent to “the rule in our view” and “the agreed rule among us” attribute different degrees of collective authority to a position. They are not interchangeable editorial ornament.

A particularly instructive example concerns Ibn ʿUmar's reported practice of sprinkling water in his eyes when performing the major ablution. Yaḥyā's recension preserves the practice without an explicit attached judgment by Mālik, while al-Shāfiʿī quotes Mālik as saying it was not accepted practice; Abū Muṣʿab and Ibn Bukayr preserve comparable statements that the action was not obligatory. El Shamsy's comparison supplies exact identifiers: al-Umm 8:696; the Yaḥyā recension no. 113; Abū Muṣʿab no. 124; Ibn Bukayr no. 128. This is an example where absence or presence of a teacher's comment can materially affect what a reader believes the teacher required. A lay reader selecting the most familiar recension might never see the interpretive problem.

A second example concerns chapter organization. El Shamsy shows that the chapter on testimony appears in markedly different positions within the Book of Judicial Rulings across the recensions. This is not evidence that one text's hadith statements were all invented; it demonstrates that the pedagogical arrangement of material also belonged to the history of transmission. A reader studying from chapter headings should therefore distinguish Mālik's teachings, later or concurrent editorial arrangement, and a school's retrospective classification of its authorities. The text's authority was produced and maintained by identifiable practices: reading, hearing, comparison, disputation, correction and regional teaching.

The historical consequence becomes clear through the expansion of legal schools. A school was not simply a student's uncritical reproduction of Mālik's or Abū Ḥanīfah's every opinion. Jurists compared precedent, authenticated reports, distinguished locally received practice from transmitted statement, and trained successors who could apply or criticize inherited reasoning. The Muwaṭṭaʾ recensions reveal an early layer of these processes; they cannot by themselves explain every later Mālikī development, still less the origin of all madhhabs. Nor is Muḥammad al-Shaybānī's work best treated as a straightforward equivalent of the three complete recensions: El Shamsy describes it more precisely as a partial critical commentary.

Textual apparatus and outstanding task. Ahmed El Shamsy, “The Ur-Muwaṭṭaʾ and Its Recensions,” Islamic Law and Society 28 (2021), 352–381, especially pp. 361–369 for examples and pp. 376–377 for chapter-order differences. The article gives exact modern-edition identifiers and compares specific witnesses, including at least one relevant manuscript. This edition has examined his detailed published comparison; it has not independently consulted every Arabic recension or the manuscript facsimiles, so these remain secondary-mediated variant reports, not a new diplomatic transcription. Critical dossier 6

Learning to act: al-Ghazālī, al-Qushayrī and the Shādhilī explanation of inward formation (chapters 9, 13, 32–33)

The Sīrah contains people praying, reciting, sheltering emigrants, sharing resources, resisting persecution, disputing wealth and accepting judgments. Later disciplines of spirituality were not already present in their later technical form. Nevertheless, the actions provided enduring questions: what makes an act of worship sincere? What habits allow a person to give while facing scarcity? Why can outward conformity coexist with vanity, anger or cruelty? To answer them we must distinguish (a) narrated practices and Qurʾānic demands, (b) the historical development of explicit psychological and pedagogical methods, and (c) later metaphysical interpretations of spiritual realization.

Al-Ghazālī's introductory plan in the Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn is a model of genuine intellectual integration. He divides the work into worship, ordinary dealings, destructive qualities and saving qualities. These are not four unrelated collections. Worship establishes recurring acts that orient the person to God; customary dealings expose the person to property, family and social obligations; destructive qualities name dispositions that can corrupt either activity; saving qualities identify the habits of attention, intention and love through which action acquires a proper spiritual quality. Al-Ghazālī's own distinction between outward acts and the inward activity of the heart is therefore an account of how one moral agent participates in several sorts of obligation. It does not mean that an eleventh-century psychological framework was explicitly formulated by every Companion.

In al-Risālah al-Qushayriyyah, the chapter on sincerity transmits Abū ʿAlī al-Daqqāq's distinction between guarding one's act against consideration of other people's approval and guarding it against self-admiration. This is more precise than the sentence “Sufis taught sincerity.” It identifies two different obstacles: one can behave well in order to be praised by others; one can also inwardly admire one's own religious achievement even in solitude. A teacher must therefore ask more than whether the observable act took place. This produces a practical method of examination, not merely a vocabulary.

Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh makes the relationship particularly concise in al-Ḥikam: «الأَعْمالُ صُوَرٌ قَائِمَةٌ»—acts are standing forms—while their animating reality is the secret of sincerity. The contrast is not between useless outward law and valuable inward experience. It is between an act considered only by its visible configuration and the act considered as deliberately directed toward God. A debt can be repaid outwardly while the agent seeks admiration for generosity; a person's prayer can conform to required forms while attention and intention remain disordered. Jurisprudence and spiritual formation therefore concern distinguishable properties of the same action. The statement must not be used to excuse violations of the outward obligations that it seeks to animate.

The historical continuity lies in practices and questions, not in asserting uninterrupted identity of institutional form. Early prayer, fasting, generosity and repentance are directly attested in Prophetic teaching. The Iḥyāʾ, the Risālah and later Shādhilī circles made methods of assessing and cultivating dispositions more explicit. Organized Sufi lineages, teaching relationships, litanies and endowed residences are developments with their own documentary histories. What persists through these changes is the problem of integrating outward conduct and inward purpose; the shape of its solution must be studied in each author rather than assumed universally identical.

Primary texts and limits. Al-Ghazālī, Iḥyāʾ, introduction and the four quarters: Arabic searchable opening and edition information. Al-Qushayrī, al-Risālah, chapter on ikhlāṣ. Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh, al-Ḥikam, text of the relevant aphorism. This is a conceptual and transmitted-text comparison, not an independent stemmatic edition. The later explanations are identified as retrospective spiritual readings of early practice, not unreported words in the mouths of the Companions.

Critical dossier 7

Prophetic inheritance and *walāyah*: do not collapse practice, doctrine and ontology (chapters 19, 32–33)

The finality of Muḥammad's prophethood does not mean that instruction, judgment, sanctification or learned transmission ceased at his death. But those continuities are not one undifferentiated inheritance. A judge issues rulings by reference to authoritative evidence and established procedures; a ḥadīth transmitter preserves reports under a discipline of criticism; a spiritual teacher guides repentance and remembrance; a metaphysician asks what makes prophetic knowledge and human perfection possible. The historical institutions that sustained these activities often overlapped, while their claims to authority differed. The Sunni–Shiʿi discussion of succession can be acknowledged where necessary without making that controversy the foundation of the entire spiritual history.

Al-Ghazālī and al-Qushayrī treat walāyah in a field of spiritual practice and sanctification. Ibn ʿArabī makes walāyah central to a larger theory of divine knowledge, prophetic inheritance and realization. Souad Hakim's analysis of al-Futūḥāt emphasizes that the way of spiritual attainment can be considered through human conduct under divine commands and through witnessing; she explicitly cautions against imposing a single mandatory ladder of stations on Ibn ʿArabī. Thus obedience and spiritual realization are connected, but the relation is not that an act automatically produces a metaphysical degree as a measurable result. The act is a site of divine address and human response; realization cannot be reduced to a behavioral checklist.

Qūnawī's opening treatment of wujūd in Miftāḥ al-ghayb, also incorporated into al-Nuṣūṣ, introduces another order of explanation. In William Chittick's translation and commentary, Qūnawī distinguishes the one wujūd of the Real from the multiplicity of its relations and loci of manifestation. The passage concerns how plurality and knowledge are intelligible without multiplying the Real as such. It does not straightforwardly answer the historical question of how a particular family in Madinah learned to pray, or whether a treaty clause was accepted by a particular tribe. To import this metaphysics directly into those narratives would confuse ontological interpretation with documentary history.

The expression al-ḥaqīqah al-muḥammadiyyah likewise needs historical differentiation. The revised Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy article on Ibn ʿArabī observes that he used the term in passing, while Farghānī offered its first detailed systematic explanation. Farghānī's exposition is connected to Qūnawī's teaching on Ibn al-Fāriḍ's Naẓm al-sulūk, first interpreted in Persian and then extensively reworked in Arabic. We must therefore not treat Farghānī's mature metaphysical language as a verbatim formulation of the first Muslims, nor even as the detailed terminology of every passage in Ibn ʿArabī himself. The historically sound account traces a chain of intellectual formulation; the theological and metaphysical account then assesses its arguments on their own terms.

One fruitful integrative question remains: how can a religious act be at once legally valid, ethically virtuous, spiritually sincere and understood by later thinkers as a locus of divine disclosure? These are different formal considerations of a single act, not four separate events. A competent commentary should start with the event's evidence; identify the legal and moral questions it raises; then reconstruct the later spiritual argument with its own premises and textual witnesses. There is room for a traditionalist cyclical interpretation of sacred time, but Guénon's historical cycles cannot be used to supply missing isnāds or infer undocumented causes of seventh-century decisions.

Sources and outstanding work. Hakim, “The Way of Walaya or Sainthood”, examining al-Futūḥāt; Chittick, “Qūnawī on the One Wujūd”, translating an early chapter of Miftāḥ al-ghayb with specified manuscript controls; Stanford Encyclopedia, “Ibn ʿArabī”, revised 2025; Chittick, “Farghānī on the Muhammadan Reality”; and Encyclopaedia Iranica, Farghānī. In this edition Ibn ʿArabī, Qūnawī and Farghānī are compared through specialist translations and studies, not independently collated from their Arabic manuscript witnesses. The latter is a substantial separate project.

Critical dossier 8

International Islam: two regional mechanisms that must not be blended (chapters 38–40)

The spread of Islamic teaching through Africa and Asia was neither one centrally directed conquest nor a single wave of Sufi preaching. The processes differed with agriculture, settlement, trade, court patronage, writing systems and the density of scholarly institutions. Bengal and Melaka can be compared precisely because they challenge the assumption of a universal sequence in which a ruler converts, his subjects follow and Islamic institutions automatically appear.

In eastern Bengal, agrarian settlement, the extension of rice-growing into forest and delta environments, and the establishment of local religious institutions developed over long periods. Richard Eaton's study The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier, 1204–1760 argues that political control, agrarian expansion and processes of Islamization did not follow identical frontiers or identical dates. This is a causal thesis to be evaluated against settlement patterns, inscriptions, grants and regional history—not a claim that cultivation mechanically generated religious belief. It invites the sharḥ to ask who cleared land, how local lineages acquired legitimacy, which religious institutions endured and what language people used for instruction.

Melaka was a maritime entrepôt rather than a delta agricultural frontier. Seasonal trade connected diverse merchants and resident communities; courts negotiated passage, customs and disputes, while teachers and families circulated religious practices and texts. The political endorsement of Islam increased some institutional opportunities, but cannot be assumed to have instantly converted every visitor or resident. A careful chapter therefore moves from the practical need for trust and settlement to the possibility of sustained instruction, then tests actual court and literary evidence. Arabic, Persian and Malay-language contact should be documented rather than inferred from a generic picture of trade.

These examples illuminate Hodgson's distinction between Islamic religious life and the broader Islamicate social world. The same route could transmit commodities, words, legal practices and devotional instruction, each by different agents and at different speeds. It does not follow that all such transmission can be labelled the same kind of religious conversion. The chronology and supporting evidence remain regional.

Controls. Richard Eaton, The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier; A. C. S. Peacock, Islam and the “Land Below the Winds” and specialist studies of Melaka; maritime-historical scholarship on the Malay trading world. Only selected secondary studies have been checked here, so the critical apparatus should not claim a complete collation of Bengali or Malay inscriptions.

Critical dossier 9

Colonial institutions: the transformation of authority must be specified (chapters 43–45)

The transformation of Islamic legal and educational institutions under colonial and modern states cannot be explained by the word “decline.” Courts, schools, state budgets and codified statutes changed who had the right to settle disputes, how judgments were recorded, how jurists were trained and which bodies could enforce their conclusions. These developments occurred differently across the Ottoman domains, British India, Egypt, French North Africa and later nation-states.

A strong commentary distinguishes at least three relationships. First, a jurist giving an opinion (fatwā) is not identical to a judge issuing an enforceable decision (qaḍāʾ). Second, a large juristic literature containing rival opinions is not the same kind of instrument as a statute that selects a particular rule for officials to administer. Third, the practical scope of a religious obligation for an individual need not be coextensive with the jurisdiction allowed to a state court. These differences explain why an alteration in the state's judicial organization can transform religious authority without ending private study or devotion.

The Ottoman Mecelle (1869–1876) is one case in which selected Ḥanafī civil-law material was arranged into articles for courts. Its emergence should be studied alongside Tanzimat-era administration and the legal constraints of its own time, not treated as a complete codification of all sharīʿah or as an instant replacement of every older court. In British India, colonial courts' selection, translation and enforcement of legal opinions produced different institutional consequences. Deoband's curriculum and Aligarh's educational programme responded to overlapping but different questions: how to sustain religious scholarship and how to train persons for a new state and professional order. Those responses should be explained through their respective institutions rather than placed along an unexamined line from tradition to modernity.

For spiritual life the issue was likewise institutional. An endowed teacher, a mosque preacher, a state employee, a privately appointed shaykh and an author of a printed manual could reach students through different networks. Printing and later broadcasting made texts more accessible to people outside established teacher-student chains, while also altering the ways readers evaluated credentials. Religious learning did not uniformly disappear or thrive: its forms of supervision, funding and audience changed. The same distinction persists in digital communication today.

Limits and later work. This dossier establishes the variables a case study must measure: jurisdiction, training, revenue, procedure, enforcement, linguistic medium and access to instruction. Detailed Ottoman archives, provincial colonial records and specific madrasa syllabi have not been collated as a cross-regional data set here. No single political explanation should be applied uniformly to all societies or periods.
