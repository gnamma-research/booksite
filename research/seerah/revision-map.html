Sīrah and the Making of Islamic Civilization

Explanatory method and revision requirements for all 46 matns

Research audit · 11 November 2026. Chapters 02–46 remain unchanged pending full source-led study. This is an assessment, not a claim of 46 completed revisions.

Working revision brief. The chapter numbering follows Seerah_1_4.epub. All 46 narrative chapters and their commentaries were consulted for this plan. Chapters 2–46 below are revision proposals, not completed rewrites or completed new source investigations. Matn 1 and its sharḥ are rewritten here. Nothing in this document changes the live website.

I. What changes in the whole work

The result should not be the old history with metaphysical observations added to it. Each chapter should explain what good people seek, how a particular form of life makes that good possible, how it can become distorted, and how guidance preserves or restores its proper relations.

The existing edition already connects events with institutions and separates different kinds of evidence. Its limitation is uneven explanatory depth: some chapters explain practical mechanisms well but leave their human and spiritual significance generic. Others name a spiritual meaning without showing the intermediate steps connecting it to an action, institution or historical change. The new work must supply those steps, not replace historical investigation with symbolism. [D1]

The method: source-led explanatory reconstruction

1. Recover the whole episode before choosing its meaning

Start with the complete relevant narrative, not the sentence that appears to support a proposed interpretation. Establish the actors, sequence, circumstances, alternatives, action and consequences. For a doctrine, read the argument and its qualifications; for an institution, examine its operation rather than merely its name. Ask what the reader would fail to understand if this episode or institution disappeared from the book.

This is why Nūḥ cannot mean only opposition to ancestors, Yūsuf only administration, or Sulaymān only kingship. The Ark, dreams, prison, judgment and exercise of authority determine what their stories actually explain.

2. Identify the proper good before diagnosing its corruption

Distinguish a power, its proper object, and its manner of exercise. Continuity can preserve truth; wealth can provision households; government can protect rights; intellectual distinction can serve understanding. Their corruption is not that these goods exist, but that an agent pursues them without the relations and limits that make their pursuit right.

The part–whole principle is therefore a question, not a universal verdict: which part, of which whole, under which relation? A family is not wrong because it is smaller than a state; a state is not right because it encompasses more people. Some differences are complementary, some disagreements legitimate, and some conflicts arise from genuine scarcity rather than a single person's vice.

3. Build the intermediate explanations

Work through a connected sequence:

Principle or ultimate end → human capacity → disposition and intention → action under particular conditions → institutional arrangement → historical consequences.

Then work backwards to test it. A spiritual interpretation of patience must explain the actual waiting, restraint or repeated labour involved. An explanation of taxation must identify assessment, collection, expenditure and the people who bear the cost. Metaphysics and psychology can describe the same reality at different levels, but a resemblance between levels does not establish a historical cause.

4. Compare interpretations without manufacturing agreement

Keep Lings as the central interpretive guide while allowing Qurʾānic context, tafsīr and other primary texts to correct an overextended synthesis. Distinguish the historical subject from a symbol of humanity, a dramatic speaker from the author's settled position, a spiritual station from a prophetic commission, and cosmic function from moral intention.

The Iblīs inquiry supplied a particularly useful test: having a place within providence is not the same as understanding that place. The Queen of Sheba supplied another: incomplete information need not imply bad judgment. Both distinctions must remain available when the preferred framework encounters inconvenient evidence.

5. Specify what changes, and why this form appears here

Every chapter should answer a before-and-after question. What can people now do, know, claim, transmit or enforce that they could not previously do in the same way? Why this arrangement rather than another? What persists through the change?

Do not deduce the precise order of historical events from a metaphysical hierarchy. A sacred centre, a capital, a teaching centre and an imperial bureaucracy need not coincide. Institutional differentiation may preserve an integrated purpose rather than represent decline.

6. Expand first; compress the explanation, not merely the word count

Prepare a research account with passages, competing readings and historical mechanisms. Write the sharḥ from the strongest connected explanation. Only then write the matn, retaining the steps without which the explanation collapses.

The matn should use concrete subjects and active verbs. The sharḥ should explain the acts, not narrate our research process. Footnotes should carry attribution, variants and uncertainty. A difficult dispute belongs in a separate dossier when including it would obscure the chapter's main movement.

7. Test the result against failure, difference and consequences

Ask whether the explanation distinguishes intention from outcome, bodily limitation from moral fault, formal affiliation from realized virtue, and political success from religious legitimacy. Ask whether another interpretation explains the evidence better. Do not diagnose inaccessible inner states, treat victims as expendable illustrations of cosmic harmony, or call every calamity a necessary purification.

Each revised chapter should leave four durable records: its concise matn, substantive sharḥ, source-and-claim ledger, and a brief note on what it prepares the next chapter to explain. The ledger should identify direct evidence, reported interpretation, our inference and unresolved claims.

II. Revision plan for all 46 matns

These are changes to explanatory emphasis and research requirements. The current chronological and regional structure remains the backbone; the later regional histories need explicit indications of overlap rather than a false impression that they occur consecutively.

Book I — The world into which the mission came

01. Prophetic beginnings: guidance addressed to human life

Replace the preliminary catalogue with the governing account of manifestation, human vocation, misdirection, repentance and differentiated prophetic guidance. Distinguish primordial capacity, realized integrity and final prophetic commission. Keep the extended Iblīs debate, alternative readings of the Flood, and the Queen of Sheba in the sharḥ or dossiers. The opening should generate the book's questions without pretending to provide a complete metaphysical history of every prophet.

02. Land, settlement and the political worlds of the Near East

Organize the material around provision and dependence: how water, cultivation, storage, craft and exchange make particular forms of cooperation necessary. Contrast river administration, pastoral mobility and sanctuary-linked settlement without treating one as an inferior stage of another. Show how the same arrangements can sustain life or concentrate extraction. The bridge to prophecy is the moral significance of actual dependencies—not a claim that geography produces revelation.

03. Israel and Judah: kingship, destruction and life beyond a kingdom

Make the central problem the distinction between a covenant and its political supports. Examine what happens when king, territory or sanctuary can no longer carry communal continuity in the same way, and how teaching, household practice and calendar sustain it. Do not turn exile into a generic allegory of spiritual fall. Preserve the difference between sacred remembrance, independently dated events and uncertain routes into Arabia.

04. Religious disciplines and the changing world of the first millennium BCE

Replace a panoramic catalogue of traditions with a controlled comparison of how communities preserve and communicate wisdom: ritual, ethical training, argument, contemplative discipline and law. Use Guénon's account of increasing differentiation as an interpretive question, not a proven global timetable. Investigate actual contacts separately. The chapter should establish why different forms of instruction arise, without making every tradition a fragment awaiting correction by ours.

05. Jesus and the communities that carried his message

Give the chapter two connected movements: the distinctive Qurʾānic significance of Word, Spirit and life-giving signs; then the historical work of communities transmitting and interpreting the message. Ask how embodied signs, texts, worship and ecclesiastical authority relate. Do not assume that all Judaism was empty externalism or that all Christian development was one uniform distortion. Compare actual claims, communities and stages of doctrinal formulation.

06. The sixth-century crossroads of Rome, Persia, Ethiopia and Arabia

Turn geography into a comparison of the settings in which religion, authority and livelihood meet. Contrast imperial administration, dependent frontier rulers, Red Sea connections and the sanctuary-centred Ḥijāz. Explain what each arrangement enables and constrains. Preserve the distinction between the conditions of the mission's first reception and those of later conquest: imperial warfare helps explain opportunities and pressures, not the divine origin of revelation.

Baseline: Edition 1.4, chapters 1–6. [D1]

Book II — The formation of believers in Makkah

07. The sanctuary, the clans and the obligations of protection

Centre the chapter on the difference between serving a sacred trust and possessing its privileges. Kinship and protection are real goods; show when they protect vulnerable people and when they obstruct a truth that judges inherited status. Make the sanctuary's religious purpose primary without neglecting livelihood. Keep Abū Ṭālib, Abū Lahab and Abū Jahl distinct, and do not infer hidden conviction or psychological inevitability from outward opposition alone.

08. Ḥirāʾ, Khadījah and the beginnings of a teaching community

Explain revelation's reception through the conjunction of divine address, embodied apprehension, established character and trusted companionship. Khadījah's household is integral, not incidental: it connects extraordinary experience with sustained recognition and support. Distinguish revelation from an experience supposedly manufactured by retreat. Let Waraqah's response connect the event with prophetic history, while the first practices show how a word becomes a shared life.

09. The Meccan proclamation: worship, reckoning and new loyalties

Trace how resurrection and divine judgment alter the significance of wealth, ancestry, reputation and immediate advantage. Show the practical formation involved in recitation, giving, restraint and care for vulnerable people. Differentiate the goods being corrected from their distortions. This becomes the first sustained historical demonstration of the Adamic problem: finite things can serve an eternal end, but cannot replace it.

10. Aksum: faith before political safety

Use asylum to distinguish spiritual fidelity, political jurisdiction and justice. The believers' dependence upon a Christian ruler is essential to the explanation: preserving a religious community does not always require that community to possess sovereignty. Examine the transmitted account of recitation and recognition alongside the practical refusal of extradition. Do not reduce the episode to either diplomacy alone or a timeless slogan about coexistence.

11. From loss of protection to an alliance at al-ʿAqabah

Retain bereavement, Ṭāʾif, teaching and the differing pledges, but restore a properly sourced treatment of the Isrāʾ and Miʿrāj, which the existing narrative does not adequately integrate. Its significance is ascent and return to service, not escape from earthly responsibility. Keep disputed dating separate from the main account. Show how spiritual confirmation, instruction and concrete commitments of protection converge without inventing a causal chain between every episode.

Baseline: Edition 1.4, chapters 7–11. [D2]

Book III — Madinah: a fellowship becomes a public community

12. The Hijrah: neighbours, newcomers and a shared order

Make the transformation one of coordinating obligations without abolishing legitimate differences. Connect intention and solidarity with food, housing, compensation, ransom and defence. Explain what the wider association changes and what kin groups retain. Religious fellowship, political protection and common jurisdiction must remain distinguishable. The Medinan agreements can then illustrate an integrated order without becoming a modern state constitution or a claim that all differences disappeared.

13. Worship takes shape in the routines of a town

Explain how prayer, fasting, almsgiving and orientation shape attention, bodily practice, time and the use of possessions. Preserve their revealed and spiritual purposes rather than reducing them to psychological techniques. Join these practices to concrete household and commercial cases: the same person worships, contracts, inherits and becomes angry. Show why law, personal virtue and adjudication cooperate without one making the others unnecessary.

14. Badr: conflict tests allegiance and public judgment

Let the Qurʾānic treatment of agency, fear, assistance, captives and spoils govern the explanation. Victory must not become proof of independent human strength or unlimited entitlement. Show how common action produces new claims requiring judgment. Keep spiritual dependence and practical causation together: neither military planning nor divine assistance authorizes the omission of the other explanatory level. Test the consequences of success as carefully as the causes of battle.

15. Uḥud and the Trench: discipline, siege and divided loyalties

Separate the tests: an apparent immediate gain at Uḥud; sustained cooperation and uncertainty during the siege; and the distinct problems of wartime agreement and judgment. Connect each with its actual tactical and material conditions. Do not diagnose every error as the same spiritual defect. Preserve rigorous treatment of the Banū Qurayẓah reports and their victims; a symbolic reading must neither erase contested evidence nor turn suffering into decorative moral instruction.

16. Al-Ḥudaybiyyah: an agreement that changed the terms of conflict

Make this a central case of limited expectation corrected through a larger unfolding. Distinguish the promised goal from an assumed timetable, and genuine compassion for the persecuted from complete knowledge of a decision's consequences. Connect treaty obligations, the immediate distress surrounding Abū Jandal, and Umm Salamah's counsel with embodied action. Do not equate the Companions' questions with Iblīs's refusal or infer that every humiliating compromise is secretly wise.

17. Khaybar, the deferred pilgrimage and relations beyond Madinah

Explain the difference between acquiring authority and sustaining the life under that authority. Cultivation needs existing expertise, seasonal labour and enforceable shares; victory cannot replace those requirements. Treat the deferred pilgrimage separately as the fulfilment of a bounded undertaking. The chapter's unity lies in reliable obligations across religious and political difference—not in making agriculture and pilgrimage instances of one vague policy of accommodation.

18. The return to Makkah and the incorporation of Arabia

Present the return as restoration of a centre's purpose, not simply possession of its site. Then use Ḥunayn, distribution and delegated teaching to examine the dangers and burdens of enlarged power. Distinguish outward allegiance from instruction and inward formation. The contrast between a sacred trust and its custodians' self-importance should recur through actual choices rather than a claim that every opponent's motives were identical.

19. The Farewell Pilgrimage and responsibilities after the Prophet

Make this the structural hinge of the book. Show the integrated prophetic example in public rites, household transmission and obligations toward others; then distinguish the functions that must continue after prophecy ends. Government, textual preservation, legal judgment and spiritual formation do not pass to successors in identical ways. Treat the differences among sermon reports carefully. Finality should generate the following history's problem, not merely close the biography.

Baseline: Edition 1.4, chapters 12–19. [D3]

Book IV — Succession, conquest and imperial government

20. After the Prophet: allegiance and the survival of obligations

Rebuild around the persistence of obligation after the founding person's death. Distinguish political coordination, religious interpretation and claims of designated authority; represent the Sunni and Shiʿi accounts accurately without collapsing them. Treat zakāt, local allegiance and rival prophetic claims as related but different issues. The chief explanatory question is which aspects of the prophetic trust each new arrangement can preserve—and which it cannot simply inherit.

21. Conquest and the problems inherited from older empires

Distinguish military possession, legitimate administration, conversion and cultural transformation. Follow one concrete chain from territory to crops, taxes, soldiers, cultivators and public responsibility. Ask how an inherited administrative technique can serve a new normative end without becoming sacred merely because Muslim rulers use it. Regional evidence must constrain the explanation. Expansion supplies responsibilities and opportunities, not automatic proof of spiritual realization.

22. The garrison, the treasury and the provincial community

Organize the chapter around rival claims upon a common resource: prior service, present need, military obligation, clientage and the rights of those who produce revenue. Examine how registers turn judgments into durable procedures. The wider community must not become a slogan concealing particular privilege. Equally, differentiated functions are not inherently unjust. Show precisely where a practical classification serves its purpose and where it becomes an entitlement insulated from correction.

23. Civil war and the limits of loyalty

Explain conflict through competing obligations, unequal information, organized forces and disputed procedures—not generic spiritual blindness. Distinguish the participants' aims from later theological classifications and the outcomes they could not fully foresee. A Sunni account can preserve respect without pretending every decision was equally right. The central problem is how a community judges among claims when its members share sacred norms but dispute their application and legitimate enforcement.

24. Damascus, Karbalāʾ and imperial administration

Keep Karbalāʾ's moral gravity distinct from administrative consolidation: successful government cannot compensate for injustice in a moral balance sheet. Then examine how coinage, inscriptions, buildings and official language represent common authority. Ask when public religious signs communicate an obligation and when rulers use them to identify religion with their own power. Distinguish the dynasty's claims, scholarly authority and the lived commitments of its subjects.

25. The Abbasid revolution and a change of ruling centre

Study the movement from a claim of restoration to the construction of another dynasty. Trace how different supporters understood the appeal to the Prophet's family, which expectations the settlement fulfilled, and which remained contested. Explain the move toward Iraq through actual resources and networks. The lesson should concern the difficult translation of a normative claim into institutions, not the cynical assertion that every appeal to religion merely conceals ambition.

Baseline: Edition 1.4, chapters 20–25. [D4]

Book V — Knowledge and spiritual formation acquire enduring institutions

26. Baghdad and the work of a learned city

Treat specialization as a potentially constructive differentiation of knowledge. Examine how translators, physicians, jurists, administrators and patrons depend upon one another without sharing one method or authority. Follow a defined project rather than repeat the House of Wisdom legend. Set the miḥnah within the distinction between intellectual argument and coerced assent. The question becomes how a city coordinates different competences without letting court power or one discipline claim the whole.

27. Travelers, households and authenticated transmission

Explain transmission as a relation among wording, witness, context, understanding and authorization. A chain, book or credential is a means of accountability, not a substitute for it. Use an actual variant or correction to show how the discipline works, including the distinctive access of household transmitters and women teachers. The deeper theme is making knowledge portable while preserving the relationships that keep it trustworthy.

28. Legal schools and recurrent cases

Replace founder-by-founder description with a worked case in which evidence, classification, analogy and circumstance produce reasoned differences. Show how a school preserves a method of judgment rather than every founder's answer unchanged. Legitimate disagreement is not automatically an instance of sinful partiality. The school's proper good is disciplined continuity; its possible distortion is treating inherited formulations as sufficient without understanding their grounds and limits.

29. Endowments, colleges and the social life of scholarship

Explain how an intention becomes durable through property, stipulations, offices and teaching relationships. Distinguish financing study, authorizing transmission, demonstrating competence and forming character. An endowed building can support all four but guarantees none of them. Use an actual endowment and teaching setting to examine both independence and patronage. This chapter should show when institutional form carries a living purpose and when prestige begins to replace it.

30. Translation, theological controversy and the uses of reason

Reconstruct a few real arguments, including their strongest objections, rather than arrange traditions along a predetermined scale of spiritual adequacy. Distinguish the objects and limits of demonstration, dialectic, transmitted report and spiritual interpretation. A disagreement may arise from different premises or meanings, not bad character. The part–whole question concerns the unjustified extension of a method beyond its competence; it must never become an excuse to dismiss rational criticism.

31. Al-Ghazālī, al-Rāzī and philosophical theology

Show what disciplined criticism changes in an inherited argument and in the education of its reader. Follow a lemma, objection and response through a limited controversy; distinguish that work from al-Ghazālī's practical formation of character. Neither technical mastery nor sincerity alone guarantees a sound argument. Commentary can preserve living inquiry or become competitive display: determine which through actual texts and practices, not a blanket verdict on later scholasticism.

32. The work of the heart becomes a teachable discipline

Make this the fullest psychological application of Matn 1. Distinguish knowledge, attention, intention, action, repetition and stable disposition. Explain how a genuine aspiration becomes resistant to correction when the person identifies the distortion as a virtue. Use al-Muḥāsibī, al-Qushayrī and al-Ghazālī in their own terms. Preserve the difference between moral fault, bodily limitation and illness; spiritual diagnosis must not become indiscriminate condemnation of human vulnerability.

33. The teacher's company and enduring Sufi networks

Separate companionship, authorization, corporate succession, spiritual realization and metaphysical doctrine. Examine how a lineage transmits practices and corrects misunderstanding without making genealogy itself proof of attainment. Centralize Lings for the interpretive account of realization, while dating the actual institutional forms independently. Place the extended Akbarian discussion here as a later articulation of prophetic inheritance—not as vocabulary secretly assumed by every seventh-century believer.

Baseline: Edition 1.4, chapters 26–33. [D5]

Book VI — An international civilization with many centres

34. Cairo, Damascus and Jerusalem

Organize the chapter around differentiated centres of worship, learning, government and military action. No city needs to be the exclusive centre of every function. Follow one campaign and one institution to show their interaction; include the people displaced or protected. The Crusades must not erase intra-Muslim disputes or the diversity of Christian communities. Shared religious orientation can sustain exchange across political division without abolishing either division or conflict.

35. Al-Andalus and the Maghrib

Distinguish the life of a political order from the transmission of knowledge, law, language and worship. Follow what survives displacement and what cannot survive without its former material support. Neither romantic coexistence nor a simple punishment narrative explains the whole. Use the movement of specific scholars, books and families to show how a centre can cease politically while parts of its inheritance continue elsewhere—without treating the losses as spiritually costless.

36. Persianate language, commerce and books

Explain language as a medium that both discloses meaning and requires trained interpretation. Compare technical prose, ethical narrative and poetry rather than treating them as interchangeable containers. Trace an actual work across teachers, scripts and regions. A universal religious end need not demand cultural uniformity, but common imagery alone does not prove identical doctrine. The chapter should show how translation expands participation while creating new responsibilities of explanation.

37. Mongol conquest, conversion and scholarly survival

Hold devastation, adaptation and renewal together without turning catastrophe into a necessary instrument of progress. Identify the people, manuscripts, resources and organizations that make survival possible. Distinguish a ruler's conversion from the formation of a population. The governing question is how inherited capabilities move into a transformed political setting, not whether conquest automatically destroys—or automatically revitalizes—a civilization.

38. The Middle Niger

Treat West African scholars and communities as makers of an intellectual world, not peripheral recipients of an eastern whole. Use a named author, scholarly household or dispute to examine commercial support, royal claims and learned independence. Distinguish imported books from locally produced understanding. The relevant totality is a shared religious orientation realized through particular societies; neither distance from Arabia nor state patronage determines the depth of that realization.

39. The monsoon seas and the Malay world

Make seasonal movement, residence, marriage, language and commercial trust the concrete means through which communities endure. Distinguish a merchant's transactions from religious teaching and a ruler's affiliation from popular conviction. Follow how temporary exchange becomes sustained social life. Show why common norms can travel while their local applications differ, and test each proposed connection through dated texts and institutions rather than treating trade itself as an explanation of conversion.

40. Bengal

Trace a bounded interaction among settlement, cultivation, landholding, worship and saintly memory. Labour transforms an environment and creates relationships within which guidance can be taught; it does not mechanically produce faith. Distinguish a documented grant, an observed practice and a later sacred biography. The chapter's explanatory strength should come from relating these levels without making either agricultural change or spiritual charisma the sole cause of everything else.

41. The Ottoman settlement

Use an actual case to distinguish imperial regulation, juristic judgment, educational qualification and local practice. Ask what central organization makes more reliable and what it risks subordinating to official advancement. Courts, colleges, endowments and spiritual networks can cooperate without possessing identical authority. The empire's scale should deepen the question of coordinated responsibility, not serve as proof that one political structure fully embodies the Muhammadan inheritance.

42. Safavid Iran and Mughal India

Make this a controlled comparison of different relations among dynasty, confession, jurists, spiritual authority and diverse subjects. Do not assign an entire empire one psychological character. Compare institutional purposes with actual reception and consequences, including coercion and resistance. Common metaphysical questions may recur, but different political arrangements and theological commitments require different explanations. Treat intellectual production as the work of identifiable scholars, not an automatic expression of state ideology.

Baseline: Edition 1.4, chapters 34–42. [D6]

Book VII — Modern changes in the conditions of religious life

43. Oceans, finance and the balance of power

Separate an increase in technical power from the ends directing its use. Reconstruct the specific links among shipping, credit, production, taxation and warfare; then assess their effects on learned and religious life. Guénon's critique of quantitative civilization can frame an evaluative question, but cannot replace those mechanisms. Avoid both effortless progress and undifferentiated decline: ask which capacities expand, who controls them, and which relationships they reorganize.

44. Colonial courts and Islamic law

Show how a doctrine can retain its wording while changing its function inside a different structure of authority. Follow one class of cases through juristic teaching, translation, court procedure and appeal. This is a particularly strong instance of a part detached from its former whole. Yet specify the losses and continuities rather than assume every change had one effect. The chapter should make institutional relocation intelligible, not merely announce secularization.

45. Renewal through teaching, reform and print

Compare projects by the capabilities they cultivate: reading, judgment, professional competence, worship, character and public service. Preservation of a text, expansion of access and formation of understanding are distinct achievements. Examine teachers and institutions rather than reduce the history to two ideological camps. Print can preserve and widen a tradition while also circulating fragments without their interpretive disciplines; the question is how communities respond to both possibilities.

46. Nation-states, migration and the new scale of Muslim life

End with the problem of coordinating distributed sources of authority and learning. Family, mosque, school, state and digital media supply different things; none automatically supplies the whole. Distinguish access from understanding, reach from authority and intensity from formation. Give the reader a practical method for locating a claim, examining its context, seeking correction and judging its consequences. Close with the continuing work of realization, not nostalgia or a claim that history has reached one uniform condition.

Baseline: Edition 1.4, chapters 43–46. [D7]

What all 46 revisions amount to

The book's main movement becomes vocation → prophetic formation → differentiated inheritance → transmission across societies → renewed problems of realization.

Book I establishes what human life is for and the different conditions in which guidance operates. Books II–III show its integration in the Prophet's life and community. Book IV asks how public responsibility continues without renewed prophecy. Book V explains the differentiated disciplines and institutions that preserve, interpret and realize guidance. Book VI tests that inheritance in genuinely different societies. Book VII examines what changes when the conditions of authority, attention, livelihood and transmission change.

The recurring subject is neither an empire nor an abstract opposition between tradition and modernity. It is the relation between truth, the persons capable of receiving it, the practices through which they live it, and the institutions that preserve or distort those practices. The whole is not identical with the state, and integration does not require one institution to monopolize every function.

This also changes the transitions. Each chapter must leave a specific problem for its successor. The later regional chapters need parallel timelines and explicit comparisons because their histories overlap. None needs a ritual repetition of “part and whole.” The principle earns its place only when it explains the concrete difference.
