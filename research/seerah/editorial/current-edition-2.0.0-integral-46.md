# Sīrah and the Making of Islamic Civilization

## Book I — The world into which the mission came

### 01. Prophetic beginnings: guidance addressed to human life

**Matn**

The source of created life does not acquire perfection by creating. Existence, knowledge, beauty and power are received by beings whose limits make their distinctive capacities intelligible. The divine Names disclose these relations without dividing the divine Source. The human being is made capable of knowing many orders at once: bodily need, particular creatures, intelligible meaning, moral obligation and worship. Ādam is taught the names and entrusted with responsibility on earth. This knowledge is fulfilled when things are understood according to their realities and treated in accordance with what is due to them.

The command to bow before Ādam brings that human vocation into view. Iblīs compares the element from which he was made with the clay of Ādam's body and elevates one feature of the creature into a judgment upon the whole. Fire and clay are real differences; neither can by itself measure the human capacity that receives the names. His refusal replaces obedience to the divine address with an enclosed understanding of his own superiority. Thereafter the same narrowing of vision becomes a danger to human thought: a genuine partial knowledge can become an idol when it claims authority over everything else.

The Garden signifies the harmony of human enjoyment with remembrance of the Giver. Its fruits are experienced as gifts within an order of obedience. Satan approaches Ādam and his spouse through the longing for abiding life. He promises immortality through the forbidden tree and directs a genuine aspiration for permanence toward a created object pursued against God's command. The deception lies in the relation he establishes: a finite means is made to carry the demand for the Eternal. A sign that should awaken remembrance becomes the centre of possessive desire.

Ādam and his spouse eat, recognize their wrongdoing and turn to God. Their prayer and the words God grants them establish the path of return. Iblīs persists in his self-justification and seeks respite for his work of temptation. The two responses disclose distinct possibilities within creaturely freedom: the recognition of dependence and the fixation upon one's own asserted excellence. Repentance restores the servant's right relation to God, including the humility through which knowledge becomes receptive again.

The tempter acts within a providential order greater than his knowledge. His intention is to turn human beings from guidance; God can bring resistance, self-knowledge and repentance from the very encounter. His avidity for human ruin blinds the tempter to mercy God may bring forth through repentance. The servant who resists the temptation exercises discernment; the servant who falls and returns learns his dependence upon divine forgiveness. God's wisdom embraces outcomes that the tempter neither foresees nor intends.

Earthly life gives these spiritual relations material form. God had announced humanity's vocation on earth before the Garden's prohibition. Descent places human beings among work, nourishment, procreation, uncertainty, language, arts and the need to cooperate. Water, land, tools and institutions become occasions for knowledge and responsibility. A sound human order relates created goods to the ends for which they are received. Disorder begins when a partial good claims the place of the whole: knowledge becomes vanity, provision becomes hoarding, rule becomes domination, or affection becomes an excuse for injustice. Worship, repentance and disciplined action restore the proper government of the faculties and open the person toward higher realization.

The prophets renew this orientation among peoples living under different conditions. Nūḥ calls his people to the worship of God and perseveres through rejection. He builds the Ark at God's command; at the flood his son refuses the call to board, and Nūḥ receives divine instruction concerning the family promised deliverance. Hūd addresses the people of ʿĀd, whose strength has become a ground for arrogance. Ṣāliḥ calls Thamūd to worship and presents the she-camel as a sign that requires them to honour her allotted access to water. Lūṭ strives to protect his guests before God delivers him. Shuʿayb calls people to worship God and to observe truthfulness in measure, exchange and the exercise of property rights.

Ibrāhīm turns from inherited idolatry toward intimate fidelity to God. His trials bring that fidelity into family, leadership and the covenant. With Ismāʿīl he raises the foundations of the House, praying for worship, provision and a messenger who will recite, teach and purify. Yūsuf receives the gift of interpreting signs; his understanding of dreams and the passage of years becomes practical provision during famine and culminates in the reunion of his family. Mūsā is formed through danger, exile and divine intimacy before confronting Pharaoh. His people must then acquire the habits of covenantal life through worship, law and responsible freedom. Dāwūd unites praise, craft and judgment; Sulaymān exercises wide dominion in gratitude to its Giver. Maryam's trust embraces both extraordinary provision and ordinary bodily effort, while ʿĪsā's signs of life manifest divine power through his created servanthood.

The final Messenger receives guidance for the fullness of the human condition. In his life worship, remembrance, family affection, public justice, counsel, patience, warfare, reconciliation and teaching are related within one actual conduct. The Muhammadan Reality is the name later metaphysical teaching gives to the comprehensive principle of this prophetic gathering; the Messenger himself is God's servant, receiving revelation and acting among real people. The life to be narrated is therefore never only an abstract pattern. Its unity appears through the accurate discernment and fulfilment of different obligations in different encounters.

The completed prophetic mission is inherited through distinct means. Companions remember speech and action; households transmit habits; jurists learn to apply normative guidance; judges determine public rights; teachers train understanding and character; spiritual guides cultivate attention and remembrance. Their responsibilities are real and partial. They can be coordinated in a living order because the human ends toward which they work are related, while each must still answer to the evidence, skill and rightful authority proper to its work.

**Sharḥ**

The plenitude of reality and the comprehensive human.

The metaphysical account begins from the dependence of manifestation upon its uncreated Source. The Infinite, as Guénon and Schuon employ the term, includes possibilities whose nature admits manifestation and possibilities remaining beyond it. Creatures reveal finite perfections through the divine Names while God remains perfect in Himself. This is an explicitly metaphysical reading of the cosmos. Its terms require careful comparison with the different Akbarian levels of determination; the divine Essence, first determination, universal Spirit and historical human being are understood under distinct considerations. Guénon's *The Multiple States of the Being*, chapter XVII, and Schuon's *Survey of Metaphysics and Esoterism*, opening metaphysical summary, supply the Traditionalist formulation.

The comprehensive human is not simply a person who possesses an unusually large collection of virtues. In the Akbarian reading, the heart can receive meanings disclosed through distinct faculties without confusing their objects or allowing one faculty to usurp the whole. This is the practical implication of the Adamic teaching of the names. Sense attends to a creature's conditions, intellect considers what it is, conscience recognizes its claims, and worship relates all three to the divine Source. The “two eyes” therefore name an ordered double attention: to the Real as the ground of dependence and to creation in its own determinate relations. The later account of the Muhammadan Reality radicalizes this pattern as a comprehensiveness of station and guidance. It does not convert historical judgments into emanations that can be deduced without evidence. Water must still be found, treaties negotiated, witnesses heard, and children fed. The prophetic unity is manifested precisely through these distinctions, not by their disappearance.

The Qurʾān narrates God teaching Ādam the names (2:30–33). The Akbarian Adamic *Fuṣūṣ* understands the comprehensive human as a mirror in which otherwise distinct names and realities receive an ordered manifestation. Al-Bayḍāwī's explanation of 2:31 includes the knowledge of things and their properties, while al-Qushayrī treats the event through reception of divine teaching and the distinction between the names of creatures and the Names of the Real. The human body receives forms through sense, imagination mediates images and particulars, intellect understands relations and the heart can become the centre of their spiritual integration. Naming has a practical consequence: the human being can discern what a creature is, what it can do, what it needs and what is due to it.

The office of *khilāfa* places this received knowledge under responsibility. A judge must learn the particulars of the disputants, a cultivator must understand the earth and a ruler must recognize those whose labour makes rule possible. These arts unfold through their own instruction, experience and evidence. Their proper coordination depends upon a soul that recognizes the claims of each in relation to the good they serve. The comprehensive human is thus a living unity of faculties and virtues, and the moral question concerns how the whole acts through each of its powers.

Iblīs and the restriction of vision.

The Qurʾān records Iblīs's refusal of the command to prostrate and his appeal to fire and clay (7:11–18; 15:28–43; 38:71–85). He identifies a material property of each created being, judges one superior, and uses that judgment to resist an explicit divine command. The deeper issue concerns the reach of his knowing. A particular comparison becomes his criterion for recognizing the value of an entire human person. He sees the clay and passes judgment upon the whole of Ādam.

Mukhtar Hussain Ali's study of Dāwūd al-Qayṣarī's *Muqaddimah* (the discussion around printed pp. 47–49 of the supplied research PDF) relates this restriction to *al-wahm al-kullī*, universal estimative imagination. Within that exposition, Iblīs apprehends the outward particular and fails to comprehend the spiritual universality disclosed in the human form. The human heart stands at the meeting of spiritual and sensible knowledge; it receives meanings and acts among particulars. This is a technical Akbarian interpretation of the Qurʾānic episode, mediated here through a modern scholarly presentation of al-Qayṣarī. It gives the temptation a theory of restricted cognition. Imagination itself possesses legitimate functions: it makes particular forms present to human knowing. Its distortion occurs when an isolated presentation of reality becomes the sovereign measure of all other knowledge.

The decisive movement from limitation to rebellion is the decision to defend the limited view against correction. Ādam's spiritual vocation comes to him through divine gift. Iblīs holds to a ranking he has produced himself. The resulting blindness is cognitive and moral together: the faculty of comparison remains active, while the will chooses to protect the conclusion from the truth that addresses it. The same pattern can return in an individual, a school or a ruling class whenever professional expertise, ancestry, power or one spiritual experience is granted the authority to define the whole human good.

The Garden, the longing for permanence and the Fall.

Qurʾān 20:120 records Satan's promise of the tree of immortality and a dominion that will never decay. The object of desire is permanence; the recommended means is a created thing pursued through disobedience. The original symbolic explanation, developed especially through Martin Lings's *The Book of Certainty*, chapters III–VI, understands the Garden as primordial harmony in which created goods direct the human being to their Source. A fruit is both nourishment and sign. Under temptation the sign attracts possession as though it could secure in itself what the soul seeks from the Eternal.

The metaphysical meaning concerns the reorientation of human attention. The creature is still a real good, capable of nourishment and beauty. Desire becomes disordered when it seizes upon that finite good as self-sufficient. Schuon's discussion of the Fall interprets this movement through the growing exteriorization of human consciousness, in which existence becomes experienced primarily through its separated appearances. Lings also differentiates inward spiritual vision from the inherited harmony of outwardly correct habits. These descriptions concern the symbolism of humanity's states; the Qurʾānic account of Ādam's individual life remains the governing narrative witness.

God had already announced a *khalīfa* on earth (2:30). Adamic descent therefore places an announced vocation under the concrete conditions of earthly existence. The Qurʾān preserves the prohibition, the lapse, the admission of wrongdoing and God's granting the words of repentance (2:37; 7:23; 20:122–123). The spiritual significance of the event comes into focus through the relation of gift, human freedom and received mercy. The right aspiration to abiding life acquires its true direction through obedience and remembrance. Repentance renews receptivity and allows the servant to learn again from God.

The Iblīs of spiritual literature: devotion, self-image and providence.

Al-Ḥallāj's *Ṭawāsīn*, particularly the *Ṭā-Sīn al-azal wa-l-iltibās*, dramatizes Iblīs in the language of exclusive attachment and refusal. Aḥmad al-Ghazālī's *Sawāniḥ*, chapter 66, develops another literary and spiritual perspective. The texts raise an exact question: a lover may relinquish a reward yet remain attached to the image of being the uniquely faithful lover. That attachment can turn correction itself into an offence against selfhood. Their portrayals belong to later spiritual interpretation, with differences of emphasis and attribution that must be respected. The Qurʾān's description of Iblīs's disobedience remains the theological measure for the act.

The obstacle is the will to preserve a spiritual self-image. A person may speak ardently about love while allowing a preferred mode of relationship to determine which divine command he will acknowledge. The Lord's address calls the servant beyond this self-chosen station. In the highest form of servanthood, recognition of the divine command releases the will from captivity to its own story about virtue. Ādam's repentance and Iblīs's continued self-justification make the difference intelligible.

Ibn ʿArabī's *Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*, Dār Ṣādir I:362, develops an additional dimension through divine *makr*. The tempter seeks to draw a person into error; the believer may resist and receive the reward of resistance, or recognize a fault and turn sincerely to God. Iblīs's avidity for human ruin veils him from the mercy that God may bring forth through those encounters. The passage is accessible at [*Futūḥāt*, I:362](https://www.futmak.com/english/maymaniya/page.php?id=362). Its central point concerns divine wisdom encompassing an adversarial intention whose final effects remain outside the adversary's comprehension.

ʿAyn al-Quḍāt's *Tamhīdāt*, second *tamhīd*, offers a different poetic image in which concealment and disclosure belong to one field of manifestation. Guénon's symbolism of opposition and complementarity helps distinguish opposed directions within a conditioned order. Their images develop a metaphysical question about the place of concealment in disclosure. The actual orientation of the will remains decisive: Iblīs wills temptation, and the servant's responsibility is to respond faithfully to God. Divine providence encompasses this conflict and can bring spiritual good from it through guidance, resistance and repentance.

From the soul to earthly *ʿumrān*.

Human beings acquire knowledge and stable dispositions through engagement with the world. Hunger brings the need for food; agriculture requires the study of soil and seasons; exchange requires trustworthy measure; marriage, parenthood, worship and judgment require attention to the concrete person. A faculty that seizes upon a particular good as sovereign distorts these relationships. A heart open to the higher end orders them through discernment, and repeated right action can cultivate *malakāt*, durable capacities for acting well.

The same structure illuminates the later history of institutions. A school has the real good of teaching; its teachers must possess knowledge and the patience to form learners. A court has the real good of judgment; its procedure must seek truth and protect rightful claims. A state has the real good of public coordination; its offices must serve the people and purposes for which authority is entrusted. These historical forms arise through actual agreements, labour, technologies, resource constraints, coercion and learning. Their healthy functioning expresses rightly ordered human capacities; their corruption is the enlargement of a partial good into a claim over the whole.

The Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ gives the comprehensive human vocation its final historically revealed exemplar. In al-Jāmī's commentary on the Muhammadan *Fuṣūṣ*, pp. 508–509, Adamic receptivity to the Names is connected with the Muhammadan gathering of differentiated prophetic wisdoms. Qūnawī and Farghānī develop the relations of the comprehensive Name, realized knowledge, revelation, the body and the heart in greater detail. The Muhammadan Reality, under its principial consideration, has priority within the Akbarian order of disclosure; Muḥammad's human biography belongs historically at the end of prophetic succession. The full spiritual station gives a higher unity to distinct excellences as they become operative through the living Prophet.

His final commission joins recitation, purification, teaching and exemplary action (Qurʾān 62:2; 33:21; 68:4). Farghānī's fourth foundation compares the cosmos as a manifest Book, the Qurʾān as comprehensive verbal disclosure and the Prophet's speech, actions and character as their enacted expression. Their correspondence is a later Akbarian hermeneutic, grounded for its author in the unity of realized knowledge and embodied service. It gives the opening episode a forward direction: the Adamic vocation disclosed through the Names is fulfilled historically in the comprehensive Muhammadan exemplar.

The later sciences attend to distinguishable aspects of his life. A report preserves an utterance or action; fiqh determines the relevant rights and obligations; ethical instruction examines the character of the agent; spiritual teaching seeks the realization of knowledge in the heart; public offices sustain recurring responsibilities. These are genuine modes of inheritance because they have true objects and standards. Their integration occurs through actual persons relating knowledge, virtue and action in living circumstances. The later historical chapters will therefore follow the transmission of that integration through relationships and institutions, while giving each historical event its own explanatory causes.

Nūḥ: worship, perseverance and fidelity beyond ancestry.

Nūḥ summons his people to worship God, seek forgiveness and turn away from their wrongdoing (71:1–10). He persists through open and private appeal. When leaders mock the standing of those who follow him, he refuses to cast the believers away (11:27–31; 26:111–115). This response protects the integrity of the prophetic summons: a person is answerable to the divine truth he has heard, and the social position assigned by others cannot decide his worth.

God commands Nūḥ to build the Ark before the waters rise (11:37–40). The prophet undertakes the work with materials and skill according to revelation, while his opponents mock him. The craft becomes an appointed means of preservation. Later Sufi interpretation can read the Ark through the relationship of outward form and inward meaning, including the conjunction of revealed law and spiritual realization. The Qurʾānic event itself concerns worship, perseverance, building, judgment and the rescue of those who respond.

At the flood Nūḥ calls to his son to board the Ark; the son trusts a mountain to save him and is drowned (11:42–43). Nūḥ appeals to God concerning his son and the divine promise. God's reply specifies the scope of the family promised rescue; Nūḥ seeks refuge from asking about what he does not know and turns to God's mercy (11:45–47). The event places faithfulness to God above the claims of ancestry while allowing the father's concern to be narrated as the Qurʾān presents it.

Paternal affection is real, and the prophet brings his concern to God in prayer. The correction teaches the servant to submit his knowledge and expectations to revelation. Later Akbarian treatments of the drowned people's condition raise distinct interpretive questions whose disagreements are retained in the research apparatus. The Qurʾānic sequence continues to govern the historical narration.

Hūd and Shuʿayb: strength, worship and exchange.

Hūd calls the people of ʿĀd to worship God and repent. He confronts their confidence in physical strength, monumental achievement and coercive power (26:123–140). The exegetes discuss *maṣāniʿ* as substantial structures, fortifications or waterworks. These works have genuine practical uses; the rebuke addresses the arrogance that makes them symbols of imagined permanence.

Shuʿayb’s opponents ask why his prayer should govern their inherited worship and use of property. Their objection separates devotion from the obligations of exchange. His reply joins them: reverence for the divine command requires care for those whom commercial acts affect. He also refuses to reserve for himself the practices he prohibits to others.

Reform here requires integrity of action as well as an accurate rule. Neither prophetic authority nor private piety grants an exemption from the standard proclaimed. Lawful provision remains good; its sufficiency challenges the claim that every profitable means is therefore legitimate. Dependence on divine enabling also limits the reformer’s self-claim: he can strive and teach, but cannot manufacture another person’s willing response.

Ṣāliḥ: the sign, the water share, and collective violence.

Ṣāliḥ's people possess gardens, water, crops and houses carved from mountains (26:146–149). God's gifts of gardens, water, crops and carved dwellings provide Thamūd with means of life. Ṣāliḥ calls the people to worship God and honour the sign entrusted to them, including the camel's appointed turn at water. The she-camel has a specified turn to drink; they have another (26:155). The shared source introduces a real, temporal restraint: their entitlement is not the only entitlement. The sign therefore enters the rhythms of provision rather than remaining an abstract spectacle.

A powerful minority resists this imposed limit. The narrative of nine corrupting groups conspiring against Ṣāliḥ and his household (27:48–50) distinguishes private envy, coordinated violence and the attempt to conceal responsibility under an oath. The corruption is not the fact that people need water, but the conversion of a legitimate resource interest into the destruction of a protected claim. The Qurʾānic verses do not identify the exact archaeological mechanism by which the camel appeared; reports of emergence from a rock belong to the exegetical tradition. The Ṣāliḥic *Fuṣūṣ* interprets the opening of rock through the wisdom of *fatḥ*, the disclosure of an unforeseen possibility, but that metaphysical reading does not establish a physical history not supplied by the Qurʾān.

**Link to the opening:** A comprehensive vocation requires knowledge of claims other than one's own. The sign tests whether a society can limit its exercise of power without regarding that limitation as an intolerable affront. The episode prepares later questions about resource allocation, agreements and the public protection of vulnerable claims.

Lūṭ: hospitality, limited power, and the refuge that exceeds it.

The arrival of guests exposes a community's disorder in the concrete obligations of hospitality and protection (11:77–81). Lūṭ strives to shield those entrusted to him while facing people he cannot repel by his own force. His wish for strength or a protective clan identifies an actual horizontal constraint: righteous intention does not itself supply the means of defence. The commentaries distinguish the ordinary sense of the *rukn shadīd*, a powerful kin group or stronghold, from the Prophetic reminder that God's support is the ultimate refuge.

The Lūṭic *Fuṣūṣ* connects *malakiyya* with force and strength, making the episode a meditation on the limits of creaturely power. But divine protection is not a justification for neglecting the duty to resist harm within one's actual capacity. The guests and kinship status matter precisely because a messenger must act among people through means that may be insufficient. Divine rescue does not retroactively make their threatened assault a legitimate use of force.

**Link to the opening:** The finite cannot become ultimate security. Unlike Iblīs, Lūṭ's distress and wish for support need not become a self-authorizing refusal of the higher command. The episode adds the protection of others to the practical meanings of vicegerency.


Ibrāhīm: intimacy, tested leadership and the House.

Ibrāhīm’s wisdom begins in *khulla*: an intimacy in which attachment to the divine permeates the person rather than remaining one interest among others. Its comprehensiveness is not physical mixture with God. It concerns the depth to which divine knowledge and love govern the servant’s reality. Trial makes that allegiance effective amid relationships and possessions that rightly matter to him.

The tests do not inform an ignorant God. They disclose fidelity in action and qualify the person’s leadership before others. The covenant therefore does not pass automatically through descent; injustice contradicts its demand. The near-sacrifice must not become praise of killing as such: surrender concerns the beloved attachment, while the ransom prevents surrender from being identified simply with the child’s death.

The sanctuary gives this allegiance durable social form. The stated purpose of the settlement is the establishment of prayer; the attraction of hearts and arrival of provisions sustain it. Return to the House, protection, pilgrimage and livelihood become related obligations rather than unrelated advantages. Its custodians cannot convert the goods supporting worship into a property claim against worship’s demands.

The sacred centre directs distinct households and communities toward a common act of worship, even as their languages, circumstances and responsibilities differ. The petition for a future messenger completes the connection between place and formation: recitation, teaching and purification must make the sanctuary’s purpose living in its people. A sacred building alone cannot do their knowing and acting for them.

Ismāʿīl: the covenant becomes household fidelity.

Ibrāhīm's sanctuary cannot secure its end through stone or descent alone. The Qurʾān independently honours Ismāʿīl for truthfulness to his promise and instruction of his household in prayer and *zakāt* (19:54–55). His agency is not reducible to standing beside his father at the House. The shared building becomes a recurring human task: promises bind conduct across time; household teaching habituates bodies and desires; giving prevents possessions from enclosing the family within private interest. Al-Qushayrī connects the promise to sacrificial trial and joins bodily and financial devotion. Ibn ʿĀshūr emphasizes the passage's independent treatment of Ismāʿīl.

The elevation attributed to Ismāʿīl in the *Fuṣūṣ* concerns a metaphysical station and the servant's utter dependence upon the Lord. It does not make the practical promise dispensable. **Link:** the higher meaning of transmitted covenant is embodied by a faithful person, not inherited automatically by biological descent.


Yūsuf: the light of interpretation and the subtle government of events.

Yūsuf’s luminous wisdom concerns the disclosure of meaning through form. An image is not self-interpreting, and the same image need not carry one fixed meaning in every recipient and circumstance. Discernment relates the presented form to the order in which it appears. The imaginal is not therefore arbitrary fantasy, nor does interpretation abolish the distinct realities of dream and waking event.

The narrative also displays divine subtlety: betrayal, displacement and confinement do not disclose the whole course to their participants. The opening vision and its eventual realization give intervening events a relation that cannot be read from immediate advantage alone. This does not vindicate the brothers’ wrong; providence exceeds their intention rather than making their intention righteous.

The approach to office has a public threshold. Yūsuf seeks clarification of the accusation before accepting release, protecting the trust on which later service will depend. Spiritual integrity is not merely private self-certainty. It can require truthful public acknowledgment when an unresolved charge would compromise the entrusted work.

Interpretation then becomes practical counsel. Harvest remains in its ears, consumption leaves reserves, and future scarcity qualifies present abundance. The physical property of stored grain belongs inside the explanation: knowledge must address how food actually survives. Trustworthiness governs the beneficiaries; competence secures the means.

The conclusion returns authority to the household. Yūsuf speaks of release from prison rather than making the brothers’ violence the centre of the reunion. Reconciliation does not deny the wrong; it prevents regained power from making revenge its end. The narrative joins symbolic discernment, moral endurance, public credibility, temporal foresight and forgiving action.

Mūsā: divine formation, intimacy and public commission.

Mūsā’s mission begins before his confrontation with Pharaoh. The infancy narrative shows preservation through what appears most dangerous: water, separation and reception into an enemy’s household. Later flight and life in Midian enter a formation already held within divine care. The commentary reads subtle favour beneath overpowering circumstances; it does not claim that violence becomes righteous because God frustrates its purpose.

The declaration that God has chosen Mūsā for Himself leads directly to his being sent to Pharaoh. Intimacy does not culminate in keeping the messenger away from the world. It equips him to return without making worldly acceptance the source of his authority. His request for assistance and his brother’s participation further distinguish received mission from self-sufficient individual power.

Deliverance then changes the community’s task. Freedom from a ruler does not automatically form truthful judgment, stable worship or ordered desire. Covenant gives freedom a governing end. The tablets’ comprehensive guidance must not be redescribed as a merely external law awaiting a later religion to supply inwardness.

The al-Khiḍr narrative preserves a further distinction between spiritual dignity and the particular knowledge God discloses to a person. A limited presentation need not yield the whole reason for an act. This does not give ordinary agents authority to set revealed obligations aside through unverified claims to inward knowledge. It places judgment within its actual evidentiary and vocational limits.

Ayyūb: dependence, patience, and the right to ask for relief.

Suffering is not self-interpreting. It can result from several causes and does not prove a prior moral fault. The detailed accounts of Ayyūb's losses and bodily condition vary widely; accusations that he deserved punishment through a specific hidden wrongdoing are neither established by the Qurʾān nor required for its lesson. The spiritual commentary prefers the possibility of affliction that raises rank without preceding fault.

Ayyūb's invocation combines acknowledgment of harm with recognition of divine mercy (21:83). His statement does not annul patience: complaint **to** God differs from accusation **against** God. His steadfastness belongs to continuing worship and trust rather than to a refusal to ask. The response includes a striking bodily act: strike the ground with the foot, then wash and drink (38:42). A restorative divine gift arrives through created water and an obedient movement. Healing neither proves that suffering was a moral failure nor that using means marks lesser realization.

The oath incident (38:44) adds the relationship between firmness and lenience. The Qurʾān commands a bundle and speaks of not breaking an oath; the surrounding story involving Ayyūb's wife has several disputed details. Its juridical applications have also been contested. The limited but secure point is that the text joins fidelity to a commanded concession rather than equating seriousness with maximum severity.

The Ayyūbic *Fuṣūṣ* treats his wisdom as *ghaybiyya*: trial, patience, provision and restoration emerge from sources not mastered by the sufferer. Ayyūb's proper response is receptive petition and action, not a theory claiming to explain each misfortune. **Link:** providence and psychology meet without making the devil an independent author of disease; spiritual formation uses rather than erases bodily life.

Yūnus: the limits of prophetic expectation and the mercy of reprieve.

Yūnus's mission reveals a community that responds after a warning, avoiding the fate of destroyed peoples (10:98). The commentaries debate whether repentance occurred after impending signs or after punishment had begun, and compare it to Pharaoh's final response. Those chronologies are not interchangeable. The Qurʾānic result is decisive: God removes the threatened worldly punishment after the community believes. The people's future cannot be read exhaustively from the messenger's expectation of their present refusal.

The messenger's departure and the ordeal of the fish establish another difference. The phrase *lan naqdira ʿalayhi* (21:87) admits readings involving constriction or punishment; it must not be made to assert that a prophet doubted God's omnipotence. Ibn ʿAjība distinguishes the general possibility of withdrawing from hostile people from the specific authorization appropriate to this particular mission. Yūnus recognizes his wrong and calls out from darkness, and God saves him. The spiritual readings describe relief as *tanfīs*: constriction is opened through confession and mercy, without turning the initial impatience into righteousness.

The link to the final Prophet is direct rather than imposed. Qurʾān 68:48 instructs Muḥammad ﷺ to remain patient and not repeat the companion of the fish's haste. The prohibition concerns impatient departure, not the excellent act of calling upon God. The Prophet's reported prohibition on claims of superiority over Yūnus also forbids using the episode to discredit a messenger.

The Yūnus chapter of the *Fuṣūṣ* additionally emphasizes preserving human life and judging acts without treating the created human form as worthless. Its legal analogies are author-specific and do not erase the received law of justice. **Link:** mercy can transform a threatened collective outcome, while the prophet must leave room for divine action not contained in his own immediate judgment.


Dāwūd and Sulaymān: gift, particular understanding and the uses of dominion.

Prophetic kingship gives vicegerency an outward jurisdiction; it does not produce prophecy through political success. The Davidic discussion emphasizes divine bestowal. The gifts establish gratitude and accountability rather than a claim that the recipient earned an independent power.

The damaged-crop judgment makes that accountability concrete. Scripture affirms that God gave both prophets judgment and knowledge while granting Sulaymān a particular understanding. In a widely transmitted explanation, his settlement restores the crop while temporarily compensating its owner through the flock’s benefits, then returns the respective properties. This reported detail illustrates a concern for restoration and both parties’ continuing means; it is not itself the full wording of the Qurʾān.

The different judgment does not reduce Dāwūd to an ignorant or spiritually deficient figure. The exegetical debate concerns particular discernment, prophetic judgment and divine correction. The wisdom already bestowed on both remains explicit.

Their relations to the cosmos also differ. Mountains and birds praise with Dāwūd; wind and other powers serve Sulaymān’s command. Praise, craft and ordered action express different relations of the same received gift. Armour-making joins inspired instruction to bodily protection, so technical work belongs within gratitude rather than beneath prophetic dignity.

The arrival of the Queen’s throne first tests Sulaymān: will extraordinary power produce gratitude or self-attribution? The Queen’s recognition matters, but it should not conceal this prior trial of the king. His extraordinary command increases his obligation to acknowledge the Giver; it does not suspend that obligation.

Zakariyyā and Yaḥyā: continuity without possession of the future.

Zakariyyā asks for a successor despite age and infertility. The Qurʾān does not hide this natural impossibility; it makes it the condition of a received gift rather than a denial of embodiment. Yet he ends by acknowledging God as the best of inheritors (21:89). His desire for continuity therefore does not authorize control of the gift. Interpretations of *yarithunī* (19:6) differ—wealth, knowledge, religious office, prophetic vocation, or combinations—and must not be reduced to a single supposedly obvious meaning.

Yaḥyā receives *ḥukm* in youth, takes hold of the Book with firmness and receives tenderness, purity and kindness to his parents (19:12–15). Commentators disagree about the exact timing and whether *ḥukm* here means early prophethood or wisdom and understanding. Neither reading makes strength and mercy rivals. A received capacity for firm judgment is perfected through gentle conduct and the absence of tyranny.

The linked conclusion of Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ identifies the prophets as people who hasten **in** good works, call upon God through hope and fear, and remain humble (21:90). This is an explicit scriptural counterweight to the one-sided spiritual fixation we studied in Iblīs. The different modes of response do not flatten into a fixed sentiment; they remain available to the command appropriate to the occasion. Neither family continuity nor spiritual fervour is complete unless it becomes receptive action.


Maryam and ʿĪsā: creative Word, means and servanthood.

Maryam supplies the clearest correction to an account that equates spiritual ascent with decreasing use of means. Gift without ordinary effort can disclose God’s agency; mature certainty can then return to effort without mistaking effort for an independent cause. Her movement from received provision to shaking the palm is therefore read as completion, not as a punishment for loving her child.

ʿĪsā’s conception discloses the creative command without an ordinary paternal cause. His signs likewise reveal divine life-giving through a created messenger. The repeated qualification of divine permission maintains the distinction between the instrument and the Source, while the form of the sign makes that Source conspicuous.

The mission does not stop at extraordinary effects. Recognition that God is his Lord and theirs leads to worship; confirmation of the Torah and the alteration of certain prohibitions connect revealed knowledge with the community’s conduct. The inward and outward are not successive competitors but coordinated requirements of response.

His final address concerning his followers further joins compassion to discernment. He does not make himself the owner of their judgment or impose his preferred outcome upon God. The divine Names of might and wisdom close the appeal, retaining the right of judgment even within the possibility of forgiveness. The passage admits different exegetical explanations of its scope; it does not independently establish an unrestricted doctrine of universal pardon.

The fuller relation among the episodes.

No prophet is confined to one excellence; their varying acts disclose different requirements of the one human vocation. Ṣāliḥ puts divine right into an actual water schedule; Lūṭ confronts a duty that exceeds his immediate strength; Ismāʿīl turns covenant into an inherited practice; Ayyūb petitions without disowning patience; Yūnus learns that a community may repent beyond his expectation; Zakariyyā seeks religious continuity without possessing its means; and Yaḥyā joins strength to gentleness.

Together they disclose **right responsiveness under changing conditions**: a person must recognize the actual duty, the relevant means, the limits of his knowledge, the good belonging to others, and God's power to act beyond his expectation. The final Qurʾānic recitation gathers these earlier narratives not only as precedents but as continuing education in judgment and spiritual character.
Muhammadan comprehensiveness as gathered adab.

The prophets share worship of God and differ in their missions, laws, circumstances and characteristic disclosures. The differentiation includes gratitude amid sovereignty, endurance under suffering, fidelity through changing fortune, and renunciation of self-importance. No prophet lacks every excellence not especially prominent in his story. Distinct emphasis is not exclusive possession.

Muhammadan gathering joins these modes of servanthood without cancelling their proper differences. Following the earlier guidance cannot mean simultaneously enacting incompatible practical laws. The commentators disagree about the precise authority of preceding laws, but the contrast between common guidance and differentiated legal provisions remains essential.

The Akbarian account explains the gathering through the comprehensive Name and the relation between Muhammadan speech and the realities deposited in Adamic humanity. It affirms the earlier prophets’ perfection while distinguishing modes of its manifestation. Principial precedence is not an earthly chronology placing the historical Prophet before Ādam.

The final mission’s historical forms give this account determinate content. Recitation presents the signs; instruction teaches revelation and practical wisdom; purification removes the habits that obstruct a truthful response. The address includes people beyond the first recipients. Its continuance involves teachers and practices that communicate inherited guidance, not new claimants to prophetic revelation.

The prophetic stories continue to form their hearers.

The Qurʾān recounts earlier prophets as living instruction for Muḥammad ﷺ and the people who receive his message. Their stories strengthen him in his actual mission and instruct his hearers in endurance, confidence and response. Their narration belongs to the divine act of guidance. The history they relate and the formation they effect are connected but distinguishable functions.

Recitation becomes the centre of an education that also embraces comprehension, practice and transformation. The revealed word accommodates human reception through letters and speech, while its purpose includes understanding and action. Attention, comprehension and transformation therefore remain distinct. One can preserve the wording while failing to let it govern conduct.

The same passage may awaken fear, hope, gratitude, restraint or resolve according to its meaning. Comprehensiveness lies in a responsive heart and rightly ordered action, not in a permanent mood or the repetition of one favoured spiritual vocabulary. Each revealed address demands a response appropriate to its meaning, not conformity to a preferred spiritual mood.

This supplies the bridge to later history. Institutions can preserve texts, teach judgment, sustain worship and form dispositions, but they do not achieve their end merely by surviving. They inherit responsibilities differentiated from the integrated prophetic example. The end remains living guidance: truth understood, conduct corrected, faculties formed, and created goods returned to their proper relations.

### 02. Land, settlement and the political worlds of the Near East

**Matn**

Humanity's work on earth unfolds within a given order of creaturely powers. Rain, soil, plants, animals and the human body have natures that must be learned; harvest demands labour and timing as well as gratitude. A family that saves grain during a good season is already joining care for its dependants to knowledge of the land. When villages share canals or grazing grounds, this same need becomes an enduring question of common rules and just access. Guidance therefore finds one of its earliest historical theatres in the material making of communities.

Along the Nile, the annual flood sustained cultivation whose rhythms joined households, villages and successive political authorities. In southern Mesopotamia, channels of the Tigris–Euphrates system supported fields and settlements requiring continuing maintenance and decisions about water. Each landscape supported several possible arrangements of cooperation, temple and palace administration, royal power and local independence. Local cooperation preceded and persisted alongside the enlargement of temples, palaces and royal administrations. The concentration of harvests supported artisans, soldiers, scribes, worship and public works; it also made possible heavier exactions, compulsory labour and the accumulation of power. The same administrative knowledge that secured a worker's ration could determine what was taken from the farmer.

Writing and accounting made obligations durable beyond personal recollection. Records distinguished quantities, recipients, stores and transfers; contracts and correspondence extended cooperation across distance and time. The truth of the written account and the justice of the obligation it records require distinct judgments. Kingship similarly brought powers of protection, adjudication and coordination that could be exercised for the common good or converted into domination. The growth of civilization therefore enlarged both the possibilities of human service and the consequences of moral disorder.

Other communities lived through mobility rather than continuous settlement. Herding joined access to pasture and water with seasonal movement, while merchants connected places whose inhabitants could not produce all that they required. Caravan journeys depended upon animals, guides, credit, hosts and agreements governing passage. Town and countryside, cultivator and pastoralist, merchant and producer participated through different forms of livelihood and in interdependent economic networks. Their relations could be cooperative, unequal or openly hostile according to circumstances and conduct.

Between Egypt, Mesopotamia and Anatolia, the Levant contained coastal ports, cultivated plains, upland settlements and routes into Arabia. Its rulers negotiated with stronger kingdoms while households continued the recurrent work of farming, herding and exchange. During the second millennium BCE, Egyptian authority extended through networks of dependent city rulers, soldiers and tribute. The disruption of several Late Bronze Age political and commercial systems around 1200 BCE did not destroy every settlement or practice. Older centres survived, others declined, and new political configurations arose, including the Phoenician coastal cities, Aramaean kingdoms and the emerging communities of Israel and Judah. Political forms changed unevenly while the obligations of provision, protection and judgment remained.

Arabia presented further variations. Oases sustained cultivation through the protection and management of scarce water, while movement across arid country required knowledge of routes and reliable arrangements with those controlling places of rest. Settled and mobile lives interacted without one being reducible to the other. The later sanctuary-centred order of Makkah would possess its own history and obligations; its significance cannot be deduced from the political institutions of Egypt or Mesopotamia.

The Near East eventually became a world of kingdoms, empires, displaced peoples and transmitted scriptures. Its languages, trade routes and inherited forms of administration would still shape the societies encountered in the prophetic age. A complete account of human action holds two truths together: all goods are dependent upon God, and every field of created action has its own real conditions that must be known and governed justly.

**Sharḥ**

Provision, embodied action and the purpose of earthly knowledge.

The human body lives through a world received from God and through relationships with other living beings. Rain precedes harvest, fertile soil precedes cultivation, animals and plants possess forms and capacities independent of human choice, and no one person's abilities suffice for every stage between seed and meal. The Qurʾān directs the human being to consider food and then names rain, the splitting of the earth, grain, fruit and pasture (80:24–32). Food becomes available through rain, fertile soil, cultivation, harvest, transport, preparation and the human capacity to employ these created means. Cultivation and cooking require work upon things whose existence and efficacy remain received. Al-Rāzī distinguishes the conditions through which food comes into being from the processes through which it nourishes the body: attention to provision must trace actual intermediate causes rather than halt at the consumed object. 

Provision discloses an important consequence of the Adamic vocation: created causes possess intelligible natures that must be learned and respected. The prayer for sustenance does not remove the need to know soils, seasons, storage and labour. The human capacity to gather meanings therefore becomes an ordered cooperation with the distinct powers of plants, animals, waterways and other persons. Within one household, the desire to feed a child meets the knowledge of cultivation, the obligation to share, and the prudence of preserving grain; a village must add rules of access, maintenance and adjudication. Metaphysical dependence on God is the universal ground of the activity, while particular technologies and institutions are historically acquired means. The difference prevents a theology of providence from becoming a substitute for explaining irrigation, taxation, harvest failure or unequal control of land.

The command to inhabit and cultivate the earth places human work within received responsibilities toward land, its inhabitants and its Lord. In the address of Ṣāliḥ, God has brought human beings forth from the earth and *istaʿmarakum fīhā* (11:61). Al-Ṭabarī explains the latter as causing people to dwell in or inhabit it. The verse joins the created setting of human life with worship and repentance, not with a particular constitution for the state or an unconditional mandate to appropriate whatever land can be seized. Human labour becomes an exercise of knowledge when it takes account of the properties of things, the claims of other people and the dependence of every result upon more than the agent's will. 

Spiritual detachment can mature through the faithful performance of practical work. The farmer who understands how a seed germinates and the builder who knows the strength of a material exercise genuine faculties. Their acts become morally excellent only when the end, the means and the claims arising from their use are rightly related. Need does not make extraction innocent; physical success does not make a purpose legitimate. The Adamic capacity to know a creature's nature is distinct from the practical virtue of responding to that knowledge justly. A society can acquire increasingly sophisticated skills while failing to form the dispositions through which those skills are used well.

Interdependence is neither equality of functions nor a warrant for domination.

Households require many unlike activities: some cultivate, some preserve, carry, grind, cook, record, heal, construct or judge. Specialization can extend the effectiveness of each activity because it permits learning through repetition and cooperation across persons. It also creates asymmetries of information and power. The person who controls a store may determine access to food; the one who assesses a harvest may misrepresent its yield; the person whose labour is indispensable may nevertheless lack the power to negotiate its terms. Each advantage needs an ordering principle beyond the mere fact of being advantageous.

Qurʾān 43:32 speaks of differentiated livelihoods and some people making use of the services of others. The immediate argument rejects the attempt by powerful Meccans to nominate the kind of person upon whom revelation ought to descend. Al-Rāzī observes that the opponents mistook wealth and public eminence for the relevant measure of prophetic excellence. The verse recognizes dependence within worldly life while simultaneously denying that possession of worldly advantage authorizes a claim to divine election. Its description of economic disparity is not a moral endorsement of every hierarchy, contract or regime of labour that happens to exist. 

An administration is therefore answerable to a dual test. It must know enough to preserve the material process for which it is responsible, and it must exercise its knowledge according to the obligations actually owed. A ruler unable to anticipate scarcity may fail through incompetence; a ruler who anticipates scarcity and withholds stores to coerce dependants may fail through injustice. Practical intelligence and right disposition belong together, but one does not substitute for the other. This conjunction will later appear in Yūsuf's claim to be both trustworthy and knowledgeable concerning the storehouses (12:55).

The Nile: the flood's rhythm and the limits of centralization.

Egyptian agriculture depended on the Nile's annual inundation and the recession that left fields ready for sowing. The level and timing of the flood varied; cultivators needed to adjust seed, labour and the protection of stores to the land actually available. Much ancient cultivation used basins that received floodwater and were then drained or allowed to recede. Coordination occurred at the scale of adjacent fields, communities and local districts. It did not require every farmer's irrigation decision to be issued from the royal court. Extensive perennial irrigation, in the later sense of year-round regulated water, must not be projected without qualification into the early pharaonic periods. 

A successful village could maintain embankments and organize work without owning the entire river. Royal authority could nevertheless tax the yield, mobilize workers and command transport over distances exceeding the reach of a single village. These are different kinds of power. A central government might survey fields, supervise levies or maintain strategic works while local groups controlled the immediate distribution of labour and water. The possibility of taxation does not prove central origination of irrigation, and the existence of local cooperation does not eliminate royal coercion. 

Storage separates the time of production from the time of consumption. A granary can sustain workers who are building, travelling or unable to cultivate; it can preserve grain against an uncertain harvest and support people whose crafts serve others. It also creates a point at which access becomes conditional upon an office, a seal, a command or an imposed debt. The farmer bears the first risk of a failed crop while an authority may possess the means to transfer the burden to another district. The actual outcome depends on assessment, collection, transport and distribution. A general statement that the state either 'provided prosperity' or 'extracted surplus' conceals the work by which either result occurs.

The Nile was thus neither an automatic engine of despotism nor a sufficient explanation of Egyptian kingship. Collective discipline, local expertise, courtly religion, military power and established social relations combined in historically changing ways. Since prosperity and extraction could employ the same channels and stores, judging an institution requires knowledge of the persons protected, the burdens imposed and the purpose for which resources were directed.

Mesopotamia: channels, city growth and the changing scale of obligation.

The lower Tigris–Euphrates plain presented another combination of possibilities and constraints. Rivers and distributaries shifted their courses; fields were supplied through natural levee openings, distributary systems and, increasingly, artificial channels. Early arrangements could be maintained by relatively small communities. More developed canal networks involved agreements about excavation, water timing, land access and maintenance. By the third millennium BCE, cities and royal or temple administrations could coordinate works extending beyond the immediate settlement, but the resulting power did not extinguish every smaller authority. 

An upstream diversion illustrates the difference between a technical capacity and a legitimate claim. A channel may improve the crops of one district while reducing the share or raising the maintenance burden of another. Its proper construction requires knowledge of gradients, soils and water flow; its rightful administration requires determining whose fields are served, who performs the work, and who bears the cost. Technical improvement and justice cannot be read off the same fact. Neither can every dispute be attributed to a uniquely evil intention: scarcity can produce competing genuine needs that remain to be judged.

Urban life intensified the effects of decisions made at such junctions. By the late fourth millennium BCE, Uruk had become one of the principal large cities of southern Mesopotamia. Concentrated populations could support scribes, potters, metalworkers and administrators who did not directly cultivate the fields supplying them. The arrangement was not a single indivisible 'temple economy'. Temples and palaces possessed estates and administrative offices, while households, local authorities and private economic actors retained roles that differed by place and century. Surviving institutional archives are disproportionately rich evidence for official activity; they cannot by themselves establish that all production and exchange took place within institutions whose documents survived. 

The city made recurrent obligations both more numerous and more durable. A worker could receive rations rather than harvest grain personally; an official could be accountable for stores he had never grown; a ruler could command resources drawn from people he might never meet. Such distance enlarges the demand for reliable measures and recognized duties. It also increases the opportunities for a powerful office to conceal the persons whose labour makes its decisions effective.

The account: what the earliest writing could—and could not—secure.

A clay tablet from southern Mesopotamia dating to approximately 3300–3100 BCE records the allocation of rations. A further Uruk-period tablet of the late fourth or early third millennium represents an administrative account of products such as barley and malt. These objects preserve signs for quantities, commodities and transfers from a time before fully developed cuneiform became suitable for extended literary composition. The first documents were not primarily abstract statements of law or theology. They grew amid procedures through which goods were received, allocated and remembered. 

A memory held outside an individual gives other persons access to a decision after the decision-maker has departed. A ration list can show which worker is owed food, reveal a discrepancy between receipts and stores or preserve the terms of a transaction across an interval. It enables greater precision of coordination and supplies evidence with which to question an officer. Its use also presupposes persons able to make and interpret the marks. Those who lack such access may find their own entitlement represented by an authority who controls the document.

The distinction between a record and a just account is exact. A scribe may enter the amount ordered rather than the amount fairly due. The storage register may be accurate while the allocation it records is oppressive. Conversely, the ability to reconstruct a lost obligation can protect a weaker claimant against the forgetfulness or denial of a stronger one. Written evidence thus enlarges human power without specifying its moral direction. The record is an instrument of truth when answerable to the reality it represents, not merely because it is written.

Writing later served letters, contracts, school exercises, ritual, law and literary remembrance. Some systems of verbal transmission also preserved matters that an archive did not contain. No simple contrast between 'written administration' and 'oral religion' describes these societies. Priests and scribes belonged to religious institutions; families handed on obligations through practice and speech; and the same document could have legal, economic and sacred implications. Yet a fiscal register anchored to one estate and a teaching deliberately reproduced across households face different conditions of survival. Their distinction becomes especially consequential when a kingdom or temple ceases to function.

Pastoral mobility, households and trading corridors.

Seasonal mobility answers to the spatial distribution of pasture, water and risk. A flock cannot always graze the same ground without depletion; an animal may make use of land unsuitable for cultivation; a household may maintain relationships with settlements in which grain or tools can be obtained. Mobile groups therefore neither simply precede cities nor exist wholly outside their economies. Herding, farming and trade can be joined within a single family network, across successive seasons or through exchanges between different communities. Political authority may follow a group of people rather than apply uniformly to a bounded tract of land.

A journey introduces requirements that a local harvest cannot meet. The caravan needs animals, fodder, repair, secure halts, trustworthy information, guides and agreements with persons capable of obstructing passage. Credit permits goods to move before their final sale, but spreads vulnerability across partners who may be separated for months. In the Old Assyrian trading networks of the early second millennium BCE, merchants from Ashur carried tin and textiles to Anatolia and obtained metals in return. The Kültepe/Kanesh tablets record consignments, loans, household correspondence, disputes and transaction costs. They attest a commercial organization that depended on both settled scribal practice and repeated travel, and on households as well as itinerant merchants. 

A route is therefore not a physical line alone. Its viability depends upon continuing assurances: the donkey and its load need food and water, the merchant needs safe passage and a forum to settle a debt, and the buyer needs confidence that goods and information are what they claim to be. Local hospitality and protection become economic and moral relations rather than optional sentiment. A powerful group can profit by guaranteeing passage; it can also turn control of a well or narrow crossing into extortion. In neither case does the desert determine the morality of the arrangement.

The contrast between a canal and a caravan is consequently relative, not absolute. Both involve material infrastructures, accumulated knowledge, specialized labour and enforceable understandings. A canal directs water along a constrained course; a caravan moves commodities between separated centres. They differ in the kind of territory and personnel through which decisions take effect. Rivers could carry boats, and desert towns could possess settled archives. No division between 'bureaucratic peoples' and 'tribal peoples' follows from the geographical distinction.

The Levant under competing powers.

The Levant linked northern Syrian cities and Anatolia with the Nile region, the Mediterranean and the approaches to Arabia. Coastal ports had access to shipping and imported materials; valleys and uplands supported different combinations of cultivation, herding and settlement. A kingdom controlling one prosperous city did not thereby administer the countryside continuously or command every route used by neighbouring communities. Its effective authority depended on surrounding villages, local chiefs, armed personnel and the willingness or inability of greater rulers to intervene.

The fourteenth-century BCE Amarna correspondence makes the structure unusually tangible. Vassal rulers in Canaan wrote to the Egyptian court about rival rulers, security, service, trade and tribute. Their professions of submission did not eliminate their local conflicts; subordination to Pharaoh could coexist with attempts to attract his support against a rival. A demand imposed through the imperial system became a burden carried by particular inhabitants, even when the written exchange records only the negotiations of their rulers. The letters are invaluable for political relations, but their elite perspective cannot be treated as a complete account of every village or household. 

An imperial change altered the practical conditions of local life without always replacing its people. A new overlord might demand tribute, station soldiers, appoint or confirm a local ruler, interfere with disputes or direct goods along a different road. Traders might gain protected access to a market while cultivators faced increased assessment; a town might be spared destruction but lose the autonomy of its decisions. The effects of empire must be followed through these mechanisms rather than expressed solely as a succession of dynastic names.

Destruction and renewal at the end of the Bronze Age.

Around 1200 BCE several interconnected palace-centred regimes of the eastern Mediterranean were disrupted. Some sites experienced destruction or abandonment; the Hittite imperial system and Ugarit's palace order ceased in their earlier forms; trading patterns and political alliances were reorganized. Yet the processes differed sharply by region. Coastal communities, especially those later identified as Phoenician, preserved substantial continuities, and other settlements adapted or developed under changed conditions. There was no single uniform day of collapse and no secure explanation assigning every regional change to one climate event, invading people or political failure. 

This unevenness matters because institutions survive in different pieces. A royal court may disappear while a harbour continues to operate; a long-range political alliance may end while village fields remain cultivated; a temple may lose an endowment while households continue older rites. Destruction can interrupt knowledge, but it may also redistribute the persons who carry it. Neither outcome is determined solely by the former power of the institution. The later Iron Age involved the formation of new regional configurations, including the Aramaean kingdoms and the small kingdoms of Israel and Judah, alongside enduring cities of the coast. These developments must be dated and reconstructed from their own sources rather than made consequences of one generalized civilizational catastrophe. 

Political plurality also changed the settings in which allegiance could be expressed. A community dependent upon a patron king may face obligations to him and to an external empire; a local religious law may command conduct that the existing government does not reward. Such conditions do not establish that one religious community was uniformly more faithful than another. They give concrete form to the distinction between the validity of an obligation and the coercive capacity of the institution ordinarily associated with it.

Arabia's oases and the coexistence of different forms of life.

Arid lands concentrate settlement at places where groundwater, springs or episodic flows permit dependable cultivation. Protection of a well or cultivated enclosure can be as important to a household as possession of a field. Agreements over water affect planting, animal use, hospitality and the possibility of passage. Oases are not merely temporary stopping places for mobile groups: they can support durable households, specialized production, written communication and fortifications.

Archaeological work in northwestern Arabia identifies long-standing walled oasis settlements, including Taymāʾ and Qurayyah, with roots in the Bronze Age; the chronology and function of individual walls remain under research. Excavations at Dadan in the al-ʿUlā valley provide safely dated evidence of local date-palm cultivation in the twelfth–eleventh centuries BCE and greater oasis development in the first millennium BCE. These finds support distinct, long-lived forms of Arabian settlement rather than a picture of an undifferentiated desert inhabited only by nomads. 

Oases did not make mobile exchange unnecessary. The cultivator needed tools or goods absent from the locality; moving groups needed water, provisions and sometimes markets; towns and travellers depended upon arrangements able to withstand disputes. The resulting interdependence could be stabilizing or exploitative. A fortified wall may protect crops and stores or mark the coercive reach of whoever controls them. The evidence does not permit an unbroken institutional genealogy from Bronze Age Taymāʾ or Dadan to the Quraysh of sixth-century Makkah. Makkah's specific sanctity, local power and seasonal trading practices require their own historically dated reconstruction.

Sacred places and political places: related but not identical.

Palace, sanctuary, market and household may share a locality while exercising different claims upon people. A temple may possess an estate or receive goods, but its rites do not reduce to the management of property. A king may protect a sanctuary and still be answerable to obligations that his protection does not create. A market can operate within political guarantees without receiving its standards of fairness from the mere will of the guarantor. A holy centre may draw people who owe political obedience elsewhere. Its religious role does not require it to function as the capital of the largest neighbouring state.

Each institution has both a proper service and a characteristic deformation. Storage becomes hoarding when goods needed for a legitimate purpose are withheld without right. Protection becomes monopoly when a guardian treats those protected as possessions. Sacred stewardship becomes appropriation when custodians present access to worship as an exclusive entitlement of their lineage or office. Kingship becomes tyranny when coordination and judgment are subordinated to the ruler's independent appetite for power. These are differences of action and relation, not conclusions drawn from the size or outward splendour of an institution.

A religious centre can unite people whose territories, languages and trades remain diverse. The Abrahamic House will give this possibility a particular revealed form. Its role cannot be deduced from the shapes of earlier Near Eastern temples, nor does its sacredness erase the material acts through which visitors are fed, defended and received. The contrast between service to a sanctuary and possession of its privileges later becomes acute in the Meccan Sīrah.

Prophetic judgment enters the actual arrangements of provision.

The prophetic stories name several misdirections of genuine capacities rather than condemning the capacities themselves. Hūd's people use construction and strength; the reproach falls upon imagined permanence and oppressive force (26:128–130). Ṣāliḥ's people cultivate, build and use a common water source; their test concerns the specified share owed to the divine sign (26:146–158). Shuʿayb confronts the use of commercial ingenuity to falsify measure and diminish the claims of others (11:84–88). Yūsuf's interpretation becomes a public discipline of cultivation, preservation and distribution (12:47–49). Mūsā confronts a ruler who has made the ordering of a population into a means of humiliation and violence (28:4). The Qurʾān's particular acts prevent 'civilization' from becoming an empty praise or an equally empty accusation.

The material conditions must be specified before the moral judgment can be understood. An accurate measure has a physical relation to the goods measured; withholding part of an agreed quantity injures the recipient rather than merely displaying an unattractive disposition. A water schedule is implemented at a place and time; destroying the protected claimant changes what others can actually receive. A grain reserve sustains life only when protected against deterioration and allocated when needed. A ruler's instruction becomes oppression through officials, categories, exactions and acts imposed upon bodies. Right worship reaches these transactions because they involve the rights of persons and creatures whose reality does not originate in the powerful agent's desire.

Providence does not absolve an unjust actor by bringing an unexpected good from his action. A great city can preserve art and knowledge while committing wrongs against those who build it. Nor does material success establish that a people have accepted guidance. The proper relation between inward recognition and outward discipline must be enacted through practices and institutions capable of responding to actual claims.

Knowledge and obligations beyond the loss of sovereignty.

Material records and religious transmissions have different vulnerabilities. A field survey may retain its precision only while the same boundaries and units remain in force; a temple archive may become obsolete when its estate is confiscated. An injunction, remembered account, prayer or legal teaching may be repeated in a different place, although language, instruction and stable communal practice are still necessary for its survival. It would be mistaken to oppose a territorially fixed state to a wholly immaterial religion. Teaching depends on teachers and households, texts on copying and preservation, rites on places and times, and justice on some means of applying judgments.

The distinction is between the obligation itself and the political apparatus that can protect or enforce it. A community may preserve an inherited command after losing its king; it may also preserve a formula while forgetting the disposition or understanding through which that formula is rightly lived. Conversely, the survival of a crown or building is not evidence that the duty it once served remains effective. The forms of continuity are therefore multiple: textual, pedagogical, familial, liturgical, judicial and political. Each can support the others without being identical to them.

The histories of Israel and Judah embody both the strength and the difficulty of this differentiation. Kings, territory and the Jerusalem sanctuary became powerful supports for communal life. Defeat and displacement transformed those supports without simply abolishing the possibility of teaching, worship or covenantal identity. The historical question is not whether a people can dispense with every institution, but which institutions can carry an obligation when its former political setting no longer exists and which practices prevent that obligation from becoming only a name.

### 03. Israel and Judah: kingship, destruction and life beyond a kingdom

**Matn**

The biblical and Qurʾānic memory of Dāwūd and Sulaymān joins kingship to worship and judgment. A king's authority might defend a sanctuary and settle disputes, but its exercise also depended on troops, tribute and subjects who owed one another rights. The kingdoms of Israel and Judah occupied the contested Levant, where Assyrian and Babylonian power steadily restricted their independence. In 722 or 721 BCE Samaria fell; Babylon later imposed control upon Judah, took Jerusalem in 597, and destroyed its temple in 587 or 586 BCE.

Conquest disrupted the relationship among people, land, king and sanctuary. Those deported had to preserve memory and practice beyond the institutions that had once gathered them; those who remained encountered new rulers and obligations. Families taught children, scribes preserved texts, and calendrical worship gave scattered communities a recognizable order. Persian conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE enabled reconstruction of the Jerusalem temple, while religious life continued across more than one place and political jurisdiction.

A community's continuity had thus become an active achievement. Its words needed transmission, disputes needed adjudication, and its rites needed persons who knew how to perform them. A throne could secure these activities without being identical with them. The later existence of Jewish communities in Arabia belongs to a long history of movement and settlement, though individual family routes cannot be reconstructed from the earliest exile alone.

These changes disclose the difference between a spiritual obligation and the contingent means by which it is carried. Fidelity may be exercised by a ruler while he governs or by a parent teaching a child under foreign rule. The obligation endures through historical interruption when people sustain the practices and relationships capable of bearing it.

**Sharḥ**

The transition from monarchy to dispersion changes the central historical problem. When the temple could no longer function and the king no longer secured the territory, continuity depended more heavily on scribal teaching, kin relations, calendar, prayer, law and ritual practice. The return under Persian supremacy restored a centre but not a self-sufficient sovereign state. This helps explain how identifiable Jewish communities could persist through Greek, Roman and Sasanian changes of rule. Jewish communities maintained religious institutions in Arabia, but the surviving evidence does not trace every family's ancestry to the Babylonian exile.

The changing medium of religious continuity is the decisive bridge. Royal patronage can maintain a central sanctuary; a dispersed community must teach children, preserve forms of worship, settle questions internally and recognize people of the same religious tradition elsewhere. Some of these activities developed over long periods, and their exact early chronology is contested. The conceptual connection to Islam's later history is nevertheless concrete: after the caliphate fragmented, Muslim communities too relied upon learning, law and travel beyond the reach of one political centre. Judean communities transmitted scripture through priests and scribes; Islamic madrasas formed much later under different political and educational conditions.

The disappearance of a throne does not extinguish every form in which a community receives and lives its religious obligations. The king, priest, scribe, parent and judge perform different kinds of work: sovereignty can defend a sanctuary, but teaching preserves words and practices within a household, and adjudication settles disputes that require an accountable procedure. This is an early historical illustration of a principial distinction, not a claim that later Muslim legal schools descended from one Israelite institution. A spiritual form can survive the failure of one political vehicle because its realization is not identical with possession of coercive power. Yet the actual survival of texts and observances depends on material transmission—teachers, families, calendars, records and places of assembly. The two levels must be explained together without making either the automatic product of the other.

The Arabian connection must remain deliberately modest. Evidence that Jews were established in Yathrib in the Prophet's lifetime is not evidence identifying the specific route by which each family reached it. Jewish communities established roots in Arabia over different periods, but surviving sources do not establish one ancestry or migration date for all of them. The stronger line of explanation is how religious observance and distinctive communal law could exist under governments not controlled by those communities.

The Qurʾān recounts the missions of Mūsā, Dāwūd and Sulaymān (2:47–61; 21:78–82; 34:10–14); Kings, Chronicles and Ezra–Nehemiah preserve related but distinct histories, while Near Eastern evidence helps date the conquests of 722, 597 and 587/586 BCE. The Qurʾān and the biblical books recount some shared prophetic figures but differ in emphases, narrative structure and theological claims. Late-antique Jewish communities could be connected by learning and ritual despite local variations in language, social hierarchy and political status. This will later matter in Madinah, where the Prophet did not encounter an abstract “Judaism” but households and groups with land, alliances and particular obligations. Before that, the Persian and Greek worlds changed the languages and intellectual settings in which such communities lived.

### 04. Religious disciplines and the changing world of the first millennium BCE

**Matn**

Empires and dispersed religious communities gave the first millennium BCE several centres of learning. Jewish households maintained teaching across different states; Iranian traditions developed their own practices of worship and moral judgment; Indian teachers transmitted disciplines of contemplation, renunciation and action. Greek cities fostered inquiry into nature, argument and political order. These histories did not occur in one place or according to one timetable, and their surviving records differ in kind.

The Iranian traditions associated with Zoroaster, the teachings remembered around the Buddha in India, and the inquiries of early Greek thinkers addressed different questions of worship, discipline, suffering and the order of nature. Their lives and words are known through sources compiled and transmitted in different periods. The comparison becomes historically responsible when a teacher and teaching are located within a real linguistic and institutional setting before their more universal philosophical significance is considered.

A teacher forms a pupil by more than transmitting a formula. Repeated recitation trains memory; disciplined conduct reshapes appetite and intention; attention to an argument develops the power to distinguish a valid conclusion from a persuasive appearance. These are diverse excellences because their objects differ. A rite, an act of moral courage and a demonstration require different preparation even when all are situated within a comprehensive pursuit of truth.

Persian imperial routes, Hellenistic cities, trading contacts and translations enabled some forms of exchange. Particular influence requires a text, a movement of people or an identifiable chain of reception; similarity of theme alone supplies a question for investigation. The different traditions also retained disagreements over God, world, liberation, law and salvation that cannot be settled by a general likeness among their disciplines.

The human search for right orientation therefore appears here through distinct intellectual and communal forms. Its historical story is written in languages, schools, temples, families and courts. The later Muslim encounter with these worlds would inherit both their books and the practical conditions through which those books had been studied.

**Sharḥ**

Karl Jaspers later grouped several developments under the label “Axial Age”; their different dates and settings do not establish one common historical cause. The dates of the Buddha, Zoroaster, Laozi and individual early Greek teachers are disputed to varying degrees. Connections must therefore be shown individually: an imperial road or shared administrative language can support movement of people and texts, whereas similarity between two doctrines does not by itself prove borrowing. Persia's administrative reach and the Hellenistic spread of Greek give stronger grounds for certain western Eurasian contacts than for a single transformation spanning India, China and Greece.

Guénon's argument also requires a correction often missing in its popular retellings. He considered the sixth century a threshold of what he called ordinary historical accessibility, but surviving Mesopotamian, Egyptian and other sources allow detailed chronology much earlier. His historical premise cannot be accepted without qualification, and his judgment of Buddhism or philosophy reflects his own conception of orthodoxy and intellectual hierarchy. The intellectually fruitful question is narrower: did some traditions respond to changing social conditions by differentiating ritual, practical discipline and contemplative knowledge? That can be studied separately in each tradition. It matters for the later Islamic account because philosophy, law, revelation and spiritual realization also came to be distinguishable modes of teaching, though their boundaries were drawn differently.

The comparison of Jewish, Iranian, Indian, Greek and Chinese traditions becomes fruitful only when it distinguishes a possible analogy of spiritual questions from evidence of direct historical contact. Disciplines of attention, sacrifice, intellectual inquiry or moral cultivation can be examined as different ways human capacities are ordered toward ends. Their doctrines are not interchangeable simply because they cultivate similar virtues. In each setting there are named teachers, languages, political patrons, practices of memorization and conflicting reports of chronology. A metaphysical interpretation may inquire into the recurrent relation between contemplation and action; historical explanation must still establish which texts travelled, who translated them, and which institutions preserved them. This discipline of the two eyes resists both a reduction of religion to imperial circumstance and an ahistorical picture in which distinctive teachings lose their content.

Guénon's metaphysical interpretation of sacred cycles and the dated history of individual teachers address different kinds of claims. Achaemenid government, Hellenistic cities and Greek scriptural translations created identifiable political and linguistic links across the western Near East. For China and India, the comparisons concern developing traditions of moral and contemplative training; their presence in one paragraph is not an argument that they directly shaped first-century Christianity or the seventh-century Ḥijāz. Guénon's sixth-century claim is a metaphysical-periodizing judgment with its own disputed historical assumptions, not an empirical explanation of the co-occurrence of named founders.

René Guénon's *The Crisis of the Modern World*, chapter 1, gives a different reading: he associates transformations around the sixth century BCE with an increasingly differentiated and outward historical phase of traditional civilizations. His claim concerns spiritual cycles and transformations in the accessibility of principial knowledge, not simply a chronology of founders. Guénon interprets the changes through spiritual cycles; the available dates do not demonstrate that a single rupture occurred across Eurasia. The Prophet's contemporaries inherited a world of earlier scriptural communities, philosophical traditions and rival institutions; none of those inheritances alone explains the emergence of his message. The immediate lineage leading to the seventh-century Near East passes through Hellenistic and Roman Judaea, Christian churches and the imperial frontiers.

### 05. Jesus and the communities that carried his message

**Matn**

In first-century Judaea, ʿĪsā lived among people governed under Roman power and shaped by the worship of Jerusalem, the authority of Scripture and disagreements over purity, rule and the promised future. The Qurʾān honours Maryam, tells of his extraordinary birth and presents his mission as a summons to God, mercy and obedience. Communities that later called themselves Christian remembered his preaching and works through accounts that came to be read and interpreted in several languages.

Paul, one of the earliest missionaries of the Christian movement, wrote letters to communities of believers across the eastern Mediterranean. Their arguments about law, faith and the identity of Jesus contributed to debates that continued long after his death. Bishops and councils later gave some doctrinal positions public forms of authority, while Christian communities in Syriac and Greek preserved traditions of worship and interpretation through their own teachers. These developments belong to histories that cannot simply be read backward into the life of ʿĪsā.

His followers gathered in households and towns where Roman roads, ports and urban networks carried letters and teaching. Their teachers had to explain the relation between Israel's Scriptures and the message they now proclaimed. Disputes over law, the status of Jesus, communal boundaries and the relation of worship to public life developed through preaching, debate and evolving offices. Greek, Syriac, Coptic and Latin-speaking communities did not share identical circumstances.

As imperial patronage expanded in the fourth century, bishops and councils acquired new public weight. A doctrinal formula could be debated in a school, required by a ruler and recited in worship, each setting assigning different powers and responsibilities. Monastic communities fostered prayer, discipline, copying and hospitality, while ordinary households maintained patterns of belief and charity across generations.

The Qurʾānic presentation of ʿĪsā and the doctrinal claims later adopted by churches differ on decisive points. Their comparison requires faithful exposition of each account. The larger history shows how religious words become intelligible and durable through persons who teach, contest, embody and organize them, and how an inherited practice can survive a political setting while still being shaped by it.

**Sharḥ**

The Islamic account of ʿĪsā and Maryam belongs to revealed history (notably Qurʾān 3:33–55; 5:110–120; 19:16–36). The earliest recoverable Christian histories rest on a different kind of evidence: first-century letters, Gospel narratives, later patristic writings and documents of councils. Christian creeds developed through councils and theological disputes; their later formulations cannot automatically be attributed to the earliest disciples. The controversy surrounding Nicaea (325), Ephesus (431) and Chalcedon (451) needs to be understood as sustained argument among Christian communities, not as a single moment when Christianity became homogeneous.

Christianity also changed the geography of learning. In Greek-speaking eastern cities, philosophical and theological argument shared a vocabulary capable of crossing political boundaries. Syriac translators and teachers moved between theological, medical and philosophical work, sometimes under rulers of different religions. This made them potential mediators when Muslim patrons later sought Greek learning in Arabic. The intellectual bridge is therefore not a vague statement that Islam was 'influenced by Christianity'; it is the documented existence of bilingual scholars, texts and schools available within the regions that Muslim armies and merchants later encountered. In the Sīrah itself the immediate issues were worship, prophecy and protection, not the ninth-century translation of Aristotle.

In the Islamic account, ʿĪsā is a messenger whose birth, words and works disclose divine guidance to a particular people. The later Christian history concerns communities receiving, disputing and institutionalizing what they understood of that mission. These are related but different objects of inquiry. A spiritual reading of prophetic dependence and mercy does not by itself settle the historical formation of Trinitarian doctrine, the authority of bishops or the relations of Syriac- and Greek-speaking churches. The outward plurality matters: translation changes the vocabulary available for argument, imperial sponsorship affects councils, and local worship forms habits within families. A properly comprehensive account attends to the spiritual question, the truth claims at issue and the historical causes separately. It also makes clear where the Qurʾānic presentation and Christian testimony offer divergent accounts rather than silently harmonizing them.

A concrete mechanism in Christian expansion is linguistic and urban. Letters to congregations could circulate along roads and shipping routes connecting Greek-speaking cities; local assemblies could read them, dispute their meaning and transmit instruction even without a common local bishop. Later councils and imperial patronage made certain disputes politically consequential. At the Arabian frontier, however, Greek, Syriac, Coptic and Geʿez Christian lives did not form one uniform audience for Islam. Each source needs to identify the community it actually describes.

The causal thread is institutional as well as doctrinal. Greek and Syriac writing made teachings travel; churches offered regular worship and communal leadership; monasticism provided spaces for disciplined life, copying and teaching. Imperial patronage offered resources and created new occasions for controversy, since bishops and emperors could have different interests. Aksum's Christianity and Syriac-speaking communities in Persian territory meant that the Prophet's contemporaries encountered Christian practices beyond Constantinople's control. Muslims encountered Christian neighbours, envoys, protectors, clergy and monks as well as arguments about the person of Christ. Their routes of movement traversed empires and Arabian borderlands where religion, commerce and military protection intersected.

### 06. The sixth-century crossroads of Rome, Persia, Ethiopia and Arabia

**Matn**

Sixth-century Arabia stood between powers that ruled great territories yet depended on local allies. From Constantinople Roman emperors governed Syria and Egypt; Sasanian kings commanded Iran and Iraq. Arab groups on the imperial frontiers entered agreements of defence, patronage and trade, while kingdoms and ports around the Red Sea connected Africa with South Arabia. At Makkah, the sanctuary and clan alliances organized religious and commercial life under different conditions from those of an imperial capital.

Two Arab federations illustrate how imperial reach depended upon local people. The Ghassanid-linked groups on the Roman frontier and the Lakhmid rulers associated with the Sasanians held their own alliances and competed for patronage. Across the Red Sea, Aksumite power also intersected with southern Arabian affairs. None of these arrangements unified Arabia, but their soldiers, routes and diplomatic relationships helped determine how people travelled and whom they expected to protect them.

War between Rome and Persia moved through these frontiers and consumed troops, treasure and agricultural resources. A caravan's safety depended on negotiated access, a traveller's kin protectors and the power of those who might obstruct the route. Merchants knew prices, transport and debt; household heads judged obligations to dependants; frontier rulers balanced loyalties to distant patrons against local survival. No single political command joined all these forms of work.

Christian, Jewish and Arabian religious communities also lived in this world. Their scriptures and worship were transmitted through teachers, families and places of assembly, and their disagreements sometimes acquired political consequences. The same region could contain imperial administration, local courts and different forms of sacred authority, each acting through identifiable people.

When the final Messenger proclaimed revelation, its hearers already inhabited this intricate order. Their decisions about whether to listen, protect him, migrate or oppose the message followed existing relations of family and power as well as conviction. Divine address would become visible in history through the transformation of particular lives, while its later public effects would require the further labour of treaties, teaching, livelihood and government.

**Sharḥ**

An empire's presence on the frontier did not make every caravan route a branch of its administration. The Ghassanid and Lakhmid networks involved military patronage, kinship and competition, while the Red Sea introduced Ethiopian, South Arabian and Persian interests with different reach. Literary accounts of Najrān and the Aksumite invasion need comparison with epigraphic and archaeological evidence; Valentina Grasso's analysis of sixth-century South Arabia cautions against reducing every political intervention to a single religious motive. Frontier rulers mobilized military allies, collected revenues and offered varying degrees of protection to religious communities.

The battle between imperial powers also reshaped what an Arabian leader might encounter as expansion proceeded. Muslim forces moving into Syria entered territory with military roads, bishoprics and taxation already organized; movements into Iraq encountered Sasanian estates and administrative practices. The conquest did not invent those systems and could not rule without engaging them. Yet the Prophet's first listeners were not Roman subjects receiving a centrally enforced imperial creed. Meccan believers depended on personal protection and voluntary allegiance; after conquest, Muslim rulers administered the taxes and offices of former imperial provinces.

The Prophet's eventual mission unfolded in a world with existing loyalties, cities, borders, armies and commercial techniques. These conditions neither produce revelation nor render the particular problems of receiving it insignificant. A merchant's promise depended on trust and enforcement, an Arabian ally's safety on kin protection, and the frontier ruler's power on supplies and more distant patrons. Such arrangements reveal different orders of practical action: personal fidelity, household and tribal responsibility, and the exercise of public command. The same human being can belong to all three, but each possesses a distinct object and set of constraints. That is why the later spread of Muslim rule must be explained through military, fiscal and linguistic processes rather than treated as a direct historical consequence of a metaphysical claim about the Prophet.

The Roman–Sasanian war weakened both empires before the Muslim conquests, but the surviving evidence does not establish that it caused the Prophetic proclamation. It did weaken both imperial systems before major Muslim conquests. Makkah's particular situation instead depended on sanctuary customs, Quraysh's clan arrangements and regional trade. Early Muslim sources place the Year of the Elephant near the Prophet's birth, but their accounts of the expedition and its dating differ. Abū Ṭālib protected Muḥammad through kinship obligations even though he did not adopt the new proclamation.

Najrān records Christian persecution and Aksumite intervention, but it supplies no direct causal evidence for Muḥammad's proclamation in Makkah. Najrān documents the presence of Christianity, regional persecution and cross-Red-Sea intervention; it does not prove that the Meccan message arose from imperial conflict. The Roman–Persian war matters differently: it transformed the military position of the two states into whose provinces Muslim forces would later move. Thus the local origin of the mission and the geopolitical environment of its later expansion have distinct histories.

## Book II — The formation of believers in Makkah

### 07. The sanctuary, the clans and the obligations of protection

**Matn**

Makkah's sanctuary gathered clans whose kinship loyalties and commercial interests often diverged. Its inviolability made certain acts of violence or pursuit especially serious; Quraysh families maintained services and agreements associated with pilgrimage. Protection nevertheless remained unequally distributed. The person with a powerful clan defender could speak more freely than someone enslaved, dependent or newly arrived without allies.

The Prophet's own kin belonged to Banū Hāshim within Quraysh. His uncle Abū Ṭālib's protection showed how the obligations of a respected household could provide space for a public proclamation. Bilāl, remembered among the earliest believers and exposed to persecution as an enslaved man, stood in a very different position of vulnerability. The two lives show why a call addressed equally to human beings encountered unequal relations of power and why the duty of protection could become costly for those able to offer it.

The Prophet's call placed worship of one God above inherited prestige. The challenge was heard by people whose daily security and livelihood were bound to the very social arrangements it questioned. His uncle Abū Ṭālib defended him through kinship even while religious disagreement persisted. Other followers met punishments or pressures from which the same ties could not protect them. The moral meaning of the summons appeared differently in each relationship: one person bore suffering, another interceded, a third relinquished power over a dependent.

The sanctuary oriented different groups toward a shared sacred place, yet sacred orientation had to acquire the further form of protection for actual persons. A promise of safety becomes effective when somebody honours it at personal cost. A religious allegiance becomes socially durable when households cultivate restraint, fidelity and mutual responsibility. The distance between these two achievements would shape the later search for refuge and alliance.

The Meccan years therefore disclose the beginning of communal formation before a Muslim polity existed. The first believers learned that the same truth required steadfastness, trust, judgment and different practical acts according to their circumstances.

**Sharḥ**

Kinship and patronage determined who could challenge prevailing religious customs without immediately losing protection. A free man with an influential guardian, an enslaved person and a visitor without an alliance did not face the same risks when accepting a new religious allegiance. This social difference helps explain why early conversions followed ties of trust and household contact, and why some believers could resist pressure while others suffered violence. It would be anachronistic to describe Quraysh's sanctuary as a modern government. Tribal obligation, ritual privilege, negotiated protection and exchange could overlap without creating one centralized state. The Sīrah tradition's accounts of the Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl and the Prophet's protection by Abū Ṭālib are valuable evidence for how contemporaries remembered justice and patronage; the wording and sequence of those accounts need comparison across transmitters.

The protection system helps explain both the survival of the Prophet's mission and the injustice suffered by vulnerable converts. Abū Ṭālib's defence of his nephew was a recognizable kin obligation, even while religious disagreement persisted. A client or enslaved convert could lack a similarly effective protector. Accepting the new call was therefore not one uniform act of social defiance. The religion's claim of common accountability sat within highly unequal capacities to resist. Later migration did not remove the importance of kinship immediately; it placed inherited loyalties into new relations with people whose commitment rested upon worship rather than birth.

The sanctuary and the duty of protection illuminate the difference between a sacred orientation and the effective justice of a city. The Kaʿbah gathered rival groups around a place invested with inviolability, but individual persons received unequal protection according to clan, gender, servitude and alliance. The Prophet's call addressed those persons within that imperfect order. One believer could be shielded by a powerful relative while another could be injured without comparable recourse. Fidelity to God became visible in an actual choice to protect, endure or bear witness; it could not be inferred from the outward prestige of a sanctuary. The movement from personal allegiance to the public security later sought in Madinah marks a development in the means by which rightful claims were recognized. Sacred direction, moral character and enforceable protection are related but irreducible.

Mecca's exact commercial scale is disputed. Accounts of Quraysh's seasonal journeys record remembered patterns of commerce, whereas claims that particular Meccan families dominated long-distance luxury trade remain disputed. The protection system is more immediately relevant to the first believers: clan standing could shield a dissenting member while enslaved or client households faced coercion. That difference explains the uneven distribution of suffering without implying that the first converts belonged to one social class.

Quraysh organized commercial journeys, but historians disagree about the volume and geographical reach of Meccan trade; evidence does not establish that its clans controlled a vast trading empire. Qurʾān 106 emphasizes journeys, security and provision, while the institutional details come through later Sīrah and genealogy. A proclamation that challenged sacred objects and inherited status also threatened the loyalties through which clans maintained protection and authority. In Yathrib, meanwhile, the aftermath of conflicts such as Buʿāth provided a different political setting. The earliest instruction would begin in Muḥammad's household, where personal confidence and protection mattered before public authority existed.

### 08. Ḥirāʾ, Khadījah and the beginnings of a teaching community

**Matn**

Muḥammad grew up in the care of relatives after losing his parents and came to be known through his conduct among the people of Makkah. He married Khadījah, whose home joined affection, economic responsibility and a dependable companionship. When he sought solitude at Ḥirāʾ, his withdrawal brought him to the threshold of an event that no human preparation could produce. The angelic address commanded him to recite; he returned deeply shaken and asked to be covered.

The early circle formed through relatives, freed persons and companions whose ordinary ties became occasions for learning and courage. Abū Bakr, the Prophet's trusted companion, and ʿAlī, his young cousin who grew up close to him, would later receive distinct responsibilities. Their first allegiance belongs to the period when knowledge of the revelation was sustained through intimate conversation and a small community of practice. These names matter because later public offices and learned traditions would develop from people initially known through their personal fidelity.

Khadījah received him in the knowledge of a life she had witnessed. She recalled his care for kin, truthfulness, hospitality and help for people in distress. She then brought him to Waraqah, a relative acquainted with earlier scripture, who recognized the encounter in relation to prophecy. These were different forms of discernment: the Messenger received revelation; his wife recognized the moral integrity of the person entrusted with it; Waraqah supplied a scriptural comparison. Their words took place before there was a public Muslim school or state.

The household became the first place of consolation, prayer and instruction. Early believers learned recitation and conduct through personal trust, repeated acts and companionship. They were not formed by a system of detachable propositions alone. The revealed words addressed a man who already lived among spouses, relatives, neighbours and dependants, and those relationships furnished the first occasions for the message to become visible as action.

The unity of the prophetic life took shape through the faithful exercise of distinct responsibilities. In the years that followed, the same guidance would be learned by people whose experiences of security, poverty, kinship and spiritual longing were very different.

**Sharḥ**

ʿĀʾishah's report in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (no. 3) recounts the first revelation, Khadījah's reassurance and Waraqah's recognition of the prophetic encounter. Khadījah's response is more than a gesture of reassurance: she names traits of conduct that can be tested in ordinary social relationships—kinship, hospitality, truthfulness and aid to the burdened. The report connects an extraordinary encounter to recognizable moral character without making a private experience into a publicly verifiable event of the same kind as a dated treaty. The initial call in Qurʾān 96:1–5 brings recitation, creation and knowledge together; passages such as 74:1–7 connect warning with purification and patience. Different reports arrange the earliest revelations and conversions differently. The surviving reports do not establish one unambiguous chronological order among the first converts.

There is an intellectual distinction between the event of revelation and the social process by which people learned to trust its messenger. The former, within Muslim belief, is God's address to the Prophet; the latter involves repeated teaching, memory, recognition of moral conduct and decisions taken by particular persons. Khadījah's role links those orders without merging them. Her reassurance addresses the Prophet personally, but the qualities she cites later become intelligible to others as grounds for trust. Similarly, an early follower's prayer is an act of worship and at the same time a repeated practice through which a shared form of life becomes recognizable.

Khadījah, the Prophet's wife and the first intimate witness to the beginning of revelation, does not merely supply social support to an otherwise complete public message. In the first-revelation account she recognizes a moral continuity between the startling divine address and the husband's known truthfulness, care for kin and defence of the vulnerable. Her discernment is exercised within marriage: the household becomes the first place where an overwhelming event is received, interpreted and answered through trust. The Prophet receives revelation; his wife recognizes the recipient through a life she already knows; Waraqah, a relative acquainted with earlier scripture, supplies a different kind of recognition. Their acts have distinct objects and cannot replace one another. The relation between spiritual reception and outward formation appears here before any developed school, state or legal profession.

Khadījah appealed to Muḥammad's established conduct, while Waraqah connected the encounter with earlier prophecy. In Bukhārī's opening revelation report, Khadījah's reassurance appeals to observable qualities of Muḥammad's conduct, while Waraqah places the encounter within remembered prophecy. That is not the same as independent archival corroboration of the private experience at Ḥirāʾ. In the transmitted account, Khadījah first reassured Muḥammad through her knowledge of his character, and Waraqah subsequently related his encounter to earlier prophecy. Khadījah, members of the household and other early believers heard the recitations, learned practices of worship and formed the first circle of instruction.

The transition from a revealed word to a growing community required human processes: someone had to hear it, learn its recitation, recognize the teacher's trustworthiness, practise prayer and make a judgment about allegiance. Khadījah's home, Abū Bakr's friendships and other networks of instruction each helped that process. Yet the new allegiance would eventually alter the meaning of those older ties. A believer might remain a son, client or merchant while refusing a form of worship expected by his household. That friction grew as teaching moved beyond familiar households into a public proclamation demanding changes in conduct.

### 09. The Meccan proclamation: worship, reckoning and new loyalties

**Matn**

The early recitations summoned Makkans to worship one God, attend to resurrection and give an account of their dealings with others. Reckoning placed the wealthy merchant, the guardian of an orphan and the vulnerable labourer before a judgment wider than family rank. It addressed actions already woven into property, inheritance, slavery, hospitality and trade. The strength of a clan could obtain obedience in this world but could not turn injustice into truth.

Bilāl's remembered endurance under persecution and the generosity of believers who assisted vulnerable people gave material shape to the proclamation. Their acts crossed the boundary between a person's inward allegiance and another person's real conditions of life. Freedom, security and food were received as goods with claims attached to them, while generosity required someone to surrender power or property. The recitations' address to the poor and the proud therefore entered the society through choices made within identifiable households.

Believers responded through specific choices. They prayed together, gave from their possessions, resisted pressure, freed enslaved persons and cared for those whom custom had left exposed. Wealth possessed a genuine use in feeding a household and sustaining commerce; its use became disordered when possession outweighed another person's rightful claim. Charity, fair dealing and faithful worship therefore engaged different faculties within one moral life.

This summons also changed relationships among people who had long depended on the same networks of protection. One household accepted the new faith while another opposed it; kinship could shelter a believer or become a source of conflict. The new fellowship had no treasury or court capable of enforcing most of its convictions. It grew through the reliability of persons willing to bear the cost of their allegiance.

The movement from recitation to formed character began in these ordinary acts. A remembered command acquired social effect through a person who understood it, restrained contrary desire and acted on the need of a neighbour. Later institutions would preserve different aspects of this original encounter between word, conscience and deed.

**Sharḥ**

Meccan revelation joins resurrection and judgment to demands for generosity, truthfulness and care for the vulnerable. Passages such as Qurʾān 81:1–14; 90:11–18; 92:5–21 and 107:1–7 join eschatological accountability to care for the vulnerable and critique ostentatious worship. The Meccan proclamation did not prohibit wealth or commerce as such; it demanded truthfulness, generosity and accountability before God in their pursuit and use. Recurring oaths, warnings, questions and scenes of the Last Day gave believers memorable language for examining daily conduct.

Giving, emancipation, prayer and restraint affected both relations among households and the intentions and habits of the believers who practised them. A person asked to free a slave must relinquish property; a person asked to give food must resist retaining it; a person commanded to endure mockery must discipline anger. The practices do not prove that every participant attained the virtue intended, but they identify occasions in which character was tested and cultivated. When later authors discuss *malakah*, intention or purification, they supply analytical language for such processes. Later writers developed theories of habit and virtue around practices that early believers already undertook: giving, prayer, restraint and endurance.

Reckoning changes the measure by which a person evaluates wealth and rank, but its social effects depend upon particular acts. A person who frees an enslaved dependent relinquishes an enforceable claim of ownership; a guardian who protects an orphan's property curbs a temptation that another person may be unable to resist. The Qurʾānic address joins inner conversion to real redistribution and a new understanding of obligation. Its comprehensive force lies in ordering belief, intention and the act itself, not in collapsing them into a single rule. Later legal classifications can specify ownership, guardianship and liability, while spiritual training can teach generosity and freedom from ostentation. Both inherit determinate aspects of guidance that originally confronted one person with another person's need.

When the proclamation made generosity and accountability obligatory, its claim reached the actual power relationships of a city. The master who owned a slave, the guardian handling an orphan's property and the rich merchant dispensing or withholding support faced newly articulated duties. The proclamation challenged exploitation, but Meccan masters and patrons continued to wield coercive power. The historical effect in Makkah was first the formation of a community that judged such conduct by different standards, often without the power to enforce them.

Later ethical writers such as al-Ghazālī describe the formation of stable dispositions through repeated action, examination of intention and correction of appetite. This offers a useful account of how practices may form character, but it is a later analytical reconstruction. Al-Ghazālī developed his technical account of moral psychology centuries after the earliest converts practised prayer, giving and patient endurance. Historically, the relevant evidence for persecution comes largely from transmitted reports about Bilāl, the family of Yāsir and other early believers; severity, chronology and the details of speeches need individual examination. Their different social protections made a common religious commitment costly in unequal ways. As persecution intensified and clan protection failed some believers, migration across the Red Sea offered an alternative source of safety.

### 10. Aksum: faith before political safety

**Matn**

As pressure increased in Makkah, groups of believers crossed the Red Sea to the Christian kingdom of Aksum. Their houses and kin ties had given them unequal protection; exile offered a different prospect under a ruler who could prevent their forced return. Travel separated families from familiar work and exposed them to dependence on strangers. Their religious fidelity now needed the additional good of a jurisdiction in which they could live.

When representatives of Quraysh sought their return, Jaʿfar ibn Abī Ṭālib, the Prophet's cousin and spokesman for the emigrants, explained their worship, their rejection of injustice and their duties toward others. In the Muslim accounts, he recited from the story of Maryam and ʿĪsā before the king, remembered as the Negus. The hearing turned upon religious difference, royal judgment and the concrete safety of displaced families. The king chose to protect them.

This refuge joined several kinds of action without confusing them. The emigrants kept faith under danger, Jaʿfar spoke truth before a foreign court, and the ruler used political power to restrain coercion. The safety of children and households rested upon the king's effective authority, while their religious practices depended upon their own continuing learning and resolve.

The later remembrance of the king includes reports of his faith and the Prophet's prayer for him. The initial migration nonetheless presents a distinct historical scene: a religious community survived because personal courage met lawful protection exercised beyond its own polity.

**Sharḥ**

The Aksum episode is usually narrated through Ibn Isḥāq as preserved by Ibn Hishām and other Sīrah collections. The dialogue reported between Jaʿfar ibn Abī Ṭālib, a Muslim emigrant and the Prophet's cousin, and the Negus, the Christian king of Aksum, illuminates how later transmitters understood the Muslim–Christian encounter, especially the claims made about ʿĪsā and Maryam. Later Sīrah recensions preserve the speech in varying forms, without establishing a contemporary verbatim transcript. The religious setting is nonetheless plausible and important: Aksum was a Christian kingdom with Red Sea connections, and Arabia's western shore participated in a network wider than Quraysh.

The Negus's later standing within the Islamic tradition belongs to the fuller account. In [al-Bukhārī 3877](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3877), the Prophet ﷺ announces Aṣḥamah's death, calls him a righteous brother and instructs the Companions to perform the funeral prayer. [Al-Bukhārī 1333](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:1333) also preserves the funeral prayer. His original protection of the migrants took place in a Christian royal setting; the later Muslim reports give a further dimension to his biography. The two moments allow a history of actual asylum and a history of faith to be understood on their own evidence.

Jaʿfar ibn Abī Ṭālib, a relative of the Prophet and spokesman for the Muslim emigrants, is remembered defending their faith before the Christian king of Aksum, traditionally called the Negus. The emigrants needed several distinct goods: a conscience able to endure persecution, a trustworthy explanation of their beliefs, royal protection from forced return, and the household stability that exile could make possible. Their witness did not itself confer political jurisdiction; it was heard by a ruler who possessed and chose to exercise that power. The reported exchange about Maryam and ʿĪsā also shows how the defence of a religious difference can coexist with the appeal to shared reverence. Later Muslim reports of the king's faith must be distinguished from the initial refuge narrative. The event links personal fidelity to international protection without turning either into a mere metaphor for the other.

Aksum also clarifies a difference between spiritual steadfastness and political capability. Perseverance may enable an individual to withstand loss, but it does not create a lawful jurisdiction under which his children can live. The migrants needed a ruler willing to refuse extradition and a place where worship could continue; those conditions were supplied by a polity not organized around their own community. The emigrants remained loyal to the Prophet while living under the Negus's protection across the Red Sea. Yathrib later supplied a different combination: local believers not only offered protection but undertook responsibilities of a common public order. Confusing these two forms of security obscures the development of the Hijrah.

The emigrants in Aksum could worship under the protection of a Christian sovereign without possessing jurisdiction over their hosts. That institutional difference matters more than anachronistic claims about modern citizenship or interfaith policy. The transmitted exchange before the Negus places Jesus and Mary at the centre of the emigrants' explanation of their faith; later Sīrah accounts preserve differing details of the speeches. Yathrib's pledges would introduce a new kind of protection—shared responsibility for defence and settlement within Arabia.

The institution of asylum mattered because it shifted the conditions of survival. Makkah's protection was commonly personal and tribal; in Aksum, the migrants depended upon a foreign sovereign who was not part of their kinship system. They did not have to become citizens of a modern pluralist state for the episode to show a real form of interreligious coexistence. Reports differ over the number of Aksum migrations, their dates and the identity of individual participants. The spiritual meaning of hijrah as leaving what distances one from God was developed in devotional interpretation, but the historical movement also involved parents, spouses, property and the uncertainty of return. Their experience prepares the later Hijrah without making the two migrations institutionally identical.

The long report from ʿĀʾishah through ʿUrwah and al-Zuhrī in al-Bukhārī 3905 also preserves a narrower Meccan episode: Abū Bakr received the protection of Ibn al-Daghinah, was initially permitted to worship privately, and then drew public attention by reciting in his courtyard. The attempted restriction on public recitation explains how an existing institution of *jiwār* could protect a person's body yet fail to protect the circulation of a new message. Women and children are described as listening, but no conversion figures follow from the report. The account records continuing allegiance and religious perseverance under a Christian ruler's protection. [Al-Bukhārī 3905](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3905).

### 11. From loss of protection to an alliance at al-ʿAqabah

**Matn**

The deaths of Khadījah and Abū Ṭālib deprived the Prophet of two different forms of support: intimate companionship and clan protection. His attempts to find a hearing beyond Makkah included a painful rejection at Ṭāʾif. He continued addressing visitors during the pilgrimage seasons. Among his listeners were people from Yathrib, a town whose contending groups had reason to seek a trustworthy guide. Their pledges gradually joined faith to the practical undertaking to protect his mission.

When the move became possible, the Prophet and Abū Bakr travelled under concealment, using a route and assistance chosen for safety. The route did not become holy through a disregard for physical danger; prudence regarding the desert, the use of a guide and the protection of vulnerable companions belonged to the same faithful action. Other emigrants left at different moments, often giving up property or familiar attachments. Prayer and reliance unfolded within these exact choices.

The Night Journey and Ascension belong to the spiritual depth of this period in Muslim remembrance. The journey brings the Messenger through signs and heavenly meetings; accounts of the ascent culminate in the gift of prayer. The servant returns to earthly life carrying an act by which bodies, hours and congregations will turn repeatedly toward God. The height of the spiritual journey becomes effective through an enduring duty fulfilled in the streets, homes and places of worship of ordinary believers.

Meanwhile the new allies acted through means available to them. Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr went to Yathrib to teach recitation and worship before the Prophet arrived. His instruction made repeated practices possible among people whose kinships and rivalries had not disappeared. The later pledge at al-ʿAqabah committed protectors to risks that an inward declaration alone could not discharge.

As the conditions of the Hijrah formed, spiritual disclosure and human prudence met in distinct acts: prayer and instruction, consultation and pledged security, travel and the protection of dependants. The prophetic return to the world appears through the exact demands of these responsibilities.

**Sharḥ**

Sīrah and maghāzī reports recount the rejection at Ṭāʾif and the subsequent pledges at al-ʿAqabah, although they vary in reported speeches and participants. The first pledge concerned belief and moral conduct; the second also committed Yathrib's supporters to protect the Prophet against hostile forces. A promise to avoid theft, unlawful sexual conduct or infanticide concerned communal moral commitment; a promise to defend the Prophet in Yathrib implicated the signatories' households, allies and military obligations. The instruction by Muṣʿab was crucial between the two because the new allegiance required neighbours able to pray and learn together before their prospective leader arrived.

The Night Journey and Ascension lead from the Prophet's heavenly encounters back to the lived obligations of prayer and communal care. Qurʾān 17:1 states the journey of God's servant; accounts such as [Muslim 162a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:162a) relate the heavenly encounters, Mūsā's counsel and the five daily prayers. The Qurʾānic passages concerning vision (53:1–18) and the reports transmitted by ʿĀʾishah and Ibn ʿAbbās require differentiated interpretation. The primary accounts establish revealed worship and narrate the ascent; their literary variations call for comparison.

The Miʿrāj gives the account of return its sharpest meaning. The Prophet ascends through the heavenly encounters and returns to a community facing bereavement, insecurity and difficult choices. In the transmitted prayer narrative, the heavenly gift becomes a recurrent earthly duty; it must be learned, led and performed by people with bodies, households and different capacities. Spiritual elevation therefore issues in service rather than permanent withdrawal from the world. An Akbarian interpretation can describe the perfected mediator as a barzakh: a human locus through which higher knowledge is received and then communicated in fitting created forms. This is a claim about the meaning of realized prophetic life, not a historical proof that every later political institution was divinely required. The migrants still need allies and safe routes; Muṣʿab, the emissary teaching Qurʾān in Yathrib, must still win particular listeners through instruction. The vertical and horizontal meet through action that respects both.

In Ibn ʿArabī's analysis of spiritual ascent, interpreted by James Morris through *Futūḥāt* chapter 367, the return to created life is a completion of realization. The prophetic servant resumes teaching, prayer and the right ordering of human relationships after an extraordinary experience of nearness. The five prayers bring each day under an orientation whose source exceeds the temporal order in which they are performed. Their bodily actions, congregation, repetition and reception by later generations give the spiritual event an enduring historical form.

Al-Qūnawī's analysis of prophetic correspondences and Farghānī's understanding of Muhammadan comprehensiveness offer further reflection on the many heavenly stations gathered in the Prophet's experience. A living person receives a distinctive divine commission and acts among particular people. The relations from the Miʿrāj to the pledges at al-ʿAqabah are explanatory: the highest knowledge returns to responsible action, while the actual pledges arise through people, agreements, instruction and protection in Yathrib. The spiritual and historical orders meet in the Prophet's life without supplying one another's missing historical evidence.

The second pledge committed Yathrib's supporters to protect the Prophet, extending their obligations beyond personal acceptance of the message. An instructed circle in Yathrib could receive the Prophet as a teacher, but a binding commitment to defend him entailed risks for its people and their allies. The supporters of Yathrib accepted the risk of armed conflict with Quraysh when they promised to protect the Prophet. Muṣʿab's period of instruction connects private adherence to public capability: people who had prayed together and learned the recitation could recognize a common cause when asked to make a demanding pledge. The Hijrah then gave that promise a real object, placing vulnerable newcomers and the Prophet himself under the hosts' protection.

Yathrib did not invite an unknown stranger into an empty political space. The Aws, Khazraj and Jewish groups had their own property, alliances and memories of violence. The Prophet's standing as a religious teacher and an outside arbiter could appeal to people seeking a settlement, but the motives of each participant cannot be reduced to a single desire for peace. The Hijrah also required courage, secrecy and trust under risk, themes central to later spiritual reading. The immediate historical consequence was more demanding: once the newcomers arrived, housing, food, disputes and protection could no longer be managed as private matters among a few Meccan believers. Those problems became urgent as soon as the emigrants arrived.

The stages of al-ʿAqabah matter because instruction preceded armed protection. Muṣʿab's work in Yathrib gave new believers common words and practices; the subsequent pledge imposed risks on households and allies whose conflicts long predated Islam. The accounts distinguish commitments to worship and moral conduct from the later undertaking to defend the Prophet against hostile forces.

## Book III — Madinah: how a religious fellowship became a public community

### 12. The Hijrah: neighbours, newcomers and a shared order

**Matn**

The emigrants reached Yathrib carrying faith and bonds of companionship but often lacking the property or kin protection that had sustained their lives in Makkah. Their hosts belonged to families and alliances whose own obligations persisted. Jewish households and groups possessed land, learning and separate ties. The Prophet established a mosque that became a place of worship, teaching, consultation and communal gathering.

The Aws and Khazraj, two major groups in Yathrib, retained memories of rivalry and warfare, while local Jewish groups entered the town's security arrangements through their own alliances and landholding. The new public association therefore included people with different previous obligations. Shared prayer could draw believers together; settling ransom or blood compensation still required named groups to recognize who would contribute and which procedures would bind them.

Hospitality required more than warm feeling. A guest needed shelter, means of earning and recognition among neighbours; a household offering help had dependants and limited resources of its own. New bonds of mutual assistance joined migrants to residents while inherited obligations of blood compensation, ransom and protection remained practical questions. These interests called for agreements that could bind different groups in a common public order.

The Medinan document remembered as the written agreement named an association while maintaining distinctions among its parties. Shared security required people to know who would defend whom, how liability would be allocated and where serious disputes could be brought. The fellowship now acquired public responsibilities that no single generous family could carry by itself.

The same spiritual allegiance worked through different scales. A believer had to restrain anger and give freely; a household had to provide fairly; a public association had to recognize and uphold claims even when people disagreed. Its unity depended on the ordered performance of those responsibilities by people of different capacities and circumstances.

**Sharḥ**

The key phrase in the Medinan document is the designation of a single community, *umma wāḥida*, alongside clauses retaining the emigrants and several groups of Yathrib within their existing arrangements of blood compensation and captive ransom. Ibn Hishām and Abū ʿUbayd preserve versions of the Medinan agreements; Michael Lecker compares their clauses, parties and transmission in *The Constitution of Medina* (2004, with later revisions). Saïd Amir Arjomand and other scholars argue over its composition and the distribution of its parts. The surviving recensions vary in clause sequence and participants, leaving the dates of individual provisions uncertain. The term “constitution” is convenient but potentially misleading: the transmitted text is a set of concrete obligations, not a modern theory of equal citizenship or centralized sovereignty. Participating groups assumed specific responsibilities for blood compensation, ransom of captives and contributions to common defence.

These provisions turned commitments of mutual aid into specified duties between groups that already possessed their own obligations. A believer can be generous toward a newcomer today but not tomorrow; an agreed rule about compensation and mutual aid makes some duties predictable and adjudicable. Neither law nor generosity replaces the other. Where kin groups retained responsibility for ransom and blood money, the agreement did not eliminate their internal authority; it directed their activity into a wider protected association. The Prophet was teaching worship while resolving issues that required enforceable cooperation. The Medinan community linked shared worship to concrete public duties of compensation, ransom, defence and adjudication.

The arrival in Yathrib brings moral generosity into contact with the requirements of public association. Welcoming a migrant is one act; determining the parties responsible for blood compensation, ransom, defence and dispute settlement is another. These duties are intelligible through the same concern for justice, but goodwill alone cannot specify their distribution. The Medinan agreement names groups already bound by different practices, and its clauses turn mutual recognition into publicly recognizable commitments. In the terms of practical philosophy, personal ethics concerns the disposition of a helper, household governance sustains actual dependants, and civic ordering distributes enforceable responsibilities between groups. Those later categories elucidate the different goods at stake; they are not words placed retrospectively in the mouths of the early participants. The unity of guidance is achieved through differentiated obligations and the political labour of making them effective.

Lecker's 2004 study and substantially updated 2024 edition warn against treating the transmitted *kitāb* as a modern constitution or assuming that every Jewish group in Madinah signed it. In the published text the emigrants and several Yathrib groups continue their customary blood-money and ransom responsibilities even as a wider *umma* is asserted. This combines continuity of local kin obligations with wider public coordination. The agreements retained named clan duties while placing them within wider obligations of mutual protection.

The social problem was acute. An emigrant had surrendered the protection and resources of his old household; an Anṣārī who helped him still had children, farms and obligations to relatives. Material generosity was therefore a test of allegiance, while the mosque made collective worship and teaching a regular practice. Qurʾān 59:8–9 describes the deprived emigrants and the hosts who preferred them despite their own need. The Qurʾān praises mutual assistance, while the Medinan agreements assign particular responsibilities to participating clans and allied groups. The Prophet's leadership united teaching with adjudication, but its historical forms remained responsive to local conditions. Public judgment increasingly concerned family obligations, property and markets, matters no less important to the community's development than warfare. The inner and outer are linked here in a demonstrable way: a disposition toward generosity mattered because real people needed food and shelter, while public obligations made mutual assistance more reliable than generosity alone.

The document's wording imposes a useful restraint on our terminology. A phrase designating a single community stands next to recurrent clauses retaining named clans' earlier compensation obligations. The legal relationship is one of enlarged coordination, not a documented abolition of kin groups. Equally, the Jewish clauses cannot be read as proof that every Jewish group of the oasis had accepted precisely the same terms. Lecker's study argues for narrower participation and separately negotiated arrangements; the recensions require collation before any one clause can be dated with certainty. Existing clans retained their identities but now shared responsibilities for defence, compensation and judgments that crossed earlier boundaries. [Ibn Hishām's text](https://islamweb.net/ar/library/content/58/584/); [Lecker's study](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-constitution-of-medina-muhammads-first-legal-document-2/).

### 13. Worship takes shape in the routines of a town

**Matn**

In Madinah the day acquired a shared rhythm through congregational prayer, and the year through fasting and almsgiving. Families sought guidance about marriage, children and inheritance; merchants asked about deferred payments and truthful transactions. The same persons worshipped, kept houses, traded and settled quarrels. Each obligation had its own form, and a sound life required the capacity to discern how they fitted together in the actual circumstances of a town.

The reports also remember Māʿiz, a man who repeatedly confessed unlawful intercourse, and describe the Prophet questioning the circumstances rather than treating accusation as sufficient evidence. The case involving the Ghāmidī woman raises additional questions because her infant's dependence could not be ignored while public judgment was pursued. These reports require different levels of attention: what was reliably transmitted, what legal rule the conduct establishes, and what kind of responsibility is demanded of those who judge or speak about a penitent person.

During one congregational prayer the Prophet heard a child crying. Knowing the distress of the mother, he shortened the prayer he had intended to prolong. The act preserved worship while attending to a dependency present in the congregation. He also carried his young granddaughter Umāmah during prayer, setting her down and lifting her as its movements required. In these encounters mercy was exercised through exact bodily judgments rather than by abandoning the rite.

A more difficult series of reports concerns a woman from Ghāmid who confessed unlawful intercourse and sought purification. Her pregnancy and the needs of the infant required decisions over time before the public penalty recorded in the narrations was carried out. The woman's repentance, the child's dependence, the requirements of legal proof and the community's treatment of her were different claims within one painful situation. Their relation could be discerned only by attending to the actual persons and the rule governing public judgment.

The practical inheritance of such acts later required distinct competences: accurate transmission of reports, juristic interpretation, the moral formation of those exercising authority and the continuing care of households. The unity of guidance became visible when each person received what was rightly due within the possibilities of the situation.

**Sharḥ**

Medinan rulings developed at different times; tafsīr, ḥadīth and Sīrah preserve evidence for their sequence, though not every date is secure. Qurʾān 2:142–150 concerns the direction of prayer; 2:183–187 fasting; 4:7–12 inheritance; 2:282 debts; 4:35 familial arbitration. These passages have different occasions and literary forms. Not every later fiqh rule can be assigned directly to an identifiable day in Madinah. What can be shown is how the community's economic and domestic life continually generated questions for religious judgment. Daily prayer and fasting structured believers' time and repeatedly demanded attention, restraint and remembrance, although individual conduct still varied.

A marriage rule, for example, joins several orders: the parties' intentions, their consent and responsibilities, the property consequences of a union, the obligations of kin and the community's means of resolving disputes. Likewise, a rule about debts depends on trust, recording, witnesses and a procedure if someone defaults. Worship and commerce intersect because the same person must judge how to act in both, not because fasting automatically settles an unpaid loan. This helps later usūl al-fiqh make sense. The jurist's task is to draw from transmitted guidance the rules applicable to cases that recur in changing social forms, while the spiritual teacher asks what dispositions enable those rules to be lived rightly.

The crying child gives a precise analysis of discernment within worship. The Prophet enters prayer intending to prolong it, hears a child's cries, knows the distress this causes the mother, and shortens the prayer (al-Bukhārī 709). No opposition between prayer and mercy needs to be imagined. The end of worship remains, while one permissible feature of its performance—duration—is determined in attention to this congregation. Later practical philosophy can distinguish the leader's personal excellence, the mother's and child's dependent relationship, and the congregation's shared rite. These are three aspects of the same act, not three rules mechanically added together. Fiqh properly asks which shortening preserves the rite and what an imam may do; ethical formation asks how he notices a burden that power might otherwise overlook; spiritual interpretation asks how worship of God makes room for the creature's rightful claim.

The woman of Ghāmid supplies a harder instance, because the legal, domestic and spiritual aspects do not converge in an easy outcome. She came from the Ghāmid branch of the Azd to confess unlawful intercourse. In the report preserved by Muslim (1695a), the Prophet first directed her to seek God's forgiveness and return in repentance; her pregnancy then brought a delay, and her infant's need for nursing had to be provided for before the prescribed penalty was carried out. Related narrations vary in the details of the child's care and the sequence. The law concerns proof, confession and public jurisdiction; the infant has a claim independent of the mother's offence; the woman's repentance has a reality not exhausted by the legal disposition; the community's manner of speaking about her also becomes morally answerable. The case does not license the inference that compassion simply cancels legal norms. It demonstrates why the true application of a norm demands attention to all the persons and rights implicated. Later hadith transmission, jurisprudence and spiritual instruction preserve distinguishable intelligibilities within this difficult historical whole.

The mother and crying child offer a precise example of an integrated prophetic act. In [al-Bukhārī 709](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:709), the Prophet ﷺ intends a longer prayer and shortens it when he hears the child and knows the mother's distress. The rite remains worship, while its permissible duration is governed by attention to an actual participant. A jurist studies the validity and conditions of the shortened prayer; a teacher studies the attentive exercise of authority; a spiritual interpreter considers how the heart apprehends the rightful claims of God and other persons in one action. Each is a genuine understanding of the event within its own field.

[Al-Bukhārī 516](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:516) reports the carrying of Umāmah during prayer; [al-Bukhārī 6129](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:6129) recounts the conversation with Abū ʿUmayr; [al-Bukhārī 676](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:676) records service within the household. Their significance comes from the very particular relationships involved. A dependent child, a grandmother or parent, a congregation and household members make different claims upon a person. Such claims become known in actual encounters and can be answered through a mature character without the formal boundaries of worship becoming arbitrary.

In [Muslim 1695](https://sunnah.com/muslim:1695), the narrations of Māʿiz and the woman of Ghāmid concern confessions of adultery. The Prophet's questioning, the woman's pregnancy, care for her child, severe punishment, repentance and the community's treatment of those punished occupy distinct parts of the transmitted reports. The jurist requires the conditions of proof and public jurisdiction. The moral and spiritual interpreter considers responsibility, repentance, dependence and how others respond. The original action unites these features in a real historical situation; its later legal classification preserves a necessary part of the guidance, while the living completeness of the prophetic presence is apprehended through the wider report and spiritual reflection.

This range of encounters supplies a generative relation to the later sciences. Hadith criticism preserves reliable accounts of what was seen and heard; fiqh derives and applies rulings; *adab* attends to conduct within relationship; *tazkiya* cultivates attention, intention and virtue. As the community expands, these capacities become teachable through families, schools, judges and companions. Their formal differentiation has its proper usefulness, and their integration requires people who relate what they know to the concrete persons they serve.

A marriage dispute or unpaid debt could bring households with competing loyalties before a shared authority whose judgment affected both parties. Qurʾān 2:282 articulates recording and witnessing for deferred debt, while 4:35 addresses arbitration in marital discord. Those passages do not establish that every Medinan transaction followed a uniform later fiqh textbook. They reveal why new public obligations required habitual instruction, practical judgment and means of recording claims.

A later example helps explain the system without retrojecting its terminology. Al-Ghazālī organized the *Iḥyāʾ* into worship, customary dealings, destructive traits and saving virtues. He could do so because ritual acts, everyday conduct and the condition of the heart had long been treated as related objects of religious instruction, even though his precise psychological scheme was eleventh-century. The Prophet's household reports and practical judgments are thus important historical evidence in addition to public campaigns. Conflict with Quraysh soon made the same problems of property, trust, discipline and judgment matters of military survival.

### 14. Badr: conflict tests allegiance and public judgment

**Matn**

The departure from Makkah had not ended the struggle with Quraysh. Caravans, alliances and the property lost by emigrants remained sources of conflict. Near Badr in the second year after the Hijrah, a Muslim force and a larger Meccan army met in a battle whose outcome reshaped the community's position among its neighbours. Victory brought danger and grief as well as captives, property and new expectations of public leadership.

Those present faced different demands. A fighter had to remain steady amid fear, a commander had to coordinate action and a person holding a captive had to recognize that an enemy remained a human being whose treatment now came under public judgment. Disputes over spoils brought private desires before an authority responsible for the whole group. The Qurʾānic address linked the outcome of the battle to gratitude, discipline and the just ordering of acquired goods.

The new community could now enforce some decisions that individuals could not have carried out alone. That capacity increased its responsibility. Rules about captivity, ransom and distribution required evidence and deliberation, while resentment, honour and material need continued to shape the parties' choices.

Badr marks an expansion of the field in which moral character had to operate. Courage, mercy, fairness and obedience acquired a common form through decisions about actual combatants, kin and resources. The means of effective public action had grown, and so had the obligations attached to them.

**Sharḥ**

The Qurʾān discusses Badr in passages including 3:123–127 and Sūrat al-Anfāl, which addresses fear, victory, captives, spoils and cohesion. Detailed troop movements, numbers and dialogues belong mainly to the later maghāzī tradition; they require comparison of versions, especially Ibn Isḥāq's material and relevant hadith. A battle's broad occurrence does not license treating each remembered speech as independently verified. Badr's importance as an institution-forming event is clearer when we ask who now bore responsibilities that had previously been limited to kin groups: commanders coordinated armed men from several backgrounds; the community faced captives and property disputes; alliances elsewhere in Arabia were affected by the perceived result.

Captivity offers a sharp case of institutional change. Before a public community could hold prisoners, conflicts of capture or ransom might be managed primarily through family or tribal retaliation. After Badr, the Prophet and the believers confronted prisoners from powerful Meccan households, whose treatment affected diplomacy and future exchange. Spoils raised related disputes among fighters who had taken different risks. These were not abstract problems added by later jurists: the community was learning to assign claims when common military action produced consequences beyond any one family's control. Later law systematized matters already encountered here, while the Qurʾānic address contested pride and entitlement arising from victory.

After Badr, victory did not dissolve competing claims: fighters disputed spoils, captives possessed lives and kin ties, and the new association had to define the reach of its authority. The Qurʾānic treatment of the event brings obedience, intention and distributive judgment into a single crisis. Each deserves its own analysis. Courage and restraint concern the combatant's character; the disposition of booty concerns a public entitlement; the fate of captives concerns persons who cannot be reduced to the victors' desires. The prophetic act is comprehensive because these differences are practically governed together, not because a single sentiment can decide them all. As later jurisprudence isolates rules of conflict and revenue, the circumstances, aims and evidentiary limits of individual reports remain essential. The public capacity to enforce a decision also depends on actual resources and trust among fighters.

In Qurʾān 8 the aftermath of Badr is not celebrated merely as successful fighting. Disputes over spoils and the treatment of captives bring human desires into the immediate interpretation of events. That permits one to connect personal formation to public judgment without claiming that believers were uniformly free of rivalry. The Qurʾānic passages address fear, victory, captives and spoils; later maghāzī reports supply fuller speeches and troop counts whose transmission differs.

Victory confronted the fighters with decisions about spoils and captives, testing their restraint, loyalties and judgment. The participants faced fear, conflicting expectations and the temptation to read success as personal entitlement. Qurʾān 8 addresses disputes over spoils and requires obedience and collective responsibility after victory. Ibn Khaldūn's later analysis of solidarity offers one angle on how a threatened group could act collectively; revelation, shared worship and the Prophet's authority provide the actors' own grounds of commitment. Social solidarity can explain mobilization without exhausting religious conviction. Uḥud will demonstrate that collective confidence does not remove the need for disciplined judgment.

### 15. Uḥud and the Trench: discipline, siege and divided loyalties

**Matn**

Quraysh returned to confront the Muslims near Uḥud. The Prophet assigned archers to a position intended to protect the army; when some left after an apparent turn toward victory, the situation reversed and the Prophet himself was wounded. The event exposed a difference between courage in an encounter and discipline within a coordinated command. A momentary gain could not be assessed apart from the duty to maintain a position that safeguarded others.

The killing of Ḥamzah, the Prophet's uncle, at Uḥud deepened the loss felt by his family and companions. The later siege also involved disputes about the conduct of Banū Qurayẓah, a Jewish group in Madinah, after an alliance was said to have been broken. The accounts of negotiations, judgment and punishment require careful assessment of their transmitted details. They belong to the practical difficulties of defence and loyalty, not simply to an abstract contrast between faith and unbelief.

At the later siege of Madinah, the defenders dug a trench and endured hunger, fear and long hours of vigilance. Families and fighters faced the strain of a threatened town while alliances around them were tested. Defence required knowledge of terrain, labour with tools, shared provisions and trustworthy command. Trust in God animated their perseverance as these created means were put to use.

The two campaigns brought out several capacities that had to act together. A soldier's intention could be sincere while his tactical judgment remained mistaken. Skill could secure a defensive line while fear or rivalry weakened cooperation. The rightful act demanded courage shaped by practical knowledge, attention to duty and a willingness to remain responsible for others.

The struggle thus formed habits whose effects reached beyond battle. A community capable of teaching, protecting households and governing shared resources depended upon people who could keep commitments under pressure, accept correction and learn from loss.

**Sharḥ**

Qurʾān 3:121–175 engages the events around Uḥud, and 33:9–27 addresses the Trench and its aftermath. These texts do not supply every detail or number in later historical accounts. Ibn Isḥāq, al-Wāqidī and later writers preserve differing materials whose relation needs criticism. The transmitted judgment of Banū Qurayẓah appears in Sunni historical and legal accounts; reports differ over particular speeches and numbers. It is unnecessary to transform this book into a monograph on the controversy, but equally unsound to present every later number as established observation.

At Uḥud some archers abandoned their positions; at the Trench the defenders maintained collective labour and vigilance during a prolonged siege. Uḥud tested whether fighters under immediate pressure would remain at assigned positions; the Trench demanded the patience of households over a sustained threat, with tasks that brought no instant victory or visible booty. Failure could arise through individual departure, exhausted supplies or divided allies. The same outward obedience therefore took different forms in battle and siege. The siege demanded vigilance, patience and restraint from its defenders; later spiritual authors examined those qualities in settings of moral discipline.

Uḥud and the Trench show why interior sincerity cannot replace learned technique. The archers' assigned position at Uḥud required obedience to a plan; apparent advantage created a temptation to leave it. The trench later demanded physical labour, vigilance and cooperation under fear. Both episodes bring courage, trust, skill and command into one practical order, but each has its own object. A spiritual account of steadfastness explains what it means to keep faith under pressure; the historical account must also examine terrain, supplies, the command's intelligibility and the consequences of departure from a post. The human excellence at stake is not a sum of isolated virtues: prudence orders courage and technical ability toward a common duty. The Qurʾānic addresses illuminate moral responsibility while leaving military particulars to evidence of the campaigns.

At Uḥud some archers abandoned their tactical assignment after an apparent advantage; at the Trench the defenders sustained collective labour and defence under scarcity and fear. The Banū Qurayẓah reports raise a third, distinct problem of alleged wartime breach and post-siege judgment. The later narratives of Banū Qurayẓah differ in reported numbers and speeches; those details have different levels of evidentiary support.

The two campaigns illustrate distinct social pressures. Uḥud exposes limits of discipline inside the fighting force; the Trench demanded coordination among households over time, with food, digging, sentries and readiness depending upon more than battlefield bravery. Military loyalty required repeated obedience to assigned positions, cooperation over supplies and dependable performance under danger. Later spiritual authors examined vigilance and intention as moral disciplines; their arguments do not establish why particular archers abandoned their positions at Uḥud. The changing regional balance after the siege created conditions in which negotiation became possible. The next decisive encounter with Quraysh ended in negotiation and a treaty at al-Ḥudaybiyyah.

### 16. Al-Ḥudaybiyyah: an agreement that changed the terms of conflict

**Matn**

In the sixth year after the Hijrah the Prophet led companions toward the sanctuary at Makkah intending pilgrimage. Quraysh prevented entry, and both parties negotiated outside the city. A settlement promised a truce and postponed the pilgrims' entry until a later year. Some terms distressed the companions, especially a provision concerning persons coming from Makkah to the Muslims.

During the negotiations Abū Jandal appeared in restraints. He was the Muslim son of Suhayl ibn ʿAmr, the Quraysh representative concluding the terms, and sought release from his father and fellow Meccans. The sight of his suffering made a public undertaking immediately painful. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb questioned the agreement and sought reassurance from the Prophet. Their exchange revealed the difficulty of judging a future peace while a vulnerable person stood before those who would be bound by its terms.

After the agreement, the companions were slow to perform the rites ending the interrupted pilgrimage. Umm Salamah, the Prophet's wife who had accompanied the journey, advised him to act first. He offered his sacrifice and cut his hair; the companions followed. Here public judgment, a wife's wise counsel, the bodily form of a rite and the community's distressed loyalty entered one connected occasion.

The truce changed relations between Makkah and Madinah through enforceable promises that required patience on both sides. Compassion for Abū Jandal remained real alongside the duty of fidelity to an agreement. The Prophet's conduct ordered several goods without making one erase the others, and embodied instruction accomplished what repeated words had not secured.

**Sharḥ**

The extended treaty account in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Book of Conditions, nos. 2731–2732, can be compared with the concise report, nos. 2711–2712, and with Ibn Hishām's Sīrah. Suhayl ibn ʿAmr, Quraysh's negotiator and Abū Jandal's father, disputed the treaty wording as his son's arrival tested the return clause. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, a leading Companion, questioned the terms; Umm Salamah, the Prophet's wife, advised him to begin the concluding rites. The treaty versions differ in wording, particularly in the scope of the clause concerning the return of fugitives. Qurʾān 48:1–29, especially its use of *fatḥ*, requires tafsīr: victory is not identified simply with immediate military conquest. The traditional reports also associate the pledge beneath the tree with the negotiations (48:18).

The return clause poses a useful limit on easy claims that religious commitment automatically yielded political harmony. The companions' reported distress was intelligible: a person who had escaped persecution might be handed back to his former protectors. The agreement therefore bound the community to enforce a decision some members considered damaging. A treaty has political force precisely when it constrains conduct under adverse circumstances. Once concluded, it also created expectations of reciprocity, and the tribal alliance clauses meant that injuries to allies could carry wider consequences. The later breach is unintelligible unless these obligations are understood. The parties accepted a binding truce despite serious misgivings; honouring its terms required restraint and altered the political relations between Makkah and Madinah.

Abū Jandal was the Muslim son of Suhayl ibn ʿAmr, the Meccan negotiator who represented Quraysh at al-Ḥudaybiyyah. He appeared during the negotiations seeking release from Meccan restraint, just as the parties were settling a clause requiring return of certain fugitives. His situation made the cost of agreement immediate and personal. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, one of the Prophet's closest companions, pressed questions about accepting terms that seemed humiliating. Umm Salamah, the Prophet's wife who accompanied the expedition, later advised him to enact the sacrifice and shaving associated with ending the interrupted pilgrimage so that the companions could follow. The connected report in al-Bukhārī 2731–2732 places vulnerable persons, a covenant, collective distress and ritual action in one sequence.

A comprehensive judgment here is neither a choice of abstract political advantage over compassion nor an indulgence that dissolves a promise. A treaty creates reciprocal obligations whose effects cannot be evaluated through the immediate suffering of one person alone; the suffering nevertheless remains real and morally salient. Exemplary action then communicates what an additional command might fail to achieve. Jurists can investigate the authority to conclude truces and the interpretation of their clauses; ethical instruction concerns patience and counsel; political history examines whether the agreement changed actual alliances and security. The unity of the act lies in a practical wisdom ordering these distinct claims, not in an institution automatically deduced from the Muhammadan Reality.

Bukhārī 2731–32 gives a connected sequence: blocked access to the sanctuary, negotiations, the return stipulation, Abū Jandal's appearance, ʿUmar's questions and Umm Salamah's counsel. The treaty demanded compliance with terms that many participants disliked, including the postponement of pilgrimage and the return clause. The wider causal mechanism is specific: a truce enabled contact and acknowledged alliances, while a later attack on an ally could become a treaty breach with broader consequences.

The mechanism of change was practical. A truce reduced the immediate cost of travel and contact, while recognized alliances expanded the obligations of both parties to third groups. Treaties create openings only when their rules affect people's choices: traders travel, emissaries speak, families visit, tribes negotiate. The unusual spiritual test lay in restraint in the face of humiliation, illustrated by Abū Jandal's situation and some companions' reaction. The accounts preserve objections to the treaty, showing that its terms did not appear advantageous to all participants. Later Muslim interpretation saw the outcome as a victory; the historical argument identifies the specific restraints and alliances that made new outcomes possible. Khaybar and the deferred pilgrimage would soon test the new freedom of action, since peace with Quraysh did not end all conflict in Arabia.

The transmitted episode links institutional restraint to actual human reactions. In al-Bukhārī 2731–2732, the arrival of Abū Jandal makes the return clause immediately painful; ʿUmar questions the decision; after negotiation the companions hesitate to sacrifice and shave. Umm Salamah's counsel changes how the order is carried out: the Prophet acts first, and the group follows. The movement from negotiated words to embodied compliance is part of the event's mechanism. The report records the participants' immediate difficulty in accepting the terms; it does not supply reliable figures for subsequent conversions. [Al-Bukhārī 2731–2732](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2731).

### 17. Khaybar, the deferred pilgrimage and changing relations beyond Madinah

**Matn**

The settlement at al-Ḥudaybiyyah gave the Muslim community room to act beyond the immediate confrontation with Quraysh. At Khaybar, fortified settlements came under Muslim control, while cultivators continued to work the land through an arrangement concerning its yield. Political victory did not itself produce grain. Harvest still required the experience of farmers, the maintenance of productive fields and rules that parties could recognize over time.

The property acquired through Khaybar also entered a different history. ʿUmar later asked the Prophet what to do with a valuable holding there and received guidance to retain its substance while directing its benefits toward others. The endowment became a precedent for later legal reflection, even though its purpose and terms must be distinguished from the agricultural agreements governing Khaybar's cultivation. A single locality thus appears in several distinct bodies of later law.

The inhabitants of Khaybar and the new authorities therefore met in a continuing relation of labour, ownership and risk. Their claims concerned the division of produce, protection of households and the means of settling disagreement. A later generation of jurists would distinguish among cultivation contracts, public land and proprietary rights, drawing on reports of arrangements that had their own histories.

Meanwhile the pilgrims returned to the sanctuary at the time permitted by the treaty. Their access depended on public promises honoured by the parties; their rites required the bodily acts learned from the Prophet. A deferred desire became a disciplined act of fidelity when the promised occasion arrived.

These two episodes join different forms of justice. Agricultural cooperation relates persons through land and recurring harvests, while a truce relates parties through time-bound covenants. In each, worshipful intention becomes effective through the precise means and rights that the historical situation requires.

**Sharḥ**

The Khaybar traditions are distributed across hadith, maghāzī and legal sources; Bukhārī's chapters on cultivation and the reports about the Prophet's dealings with Khaybar's inhabitants are important. Reports differ about the settlement's subsequent administration, and later jurists derived rulings on land and crop-sharing from the Khaybar precedent. Khaybar's agricultural settlement preserved cultivation while transferring control over land revenues. Agricultural land cannot be treated as a bag of captured goods: cultivation requires labour, access to water, seasonal effort and some expectation about shares. Continuing arrangements therefore put the new authorities in relation to people who did not all become part of the same religious household.

The agricultural dimension also gives the later Islamic law of landholding a historical problem to solve. Crop-sharing is a continuing relationship, not a one-time transaction: access to fields, division of produce and the labour of cultivators must be sustained season after season. New authority could enforce an arrangement while relying upon the knowledge of existing cultivators. The deferred pilgrimage instead tested whether Quraysh and the Muslims would honour the agreed truce and access arrangements. Placing the episodes together is useful only if their distinct effects are explained: one enlarged the community's fiscal responsibilities, the other demonstrated the public credibility of negotiated commitments.

Khaybar and the deferred pilgrimage place two different forms of trust alongside one another. Agricultural production requires continuing cooperation between those controlling land and those cultivating it; a pilgrimage agreement requires parties to honour a promise about movement and sacred access at a specified time. Both involve law, but their causes and possible failures differ. A worker's share of produce, the terms of continued cultivation and the settlement of disputes require institutional description; a traveller's willingness to turn back requires patience and a reliable public undertaking. In the broader account, created goods carry responsibilities precisely because persons depend upon them. Later jurists may derive distinct bodies of law concerning cultivation, ownership and covenants without fragmenting the spiritual purpose of just dealing. The historian must not conflate the several Khaybar arrangements, the ʿumrah terms or their changing dates.

A tangible contrast helps the two episodes belong together. The deferred ʿumrah tested whether public promises about access to the sanctuary would be honoured; the Khaybar agreement concerned agricultural labour over repeated harvests. Bukhārī 2329 transmits Ibn ʿUmar's report of an arrangement for half the produce of fruit or crops, with later provisions for the Prophet's wives also narrated. The reported initial agricultural settlement and later adjustments belonged to distinct stages of Khaybar's administration. Khaybar involved continued cultivation under an agricultural arrangement; the deferred pilgrimage depended on a negotiated agreement with Quraysh.

The deferred pilgrimage under the treaty also had a distinct function. It was an embodied return to a sanctuary that had shaped Meccan worship and identity, now undertaken by a community whose collective commitments had changed. Religiously it confirmed the continuity of the Kaʿbah with Abrahamic worship; politically it showed that an agreement could restrain old enemies while allowing public rites. Later narratives sometimes run treaty, Khaybar and pilgrimage together, but their institutional consequences should remain distinguishable. The linked expansion of alliance and obligation made the eventual breach between Quraysh-associated and Muslim-associated tribes especially consequential.

The reported agreement allowed local cultivators to work the land in return for a stipulated share of its produce. Ibn ʿUmar's report in al-Bukhārī 2331 and 4248 says that Khaybar's Jewish cultivators were to work the land for half of its produce. The two versions pass through the same Ibn ʿUmar–Nāfiʿ line, so their agreement is not independent documentary corroboration. This nevertheless grounds an explanation of why victory led immediately to questions of labour, possession, revenue and ongoing obligations. The record does not define the land-tenure status of every household or settle later juristic disputes about agricultural contracts. [Al-Bukhārī 2331](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2331); [4248](https://sunnah.com/bukhari/64/283).

### 18. The return to Makkah and the incorporation of Arabia

**Matn**

When allied groups broke the arrangements surrounding al-Ḥudaybiyyah, the Prophet led a large force toward Makkah. The city came under Muslim control with limited organized resistance. Idols were removed from the Kaʿbah, and the sanctuary became a centre for worship of the one God. The change was political and religious, yet the lives of those who had opposed the new community remained before its leaders as personal claims requiring judgment.

At Ḥunayn, soon after the return to Makkah, the newly enlarged Muslim force experienced danger despite its numbers. The encounter tested whether growth in outward power was accompanied by discipline, patience and truthful reliance. Envoys and teachers later carried guidance to communities whose needs had to be learned locally. The political integration of Arabia proceeded through these acts of instruction, assessment and correction, as well as through military submission.

The accounts describe restraint, reconciliation and the ordering of new relationships among former adversaries. Their detailed speeches vary in the later transmissions. What the people of Makkah could now expect from public power depended on concrete decisions about protection, property and the conduct of those who entered the city. Trust could grow through acts of fairness over time even where allegiance had previously been compelled by circumstance or fear.

The subsequent campaigns and delegations brought more Arabian groups into relation with Madinah. A distant household required instruction in prayer and obligations, a governor required authority to hear disputes, and a collector required rules governing what could be demanded. None of these tasks was accomplished simply by the conquest of a city.

The Prophet's mission continued in this differentiated labour: fidelity to the sanctuary, restraint toward opponents, the teaching of new believers and the establishment of effective obligations. The wider fellowship acquired form through the persons entrusted with carrying particular aspects of its life.

**Sharḥ**

The conquest is discussed in Qurʾān 110 and in hadith and Sīrah accounts; the Ḥunayn reversals in 9:25–27. The exact numbers, speeches and scope of amnesty are not all equally secure. The principal Muslim accounts describe Muḥammad's entry into Makkah and removal of idols; reports about individual exceptions differ and require separate evaluation. The Meccan sanctuary was not discarded: it was redefined within the worship taught by the Prophet. That mattered because pilgrimage, kin memory and the practical gathering of tribes were already rooted in it.

Integration also depended on the limits of central presence. A new Muslim household in a distant region could not consult the Prophet about every prayer, marriage or commercial dispute. Envoys and local teachers therefore had to communicate a core of practice while learning what questions the community actually faced. Tax collection brought another kind of encounter: people had to know the amount, purpose and authority of payment, and collectors had to be trusted not to abuse their power. Religious identity could expand rapidly after military victory, but the competence to live that identity required sustained instruction. After the Farewell Pilgrimage, the Companions carried the Prophet's instructions into communities that had not witnessed his conduct directly.

Entry into Makkah changes an outward political relation, while the transformation of persons and households remains a further task. Restraint toward former opponents, removal of idols from the sanctuary, and delegation of teachers or collectors involve different competences. Amnesty cannot be treated as a slogan that fixes the wording or scope of every individual decision; reports require comparison. A comprehensive prophetic practice can protect life, restore worship and establish public responsibility at once, but maintaining these goods after his departure from a town requires persons trained to teach, judge and collect resources. Here spiritual sincerity becomes historically consequential through specified acts and delegated offices. The new public order should be assessed through what its agents actually did, not through the assumption that political incorporation immediately produced uniform religious formation.

Incorporation is not an event completed by the removal of idols. After entry into Makkah, teachers, delegates and collectors had to make ritual competence and recurrent payments possible in regions distant from the Prophet. Newly allied groups could acknowledge the Prophet's authority before teachers and revenue agents established regular religious practice; formal allegiance alone did not establish inward conviction. The exact accounts of amnesty, individual exceptions and numbers of troops require report-level comparison.

Once many groups entered new relations, instructions required carriers. An envoy could convey belief and prayer, but regular contributions and disputes called for local collectors, teachers and judgments. The Prophet's dealings with delegations thus show the beginnings of a transmission problem: guidance could not depend indefinitely upon every person travelling to Madinah. Religious language, social custom and economic arrangements varied across Arabia. The Farewell Pilgrimage would give a public and widely witnessed example of worship while drawing attention to common obligations; the Prophet's subsequent death would make questions of transmission and authority unavoidable.

### 19. The Farewell Pilgrimage and the responsibilities that survived the Prophet

**Matn**

The Prophet performed his final pilgrimage among believers who had come from many parts of Arabia. He enacted the rites at the sanctuary and addressed duties concerning worship, property, family relationships and the protection of human life. The gathering was a public occasion in which bodily practice, spoken teaching and the claims of a large community were brought together.

The Companions transmitted the final pilgrimage from different positions of observation. Jābir's remembered account gave later Muslims a detailed sequence of rites, while other witnesses preserved words and acts from different moments. These testimonies formed part of the practical inheritance through which those not present could perform a pilgrimage recognizably connected to the Prophet's example. Their differences called for careful interpretation rather than an assumption that every witness narrated precisely the same portion of the event.

Different companions saw different moments, remembered particular instructions and later answered questions from those who had not been present. A report about a ritual movement preserved one aspect of the event; another transmitted words concerning rights and responsibilities. Their combined testimony allowed later believers to learn an action they could no longer witness directly.

The pilgrimage also brought old relationships into a renewed moral measure. A person who returned home had to worship faithfully and treat a spouse, neighbour or trading partner with justice. No single memorized formula could perform those obligations on his behalf. Repeated action and corrected understanding gradually made them parts of a formed life.

With the Prophet's death approaching, the community would soon lose direct access to the living source of its example. Its continuation required faithful remembrance, competent interpretation and the exercise of public responsibilities by different people. The unity of the prophetic vocation now had to be inherited through actual acts, sciences and institutions.

**Sharḥ**

The pilgrimage's actions are preserved through differing Companions, notably the extended report of Jābir in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Book of Pilgrimage, no. 1218 in common numbering. Qurʾān 5:3 is linked in the received tradition to the completion of religion, while legal details arise through multiple reports and juristic comparisons. Versions of the Farewell Sermon differ in wording, while repeatedly addressing the sanctity of life and property, inherited abuses and obligations between members of the community. The historical significance of embodied instruction is strong: when an act is publicly demonstrated before witnesses, its later transmission can draw upon what people saw as well as what they heard. But witnesses differ in attention and memory, explaining why later scholars examined reports and reconciled apparent discrepancies.

After the Prophet's death, legal adjudication, report transmission and spiritual instruction increasingly developed through different scholars and institutions. What did the Prophet teach or do? Who witnessed it? Which of those actions binds others, and under what conditions? Who has authority to judge a dispute now? What dispositions make the instruction inwardly fruitful? Hadith criticism addresses the reliability of transmission; jurisprudence its normative application; political theory the government of the community; spiritual instruction its realization in character. In the Prophet's lifetime these functions met in a single authoritative person, though many companions already served as teachers or agents. His death therefore did not create knowledge out of nothing, but changed how different kinds of authority were accessed and coordinated.

The Farewell Pilgrimage brings together an enacted ritual and a public account of obligations. A believer sees how a rite is performed, hears instructions concerning rights, and returns to a household or district in which those acts must be taught. Different companions preserved different details because they occupied different places in the event and later answered different questions. Their reports transmit the practice through multiple, partially overlapping lines of witness. This is an especially clear case of differentiated inheritance: the ritual specialist asks how to perform an action validly; the jurist determines its obligatory and recommended elements; the moral teacher concerns himself with the rights and dispositions stated alongside the rite. The prophetic form is lived as a unity in the historical event, while later communities must coordinate several kinds of knowledge to preserve its intelligibility.

The Prophet's death made an analytical distinction unavoidable. His authority included receiving revelation, teaching its meaning, judging disputes, leading worship and directing communal affairs. The end of prophecy did not end any of the community's practical needs, yet the functions could not all be inherited in the same manner. Sunni accounts emphasize the formation of the caliphate and the wider transmission of knowledge by the Companions; Imāmī accounts place particular interpretive authority in the divinely designated Imams. Sunni and Shiʿi accounts of the Prophet's final instructions later informed distinct understandings of political leadership and religious authority. Al-Ghazālī, Shādhilī authors and Akbarian thinkers later explored prophetic inheritance as the formation of character or realization of spiritual wisdom. These are serious retrospective interpretations, not evidence that the first assembly at Saqīfah already used their technical distinctions. The demands of leadership and expansion made these distinctions urgent long before jurists and spiritual authors gave them their later technical forms.

The Farewell Pilgrimage provides a public model of worship, but a great deal of the Sunnah reached later generations through domestic life and instruction outside large gatherings. Reports through ʿĀʾishah, Umm Salamah and others matter particularly for private conduct and interpretation of what the Prophet intended. Later scholars' distinctions between common practice, judicial rulings and prophetic particularities are analytical responses to the differing settings of those reports; they are not a ready-made classification uttered at the final pilgrimage.

The later question of succession arises because different functions performed by the Prophet could be transmitted in different ways without implying that revelation continued. A transmitted report could preserve his practice; a judge could apply it; a teacher could cultivate virtues exemplified in it; political leadership required a decision about public allegiance and coercive power. Sunni and Shiʿi traditions developed different doctrines of legitimate succession, while later spiritual authors elaborated additional accounts of prophetic inheritance. Ibn ʿArabī, Qūnawī and Farghānī developed distinct technical accounts of *walāyah* through their own arguments and writings.

## Book IV — Succession, conquest and imperial government

### 20. After the Prophet: allegiance and the survival of obligations

**Matn**

The Prophet's death left the community with obligations that had become effective through his personal judgment and authority. His companions could no longer ask him to appoint a commander, settle a claim or decide a new dispute. The Anṣār gathered at the Saqīfah of Banū Sāʿidah to discuss leadership; Abū Bakr and other leading emigrants entered the deliberation, and he received allegiance. Members of the Prophet's household had not participated in the initial meeting. The circumstances and meaning of the succession remained subjects of profound disagreement.

The relationship of ʿAlī, the Prophet's cousin and son-in-law, and Fāṭimah, his daughter, to the initial settlement remained central to later debate. Claims concerning inheritance and the rights of the household occupied a different forum from the urgent appointment of commanders and the collection of public contributions. Communities later articulated distinct understandings of both succession and religious guidance, and their arguments cannot be compressed into a single description of 'political necessity'.

Public action nonetheless continued. Some groups in Arabia disputed the payment of zakāt to Madinah, others withdrew allegiance or followed new claimants to prophecy. Abū Bakr insisted that obligations of the common community persisted beyond the life of the Messenger, and commanders fought campaigns to restore its authority. At al-Yamāmah the struggle against Musaylimah caused severe losses among people who had learned and recited the Qurʾān. The need to preserve revelation and coordinate government pressed upon the same generation.

A new distinction became unavoidable. The Prophet's reception of revelation was unique; his death did not end duties of worship, judgment, mutual aid and public security. These could be carried through remembered guidance, qualified persons and recognized offices, though the right to lead and interpret was contested. Sunni and Shiʿi communities would later articulate different accounts of allegiance, designation and the spiritual authority of the Prophet's family.

What survived was therefore neither a simple replacement of the Prophet nor the abandonment of his social responsibilities. The early community entered a history of differentiated inheritance in which rulers, transmitters, families and teachers had to carry distinct obligations while disagreeing over their legitimate ordering.

**Sharḥ**

The surviving accounts identify disputes over allegiance, consultation, the Prophet's family and the authority of the first caliph. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, reports concerning the allegiance and ʿUmar's retrospective description of Saqīfah, and the traditions concerning Fāṭimah's claim and ʿAlī's later allegiance are essential Sunni witnesses. They require direct reading, not paraphrases that erase conflict. Sunni theology generally distinguishes the non-prophetic office of caliph from the Prophetic gift of revelation; Twelver Shiʿi theology argues that ʿAlī and the Imams possess a divinely designated leadership and authoritative religious guidance. Ghadīr Khumm and the relation of *mawlā* to *wilāyah* feature in the dispute, but a full evaluation of competing reports would displace the history's principal purpose. Sunni discussions generally ground Abū Bakr's caliphate in allegiance and communal obligation; Twelver Shiʿi discussions understand ʿAlī's authority through the doctrine of designation and the Imam's religious office.

The difficulty of succession was especially acute because the Prophet left several kinds of obligation in operation. A governor needed orders, a tax collector needed authorization, an army needed command, and a family needed to know whom to consult over a dispute. The early pledge to Abū Bakr addressed the public coordination of those tasks. It did not remove affection and distinct religious claims attached to the Prophet's household. Keeping these forms of concern separate clarifies both the practical necessity Sunni sources emphasize and the continuing Imāmī argument about divinely designated guidance. Conquests subsequently multiplied the need for commanders, governors, judges and officials able to administer distant provinces.

Succession separated functions that had met in the person of the Messenger. Revelation was complete and its recipient had died. The assessment of testimony, interpretation of commands, care for the Prophet's family, public command of armies and collection of zakāt consequently passed into different hands. They had different principles of authorization: a trustworthy report could establish what the Prophet taught; an argument could show what a rule required; allegiance or designation supplied rival accounts of the right to exercise public or religious leadership. The legitimacy of each claim could not be settled merely by pointing to the need for coordination. At the same time, the duties of taxation, defence and adjudication could not be suspended while every deeper dispute was resolved. The first succession therefore offers a rigorous instance of the distinction between spiritual authority, normative knowledge and effective temporal office. Sunni and Imāmī accounts differ fundamentally in their interpretation of the Prophet's intended succession. The Akbarian category of prophetic comprehensiveness may clarify why inheritance has multiple dimensions, but does not establish the historical verdict between those accounts.

The Ridda conflicts reveal how a continuing duty passes into public action. Abū Bakr held that certain obligations remained binding after the death of their original messenger; enforcement required commanders, provisions and the capacity to distinguish rebellion, renegotiated allegiance and rival prophecy in particular cases. The Qurʾān's preservation called instead for testimony, memorization and careful collection. A community that treats these activities as one undifferentiated 'leadership' loses their distinct evidentiary and moral requirements. The inheritance of final guidance therefore becomes a problem of real formal coordination across specialized offices, with none able to claim the undivided prophetic station.

Abū Bakr's insistence on continued zakāt payments was a decision about whether public obligations survived the Prophet's death. Enforcing zakāt after the Prophet's death concerned public obligation; the grounds of authoritative succession remained a separate theological dispute. The Sunni account treats his caliphate as the first political continuation; Twelver Shiʿi doctrine gives ʿAlī a divinely designated place in the succession to religious guidance. Only the indispensable evidence and principal interpretive difference belong here; the disputed speeches and details of Fadak can remain in a focused note.

The Ridda involved more than a uniform attempt to renounce religion. The actions of different tribes concerned tax, allegiance, local autonomy and prophetic claimants in varying combinations. Keeping military and fiscal relationships functioning was crucial to sustaining the new association: without it, local commanders, teachers and communities would operate under incompatible claims of authority. Al-Bukhārī and later collections associate losses at al-Yamāmah with a collection of Qurʾānic material; other reports also attest written Qurʾānic passages before 632. The expansion after Abū Bakr built on this reconsolidation but exposed the community to institutions much larger than those of Arabia.

### 21. Conquest and the problems inherited from older empires

**Matn**

With the Arabian campaigns still in living memory, armies under ʿUmar reached lands long administered by Rome and Persia. Military victories at al-Yarmūk and al-Qādisiyyah opened Syria and Iraq to new rulers; Egypt also came under Muslim command. The conquered populations still needed grain and irrigation, and their towns possessed officials who understood older records, languages and fiscal arrangements.

Commanders could secure a submission agreement within days; cultivation and revenue required continuous relationships. Christians, Jews, farmers, traders and other residents did not change their languages or beliefs when one garrison replaced another. Soldiers needed payment, taxpayers expected that demands would have known limits, and households sought protection for property and worship. Early governors retained and altered inherited practices according to local conditions.

New garrison towns such as al-Baṣrah, al-Kūfah and al-Fusṭāṭ gathered troops while leaving much of the countryside under continuing cultivation. These settlements became places where military, domestic, mercantile and religious questions met. A disagreement over land or a levy raised a question of just authority as surely as a dispute within a household, but its resolution needed surveyors, accountants, judges and agreements as well as piety.

The expansion widened the field of government beyond anything that could be managed through direct companionship with the Prophet. Justice remained an orientation, while its effect depended upon distinct forms of knowledge and on the accuracy of the persons exercising them. Later legal and administrative traditions grew within these contingent arrangements, not from a single immutable plan for imperial government.

**Sharḥ**

Conquest gave commanders territory, but administering that territory required decisions about cultivators, local officials, soldiers and revenue. Armies could acquire territorial authority in a campaigning season; provisioning troops and assessing land required continuing negotiation with cultivators, notables and former officials. Al-Balādhurī's *Futūḥ al-buldān* preserves accounts of treaties and settlements, but details vary across recensions and may reflect subsequent fiscal controversies. Administrative papyri and coins offer independent controls on gradual changes in government and language. Muslim governors adapted some existing fiscal practices and altered others; the conquered provinces did not immediately share one uniform tax code. The Syrian, Iraqi and Egyptian arrangements followed different trajectories.

The division between garrison and countryside helps explain why the conquerors' language and legal practices could spread without immediately making the subject population Muslim. Tax resources went to military stipends and government, while established cultivators and religious institutions continued many activities. The eventual change of language, conversion and local custom therefore took generations and differed by region. Governors in conquered provinces had to determine levies, recognize land claims, uphold agreements and restrain soldiers far from Madinah. The moral language of justice was inherited from the Prophetic teaching, but its application now involved a more complex set of agents and documents. Hugh Kennedy and the *New Cambridge History of Islam* document the variation among conquest settlements; surviving treaty reports do not preserve one uniform agreement.

Expansion brings the two eyes of practical judgment into public administration. The universal end—justice toward people whose rights are engaged—must be seen together with the particulars of each province. A cultivator's irrigation cycle is not a replaceable detail; the harvest is materially dependent upon it. A fiscal record in Greek or Coptic has an exact meaning that the governor must learn through competent officials. A treaty clause binds specified parties, while a jurist's classification examines its normative effect. This encounter between moral orientation and local knowledge explains why military victory can create obligations that conquest itself cannot fulfil. The Prophet's example of just dealing supplies a standard of action; it neither dictates a single Egyptian tax ledger nor substitutes for comparing surviving papyri, coinage and agreements.

The three practical orders are visible within one dispute. A commander needs personal restraint when tempted to confiscate; a peasant household needs dependable possession of its means of subsistence; a government must provide revenue and security through rules that others can know. Each order conditions the others, yet its object remains distinct. When governors coordinate soldiers, judges and administrators through an enforceable public form, their unity is achieved by truthful, trained action rather than the political accumulation of every competence in one officer. The historical mechanisms of such coordination include garrisons, inherited offices, petitions and revenues whose evidence must be investigated province by province.

Dated receipts and correspondence from Egypt preserve administrative practices that continued or changed under Muslim rule. A landholder and a soldier encountered power differently: the soldier needed regular provisions; the cultivator needed a predictable demand and protection from arbitrary seizure. Their interaction connected military policy to harvest cycles and irrigation. Nor is the word *jizyah* consistently used for one identical early fiscal arrangement in all sources; terminology and practice need local comparison. This matters to the later juristic tradition because arguments about the rights of cultivators and protected communities emerged in the aftermath of real negotiated settlements, some remembered through legalizing retrospective narratives. After victory, officials collected revenue, cultivators worked the land, and commanders supplied troops; these recurring duties sustained political control beyond the battlefield.

### 22. The garrison, the treasury and the provincial community

**Matn**

The garrison towns founded during the conquests gradually became households and marketplaces as well as military stations. Soldiers received payments recorded in registers; their relatives, clients, traders and teachers formed neighbourhoods around them. People who shared prayer did not always share the same claim to revenue, standing or patronage. The treasury therefore entered daily life through the allotment of stipends, the assessment of land and the expectations of dependants.

The registers associated with ʿUmar's government made the treasury legible through names, ranked entitlements and recorded payments. A soldier's stipend could sustain a household far from the fields that produced the revenue, so the form of accounting joined urban and rural obligations. A clerk's accuracy was not merely a technical adornment of government: error could deny food or status to a family, while an unjust priority rule could remain oppressive even when every calculation was correct.

The payment register, or dīwān, required information about service and entitlement. In Iraq the decision to leave broad cultivated areas under recurring taxation gave the state resources for more than one campaign, while cultivators remained responsible for production. A policy about land could support soldiers far from the field, but its burdens were borne by families working the soil. Errors or inequalities in an account thus became acts with moral and political consequences.

As new converts and clients entered provincial communities, inherited tribal rankings no longer settled every question of membership. A person could possess religious knowledge and still have little influence over a pay list or appointment. Others could accumulate influence through the very offices that had been created to administer shared resources. Judges and teachers emerged among the inhabitants whose disputes and expectations exceeded a garrison's initial purpose.

A just community needed courage in defence, accuracy in accounts, fairness in office and recognition of claims that had not been present at its beginning. These excellences had their own techniques and forms of training. Their coordination became a test of the authority that claimed to govern believers as one association.

**Sharḥ**

The *dīwān* was a register and payment system, not the equivalent of a modern civil service or a census of all residents. Accounts of garrison foundations and stipend rankings vary, while later settlement and fiscal needs altered administrative practice. Fred Donner traces the interaction of military recruitment, settlement and revenue administration during the early Islamic conquests. The precise policy on Iraqi land is known partly through retrospective juristic accounts in which allocation of conquered lands had become a legal problem.

Ibn Khaldūn later argued that group solidarity helps establish military authority, while durable rule also requires revenue and administration across wider territories. Yet he cannot substitute for the details of seventh-century stipends and provinces. A converted Persian client in al-Kūfah, a landed Christian family in Syria and an Arab soldier receiving payments did not enter the same arrangement. Soldiers, cultivators, clients, officials and scholars held different claims on revenues, property and public authority. It also explains why towns of conquest became centres of learning: they housed people who needed judgments on marriage, sale, slavery, inheritance and disputes involving communities with different practices. Students learned from Companions and successors amid these recurring problems rather than in a social vacuum.

The dīwān is an instructive case of real formal unity because a written register mediates between persons who may never meet. It records a service or entitlement, connects it to the treasury and enables the payment that supports a household. The mathematics of a stipend has its own necessary accuracy; an unjust ranking remains objectionable even if the sum is computed without error. Moral character, quantitative technique and public authority therefore converge upon the same disbursement while contributing different determinations to it. This is the practical form of discerning a whole through valid partial standpoints. The register is not itself a conscience, but an official's trained capacity for truthful calculation is an essential part of just conduct.

The growing problem of mawālī and differential status also illustrates the distinction between formal community membership and effective social recognition. The same prayer can join people whose access to office, land or military stipends differs. These inequalities have to be described in the fiscal and social conditions of each place before the historian can judge their consequences. Later juristic reflection about conquered land, the treasury and non-Muslim subjects is a genuine normative inheritance of the effort to order relations justly, while the seventh-century arrangements arose through contingent political choices. The universal end gives judgments a measure; it does not erase the historically particular means through which their effects were secured.

Differences in military stipends shaped household income, prestige and competition for public resources. If an early participant in the campaigns held a recognised claim on the treasury, a later convert or client might not possess an equivalent claim even when sharing the same worship. That tension helps explain debates about the *mawālī* and the eventual pressures on Umayyad government. Clients and provincial populations encountered unequal arrangements, although access to stipends and public office varied across regions and periods. Some were wealthy and influential, and recruitment and fiscal practice changed. The closer historical explanation follows a particular garrison's register, its sources of revenue and its social composition. The relation between inherited solidarity and religious universalism becomes visible through conflicts over payment and status. It also clarifies why instruction mattered: a growing town needed means to identify valid testimony, settle competing property claims and teach new residents obligations that had developed first among a much smaller community.

### 23. Civil war and the limits of loyalty

**Matn**

As governors commanded armies and drew upon the resources of distant provinces, disagreements over appointments and justice sharpened. Rebels killed ʿUthmān in Madinah in 656. ʿAlī received allegiance, while other groups demanded punishment of the killers and disputed who had authority or the practical ability to obtain it. Battles near al-Baṣrah and at Ṣiffīn brought companions and their followers into armed opposition; the attempt at arbitration divided some of ʿAlī's supporters, and further violence followed.

The confrontation near al-Baṣrah, later remembered as the Battle of the Camel, involved ʿĀʾishah, the Prophet's widow, and leading companions whose demands concerning ʿUthmān's death entered the contest with ʿAlī's supporters. This is a history of people who had learned directly from the Messenger and nevertheless faced incompatible views of what justice required amid armed factions. Their motives and reported speeches must be read through surviving accounts that were transmitted under later political and sectarian pressures.

The contending parties appealed to duties that could not easily be ordered within a fractured community. Allegiance, retaliation, the right to command and the protection of persons all carried moral weight. An army could enforce a claim only if its members recognized the authority directing it. A verdict that no effective power could execute might fail to provide the justice that its wording promised.

Iraq and Syria supplied different troops, commanders and loyalties. Their material organization mattered to the course of war just as the principles invoked by their leaders mattered to the participants. ʿAlī was killed in 661; his son al-Ḥasan subsequently reached a settlement with Muʿāwiyah. The fighting ended in one form while its memories and arguments continued.

The first civil war made clear how the truth of a norm, the right to exercise judgment and the capacity to implement it can become historically separated. Later communities would study these events through inherited loyalties and theological disagreements, while the original suffering and the claims of those involved continued to demand careful remembrance.

**Sharḥ**

The broad order of events is well attested in early Muslim historiography; the words attributed to individual participants, precise motives and details of arbitration require more caution. Al-Ṭabarī brings together reports from transmitters with different affiliations, and these are not independent eyewitness accounts merely because they appear side by side. Sunni histories recognize the leading Companions' standing while preserving accounts of disputed decisions, armed conflict and deaths among their supporters. Shiʿi accounts give the rights and designation of ʿAlī a different constitutional and theological meaning. Sunni and Shiʿi arguments about legitimate authority developed amid disputes over retaliation, military allegiance and the duties of government.

The civil war also had geographical and material mechanisms. The garrison army of Iraq and the Syrian command were organized forces with their own leadership, pay and attachments. Muʿāwiyah could rely upon a developed provincial military apparatus; ʿAlī had to govern amid divided coalitions and disputes about the killing of ʿUthmān. Later kalām arguments about a grave sinner and political obedience grew in a world that remembered those choices. Rival factions invoked justice, but disputed who possessed authority to punish ʿUthmān's killers and whether political allegiance must precede retaliation. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Balādhurī preserve differing accounts of the first fitna, especially in reported speeches and motives; their transmissions do not independently establish each participant's private intentions.

The division of powers during the first fitna makes authority a practical and epistemic problem at once. The demand for retaliation depends upon knowing who bore responsibility, under what procedure guilt could be established, who possessed jurisdiction and which force could execute a judgment without producing further injustice. A person may rightly desire justice while misjudging the temporal conditions of its implementation. The later philosophy of practical wisdom can clarify this difference between a true end and a prudent, adequately informed choice of means. A moral norm neither creates evidence nor commands a functioning army by itself. The reported speeches, disputed motives and shifting coalitions must be examined with historical methods proper to those questions.

The unity of religious life was now experienced under conditions in which the offices carrying it were in conflict. Allegiance to a ruler, knowledge of a transmitted report, kinship with the Prophet's family and personal integrity acquired distinct claims to recognition. No concept of Muhammadan comprehensiveness licenses identifying any army with the totality of the prophetic inheritance. It can instead make the historian attentive to the tragic separation of valid concerns that a perfected discernment would order appropriately. Later Sunnī, Imāmī and Khārijite arguments must be studied in their developed forms while the earliest reports remain accountable to their own transmission histories.

Later theological classifications of the first fitna do not establish the exact beliefs or motives of every participant in the original conflicts. The battle at Ṣiffīn and the division over arbitration help explain how political disagreement prompted debates over the relation between judgment and divine law. The outcomes of the battles do not establish that every participant held the fully developed doctrines later associated with Khārijites, Sunnis or Shiʿa. The extant speeches are transmitted through literary and partisan filters. The strongest causal account connects the troops' alliances, demands about the killing of ʿUthmān and the procedural problem of retaliation. The later theological writings can then be read as responses to the remembered crisis, with their own dates and purposes. The disputes over allegiance, retaliation and ʿAlī's authority shaped later Sunni and Shiʿi understandings of legitimate government.

### 24. Damascus, Karbalāʾ and the making of an imperial administration

**Matn**

Muʿāwiyah governed from Damascus with a powerful Syrian command and administrative resources inherited and altered from earlier provincial systems. His decision to nominate his son Yazīd renewed the question of how a community should recognize rightful rule. Al-Ḥusayn, grandson of the Prophet, rejected that allegiance and travelled toward Iraq after receiving appeals for support. At Karbalāʾ in 680 he and members of his family and company were killed. His death placed personal loyalty, political power and the claims of the prophetic household before later generations in an enduringly painful form.

Government nevertheless continued to grow in outward capacity. After further civil wars, ʿAbd al-Malik consolidated Umayyad authority, changed coinage and gave Arabic greater prominence in administration. Officials required records of revenue, commanders required disciplined troops, and public monuments represented the dynasty's claims. These means could establish a political order across distant provinces without ensuring that every inhabitant accepted its moral authority.

Believers also received guidance through reciters, teachers, jurists and families whose reputation did not simply follow the line of state appointment. Some were supported by rulers, others criticized them or kept a prudent distance. A public office controlled certain resources, while spiritual trust and learned competence had different grounds of recognition.

The gap between effective rule and rightful guidance became a central historical problem. Its resolution could not be achieved through control of coinage alone, yet a community's welfare still required courts, provisions and protection. Later Muslim histories would carry the pain of Karbalāʾ alongside the administrative achievements and injustices of empire.

**Sharḥ**

Al-Ḥusayn and his companions were killed at Karbalāʾ; reports of speeches and exact casualty figures vary among later transmitters. The death of al-Ḥusayn and his companions is foundational to Shiʿi historical memory, while Sunni accounts also affirm the wrong and grief of the event, even as theological readings diverge. Al-Ṭabarī's use of Abū Mikhnaf and later treatments by al-Mufīd overlap in transmission; agreement between them cannot automatically be counted as independent attestation.

Umayyad coins, inscriptions and buildings record official policies and dynastic claims that later chronicles recount through their own traditions. Currency reform mattered because weights, taxes and payments connected distant provinces through common state symbols and procedures. Arabic administrative change was uneven, and the presence of Arabic in registers did not immediately displace Greek, Coptic and Persian from society. Nor should political consolidation be equated with universal religious acceptance. Schools of Qurʾānic recitation, ascetic teaching and early legal discussion grew within and sometimes against the horizons of state power. Al-Ḥajjāj's governorship and the experience of Iraqi opposition also reveal coercive dimensions of the imperial project. The distinction between the caliph's political authority and a teacher's credibility becomes more intelligible through these particular institutional pressures.

Karbalāʾ marks the irreducibility of moral witness to political success. Al-Ḥusayn's relationship to the Prophet, his refusal of allegiance, the expectations of his supporters and the military capacity available in Iraq were distinct realities that later accounts join through powerful memory. A ruler's command and a person's truthful fidelity need not coincide. Yet a metaphysical reading of witness cannot determine unreported motives, precise speeches or the independent attestation of each dramatic episode. The sources of grief and the formation of ritual memory belong to different stages of transmission. The Akbarian question of spiritual and temporal authority offers a conceptual differentiation without settling the contested doctrines of succession by analogy.

Umayyad coinage and administrative reform place the same distinction at the scale of empire. A coin can bear a public theological claim and function as an instrument of exchange; its metal, inscription and circulation have empirically recoverable histories. A learned teacher can preserve recitation or moral guidance without holding office in the dynasty that issues it. The formal unity of a community must therefore be pursued through relations among state coercion, legal competence, worship, family obligation and spiritual recognition. Their interaction is historically demonstrable only through coins, inscriptions, reports, documentary records and institutional histories, not by assuming that the outward universality of a caliphal title establishes an inwardly comprehensive authority.

The Dome of the Rock supplies a particularly revealing contrast between official and social evidence. Its Arabic inscriptions present theological claims in a public imperial setting; its construction dates and physical form can be investigated independently of late court anecdotes. Coin inscriptions and administrative documents trace changes in how the state represented and financed itself. Yet none of these tells us what every market trader believed or how every household taught the Qurʾān. That difference matters: an empire may adopt common public signs while local traditions continue in distinct languages and social groups. In later decades the *mawālī*, tax burdens and provincial grievances entered contests over rule. The Abbasid movement arose within a consolidated Umayyad empire where political stability had not settled competing claims to leadership and status.

### 25. The Abbasid revolution and a change of ruling centre

**Matn**

By the middle of the eighth century, the Umayyad state governed great territories yet faced opposition over status, revenue and legitimacy. Networks in Khurāsān gathered Arab settlers and other supporters around a movement invoking the Prophet's family. Under Abū Muslim, its armies moved west, defeated Umayyad forces and established Abbasid rule in 750. The new dynasty inherited both the powers and the tensions of its predecessor.

Promises of just rule had to pass through soldiers, advisers and rival claimants whose purposes were not identical. The Abbasids developed their own household of power; hopes associated with other descendants of the Prophet remained alive. Al-Manṣūr later established Baghdad on the Tigris, near agricultural revenues and routes joining Iraq with eastern provinces. The capital brought secretaries, merchants, artisans, doctors and scholars into a shared urban economy.

A city of learning depends upon more than the curiosity of its inhabitants. Books require materials and copying, physicians patients and observation, teachers patrons or fees, and judges public recognition of their decisions. The court could sustain some of these activities without owning their intellectual truth. As the metropolis grew, learned authority and state authority became increasingly distinguishable through the different works each performed.

The revolution moved the centre of rule; Baghdad's institutions gave that change wider historical consequence. Power supplied opportunities and constraints for disciplines whose proper ends—truthful knowledge, healing, fair judgment and cultivated character—could not be reduced to dynastic advantage.

**Sharḥ**

The Abbasid movement recruited support across several groups, including Khurāsānian military and political networks, and did not represent only non-Arab converts. The movement involved overlapping interests in Khurāsān, including established Arab settler communities, patron-client networks, religious expectations and military organization. Its slogans did not prescribe a single later theory of authority. Consult al-Ṭabarī alongside studies of the Abbasid revolution by M. A. Shaban and Elton Daniel, while recognizing disagreements in their reconstructions.

Khurāsānian support and the establishment of Baghdad in Iraq shifted the caliphate's military and administrative centre eastward. Iraq sat near productive agricultural districts and routes connecting Iran, the Gulf and Syria; Baghdad's foundation under al-Manṣūr in 762 deliberately concentrated administration. But courts do not create intellectual life by decree. The city inherited teachers, translators and merchants from older centres, while paper, libraries, private patrons and students increasingly made scholarship possible beyond the immediate court. Baghdad's scholarly activity depended on administrators, merchants, patrons, booksellers, translators and teachers, not on its foundation alone. Dynastic legitimacy, in turn, continued to be debated among jurists, Alids and different provincial populations; the Abbasid revolution settled who ruled, not every question of who could teach or interpret revelation.

The Abbasid transformation separates three causes that a dynastic narrative often fuses. Military organization in Khurāsān made a change of rulers feasible; competing claims to the Prophet's family helped rally allegiance; the movement of the court toward Iraq altered the resources and learned personnel available to a new government. Each cause has its own witnesses and temporal order. The righteousness of a claimed goal cannot substitute for reconstructing how troops were enrolled, promises made or administrators appointed. Nor does victory establish the rightful distribution of spiritual or interpretive authority among the supporters.

Baghdad's later growth gives the positive development of this differentiation. A physician's art aims at bodily health, a logician's at valid inference, a jurist's at determination of rights and duties, and a ruler's office at public coordination. These excellences may coexist in a city without being produced by royal will. When a patron provides books or wages, he supplies material means for an intellectual good whose truth depends on its own proper practice. The comprehensive human horizon is realized socially through just relations among specialized workers: the court has responsibilities to them, while their knowledge is answerable to standards not reducible to patronage. This supplies a more exact account of a learned metropolis than attributing its formation to the mere greatness of a dynasty.

## Book V — How knowledge and spiritual formation acquired lasting institutions

### 26. Baghdad and the work of a learned city

**Matn**

Baghdad gathered people engaged in work that required many different kinds of knowledge. Translators moved among Greek, Syriac, Persian and Arabic; physicians studied bodies and treatments; mathematicians calculated; theologians debated doctrine; secretaries kept accounts and jurists heard questions arising from city life. Workshops, libraries, courts and wealthy households supplied the settings in which these competences were learned and exchanged.

A translation moved meaning across languages only through labour. One person knew a technical vocabulary, another could test it against practical experience, and a patron paid for materials, scribes and time. Students compared copies and teachers corrected error. Writing and paper widened the circulation of books, but the written page still required a reader capable of understanding its claims.

The city's abundance of disciplines raised a question of order. The skilled physician could know how a treatment affects the body without thereby settling a dispute about property; a theologian might answer a doctrinal question while depending on an astronomer's calculations for a different purpose. Their knowledge was valid within its field and needed an intelligent relation to other fields when an action involved several. Public institutions offered one possible arrangement of their cooperation.

Learning thus spread through differentiated arts joined by the needs of the people who practised them. The search for a comprehensive human good did not erase specialization; it demanded that specialization remain accountable to the person and community it served.

**Sharḥ**

Hārūn al-Rashīd's court and al-Maʾmūn's patronage are important, but the familiar story of a single 'House of Wisdom' producing all the sciences suppresses the multiplicity of patrons and workplaces. Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq's translation projects, al-Kindī's philosophical circle and the mathematical writing associated with al-Khwārizmī had distinct aims and constituencies. Dimitri Gutas, *Greek Thought, Arabic Culture*, and research on medicine and translation distinguish court translation from wider patterns of scholarly patronage. The adoption of paper expanded the possibilities for copying without making manuscripts cheap for every reader.

Baghdad gathered translators, booksellers, physicians, administrators and patrons who sustained scholarly work through different institutions. Secretarial administration required numeracy and records; medicine attracted patrons and clinical expertise; juridical differences demanded teachers able to transmit and reason; rich households could sustain independent specialists. State patrons, merchants and scholarly specialists jointly sustained Baghdad's translation and book culture, a relationship central to Hodgson's account of the period. But the learned world was not always harmoniously plural. In the *miḥnah*, caliphal demands about the createdness of the Qurʾān brought public theology and state coercion together. Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal's refusal and later memory of the episode exemplify a struggle over the boundary between the ruler's command and a scholar's religious authority. It would be misleading either to call this a conflict between 'religion' and 'science' or to identify all speculative inquiry with official coercion.

The learned city illustrates how the One is approached through distinguishable meanings without confusing the object of every science. Medicine investigates bodily conditions and efficacy; mathematical demonstration establishes consequences of defined premises; philology determines what words mean in the usage of their speakers; jurisprudence asks about normative relations and their authoritative grounds. A person may need several of these when acting—for instance, treating a patient under rules of consent and payment. Their conjunction is a real unity of the concrete case, not a new science that renders their methods dispensable. The Akbarian interpretation of a comprehensive human presence helps explain why specialized truths may be integrated in a rightly formed knower, while it supplies no substitute for a successful experiment or a demonstrative proof.

Translation is equally an embodied discipline of fidelity. A translator's semantic skill, a patron's resources, a scribe's accuracy and a student's capacity to understand belong to different causal positions in the life of a book. Knowledge passes through all of them and can be damaged at any junction. The descent of a meaning into a new language is a useful analogy of form and meaning when its limits are observed: human translation requires correction and comparison, while Qurʾānic revelation has its own unrepeatable mode of authorization. The historical development of Baghdad must be assessed through named translations, manuscripts, teachers and institutions rather than the analogy alone.

The history of translation is most persuasive when treated through a defined task. Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq's work on Greek medical texts involved comparing copies, choosing technical equivalents and revising earlier translations; a practitioner then had to interpret the resulting Arabic work in relation to patients and other medical knowledge. The procedure differed from al-Khwārizmī's mathematical writing or a jurist's evaluation of a report, even when they shared bookshops and patrons. For this reason 'Islamic science' is a historical umbrella, not the name of one single method. During the *miḥnah*, caliphal authorities sponsored intellectual inquiry while pressuring scholars to affirm a particular doctrine about the Qurʾān. Intellectual life became durable through a plurality of communities capable of criticising, transmitting and sponsoring knowledge, although their relative independence varied. Individual translations passed through named patrons, scribes, translators and readers, often with several stages of revision.

### 27. Travelers, households and the authentication of transmitted learning

**Matn**

As the first generations of Muslims passed away, knowledge of the Prophet's words and acts depended increasingly on people who had learned them through other people. A report might reach one town through a household and another through a traveller. Questions about worship, trade or family life made the accuracy of these transmissions a matter with practical consequences.

ʿĀʾishah, the Prophet's wife, had knowledge of household practices and private conduct that visitors to public assemblies could not normally observe. Her reports reached later teachers through particular pupils and chains. Such testimony became important for prayer, ritual purity, family relations and the ordinary habits of the Messenger. Preserving a report required trust in a human witness; deriving its legal significance required a further judgment about wording, occasion and the relationship with other evidence.

Scholars visited teachers, compared wordings and examined the reliability and opportunity of narrators. A report's chain, its wording and its place among other accounts presented different questions. A learner might memorize a tradition faithfully yet still require guidance in understanding the circumstances to which its norm applied. Families, including women who transmitted knowledge acquired within the Prophet's household, were essential sources for matters that public assemblies did not disclose.

Cities formed their own teaching relations, copying practices and conventions of correction. A teacher's reputation was supported through repeated encounters; the written record enabled wider comparison and preservation. At the same time, differences among reports demanded judgment about strength, meaning and possible reconciliation.

The movement from living witness to teachable science reveals one path of prophetic inheritance. Recollection safeguards access to the original act; disciplined reasoning understands its conditions; practice makes its guidance operative in a new person's life. Each achievement requires its own evidence and training, and no single one can replace the others.

**Sharḥ**

The *isnād* was an explicit claim about routes of transmission, not a mechanical guarantee of truth. Some students transmitted from personal audition, some from reading or authorization, and scholars debated these methods. Differences among *ṣaḥīḥ*, *ḥasan*, *ḍaʿīf* and other classifications developed through particular critical conventions; the two canonical Ṣaḥīḥs were not produced under one uniform ancient state procedure. The introductory material in Muslim's *Ṣaḥīḥ*, al-Bukhārī's chapter headings and later discussions by Ibn al-Ṣalāḥ help explain the criteria. The historical date of a report and a ḥadīth critic's judgment of its acceptability answer separate questions. Harald Motzki compares chains and wording to reconstruct stages of transmission; scholars contest some of the conclusions drawn from those comparisons.

The household was not marginal to this process. ʿĀʾishah was asked about private worship, domestic conduct and the circumstances of legal pronouncements because these subjects often lay outside male public observation. Later women transmitters and teachers, documented in biographical dictionaries, were associated with scholarly households and institutions, although participation varied by region and century. Households, mosques and traveling students preserved Prophetic practice across cities without relying on a single caliphal archive. Traveling students and scholarly correspondence gradually connected distant communities while leaving room for local practices and disagreements.

Hadith study distinguishes preservation from understanding and enactment. The transmitter answers whether particular words or conduct may responsibly be attributed to the Prophet, taking account of witnesses, links, wording and possible variation. The jurist investigates which norm follows and its conditions, while a person acting upon the guidance needs knowledge of the circumstances before him. One authentic report may require several kinds of interpretation; one ethically admirable motive does not improve the reliability of an unreliable chain. Their conjunction therefore involves compatible but independent judgments. The complete prophetic act is the living original to which these disciplines refer, each under a valid aspect.

Women and men learned different details through particular relationships and opportunities of observation. Reports preserved from the Prophet's household must be considered within their context, and their authority argued through the same relevant standards of transmission rather than assigned a lesser rank because the event occurred in private. The labor of comparison, recitation and correction creates malakāt: trained abilities that make someone capable of reliable memory and discrimination over time. These are real acquired perfections. In the spiritual vocabulary of takhalluq and tahaqquq, the formation of a craft or virtue is distinguishable from a claim to realized inner knowledge; neither allows a scholar to claim the entire Muhammadan station. Their shared end is trustworthy guidance used according to its proper object.

Report critics compared transmissions from different cities to identify common wording, variations and possible shared sources. If two students in different cities transmitted what they said was the same Prophetic report, the critic might compare the named links in their chains, the wording of the two texts and reports about the reliability and contact of the transmitters. Agreement might reflect shared earlier transmission rather than independent corroboration; difference might arise from abbreviation, memory or a genuine alternate wording. Inclusion in a collection did not settle a report's wording, original setting or legal application. A jurist might accept a report's transmission yet debate whether an action was obligatory, recommended or context-specific. Jurists then assessed whether reliable reports established obligation, recommendation, permission or a ruling specific to one occasion. Women such as ʿĀʾishah transmitted knowledge through identifiable households and teaching relationships; their roles and opportunities varied among regions and generations.

Transmission became accountable precisely because different students could preserve different wordings or levels of detail. A particularly strong example comes from the *Muwaṭṭaʾ*: El Shamsy compares the recensions of Yaḥyā al-Laythī, Abū Muṣʿab and Ibn Bukayr with quotations in al-Shāfiʿī's *Umm*. Some variants alter only phrasing; others affect the degree of agreement a position claims, the narrator chain, or the teacher's attached explanation. Different recensions preserve variations in wording, attribution and juristic comment, so no single printed recension reproduces every form in which a teaching circulated. ### 28. Legal schools emerge from the need to decide recurrent cases

**Matn**

Muslim judges, merchants, spouses and heirs faced disputes that the Prophet's companions had not encountered in identical form. A sale might pass through an agent, an inherited estate might include claims in several places, and a household might follow a custom unknown in Madinah. Jurists turned to the Qurʾān, transmitted reports, communal practice and reasoned methods for determining obligations that could be applied fairly.

Abū Ḥanīfah taught in Iraq; his students developed and transmitted forms of legal reasoning responsive to cases of commerce and public life. Mālik's work in Madinah joined reports to the practice of its learned community. Al-Shāfiʿī argued carefully about the authority of particular sources, and Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal became associated with painstaking attention to transmitted reports. The schools formed through the subsequent work of many pupils and judges, not through the separate founders handing down finished books of every possible ruling.

Study gathered around teachers whose judgments acquired authority through knowledge and trust. Their students preserved, disputed and organized conclusions; distinct schools came to develop techniques for weighing reports, recognizing agreement, extending principles and giving effect to custom. The emergence of several juristic traditions was an institutional history of learning as well as a sustained inquiry into the proper scope of divine law.

The object of a legal judgment is an action with a determinate status, yet the action occurs in a world of particular persons, intentions and available evidence. A judge who determines ownership uses a procedure different from the spiritual teacher who helps a person give generously. The two concerns can meet in the same transaction because rights and moral formation belong to one life. They are not interchangeable kinds of expertise.

The living Prophetic example was therefore carried through multiple forms of knowledge. Fiqh articulated norms and their conditions; legal theory examined how those norms could be known; education formed the habits needed to exercise judgment without partiality. The schools remained answerable to the sources and to the needs of cases that recurred in changing communities.

**Sharḥ**

Disputes over sales, agency and liability required jurists to apply transmitted rulings and broader principles to new transactions. Suppose a merchant gives goods to an agent who sells to a third party, then disputes the price or liability for loss. A jurist needs to know the contractual terms, local usage, rules of agency and the relation of evidence to general principle. Competing schools may differ because they classify the transaction differently, trust different reports or apply analogical reasoning under different restrictions. The legal concepts are formed precisely to make difficult cases teachable and repeatable. Al-Shāfiʿī's *al-Risālah*, Mālik's *Muwaṭṭaʾ*, works transmitted from Muḥammad al-Shaybānī and early Ḥanbalī collections arose in different settings; later school manuals reorganized their arguments and rulings.

The legal sciences inherited the Prophet's actual judgments within a comprehensive life. His response to the woman of Ghāmid in [Muslim 1695](https://sunnah.com/muslim:1695) includes a legal decision and also an encounter shaped by confession, pregnancy, care for a child and repentance. A jurist must identify the kind of evidence that can establish an offense, the qualifications governing a sanction and the authority that may apply it. Those determinations protect claimants and defendants from the arbitrariness of an official's private feeling. The discipline is a genuine preservation of the revealed and transmitted normative order.

The juristic tradition is one of the clearest cases of a true partial inheritance. A legal proposition determines whether an act is obligatory, forbidden, valid, void or otherwise classified under conditions supplied by authoritative evidence. This work can be precise without exhausting the act's moral, personal and spiritual meaning. In the case of a confession, rules of admissibility, proof and jurisdiction preserve genuine normative aspects, while care for a child and the penitent's inward return remain distinct objects of attention. The unity of the original judgment depends on ordering these aspects in the real situation. Jurists differ because they weigh different reports, understand language in different ways or identify different grounds for extending a rule; such disagreement requires methodical analysis, not an appeal to undifferentiated spiritual wholeness.

A sound theory of fiqh should distinguish an enduring norm from a contingent administrative form. The law concerning an endowment can be applied under different regimes with altered deeds, tribunals and social conditions. Its obligations must be derived through usūl, evidence and juristic reasoning; the capacity to enforce it depends on actually constituted authority. The schools preserve a living capacity for normative discernment through teaching, correction and controversy, rather than a complete institutional blueprint fixed at the Prophet's death. This distinction is necessary for the later history of colonial courts and educational reform.

The same original can be read through ethical and spiritual questions concerning repentance and the disposition of those who witness another person's wrongdoing. Ibn Rushd's method in the *Bidāyat al-mujtahid* and the argumentation of later uṣūl works make the legal determination explicit through reasons, differences of evidence and the conditions of cases. The general norm becomes effective through *taḥqīq al-manāṭ*, the recognition of what is present in a particular instance. Understanding and correctly applying a ruling is a perfected legal activity; the Prophet's complete realization is the spiritual and moral horizon to which that activity ultimately answers.

The legal schools were historically made by students, teachers and later jurists. For this reason a school's 'position' in a late manual cannot simply be attributed to its eponymous founder. Theological commitments, juristic reasoning and actual courts intersected without being identical. The Sunni four-school map was also not a description of every Muslim juristic tradition: Imāmī and Zaydī law developed distinct authorities, and other early schools later lost institutional continuity. For broad historical interpretation use Wael Hallaq, *The Origins and Evolution of Islamic Law*; for the necessary critical note, compare its reconstructions with primary texts and other modern models of legal emergence. Jurists trained students to classify acts, resolve conflicts among reports and reason from principles when inherited formulations did not settle a new case.

A revealing manuscript-level comparison concerns Mālik's *Muwaṭṭaʾ*. Its transmitted recensions differ, and the version associated with Muḥammad al-Shaybānī sometimes sets Mālik's Medinan positions beside Ḥanafī objections. Teachers carried texts between cities, compared their variants and argued about the rulings attributed to earlier authorities. Later western recensions had their own histories of circulation and authority. A student who learns one line of argument about sales, inheritance or ritual practice may therefore enter a dialogue already shaped by several teachers. The school becomes a discipline when these arguments can be preserved, criticised and extended while remaining recognisably related. Wael Hallaq's histories of school formation provide a model for examining this institutional process, but individual recensions and their manuscript witnesses remain the primary controls.

One *Muwaṭṭaʾ* report describes Ibn ʿUmar sprinkling water in his eyes during *ghusl*, while recensions differ over Mālik's accompanying juristic judgment. One recension preserves the practice without Mālik's judgment; al-Shāfiʿī quotes Mālik's comment that it was not accepted practice, while two other recensions give comparable statements that it was not obligatory. The recensions distinguish Ibn ʿUmar's reported conduct, Mālik's interpretation of its legal force and the rulings attributed to later schools. Students and jurists used such variants to decide whether reported conduct established an obligation, a recommendation or a permitted practice. ### 29. Endowments, colleges and the social life of scholarship

**Matn**

A teacher's knowledge could be transmitted in a courtyard or a household, but sustained learning also required food, books and time. As Muslim cities grew, founders assigned income-bearing properties to mosques, colleges, hospitals, lodgings and other purposes. Through waqf, an asset could support an obligation or benefit beyond the life of the donor while remaining subject to legal conditions.

The form has an early exemplary source in the property associated with ʿUmar at Khaybar. Seeking guidance about valuable land, he was instructed to preserve its substance while directing its fruits toward specified recipients. Later institutions adopted different instruments and arrangements, and their actual working depended upon managers, documents, judges and reliable revenue. A donor's good intention needed competent administration if dependants and students were to receive the promised benefit.

In a college, learning became a shared social practice. Teachers demonstrated how to read, students acquired habits through correction, books passed from one generation to another and funds made sustained attention possible. Those means could also be misused through rivalry, exclusion or prestige-seeking. Their value lay in the persons formed and the real goods delivered.

A spiritual intention, a property right and a teaching institution can be related without becoming the same thing. The first directs a human will; the second establishes a durable claim; the third coordinates work across many lives. Waqf became a major instrument through which a temporary decision was carried into long historical time.

**Sharḥ**

The institution of *waqf* connected property to an intended continuing purpose under legally defined conditions. Its resources could pay stipends, maintain lodging and support copying; they could also sustain the prestige of the founding household. Michael Chamberlain documents how medieval Damascene colleges operated through endowments, elite patronage, scholarly households and personal relationships rather than modern university bureaucracy. In Damascus, endowed institutions were bound to elite strategies, offices and ceremonies, while much learning still occurred personally and privately. George Makdisi's account of institutionalized education is an important comparison, not an uncontested template.

The Prophet's mosque accommodated worship and instruction; later endowed colleges financed teachers and lodging through arrangements developed under different political conditions. The Prophet's mosque sustained public worship and instruction; later cities with large populations required more differentiated means of financing teachers and accommodating traveling students. An endowment is a historical institutional solution, not an originally complete blueprint present in seventh-century Madinah. It can, however, be evaluated within the religious language of charity, useful knowledge and obligation to others. The college also exposes a tension between credential and formation: a person may acquire the right to occupy a chair through patronage without demonstrating wisdom, while an influential teacher may have no such office. Teachers cultivated *adab* and stable capacities through particular curricula, exercises, personal correction and habits of study, although their methods differed among institutions.

The Khaybar endowment supplies a traceable line from embodied counsel to a durable legal and educational form. A landowner asks what to do with a valuable asset. The reported answer preserves the underlying property while directing its yield to specified beneficiaries. Its enduring effect calls for distinctions among ownership, administration, the founder's conditions, the rights of recipients and public adjudication. Each is a true aspect of the same purposeful undertaking. A later college supported by waqf involves another historical operation: the property must produce reliable income, managers must distribute it and students must receive instruction worth sustaining. Continuity of intention does not itself prove identity of legal structure or uninterrupted transmission between every institution.

The three practical scales are present from the beginning. Ethical formation concerns the donor's freedom from possessiveness; household governance concerns dependants and legitimate family claims; civic ordering concerns enforceable commitments and a reliable forum for dispute. A school then adds a fourth field of competence in the actual teaching of its subject. In the language of malakāt, the students' acquisition of habits and skills is an end for which the property is a means. A beautiful building or a large endowment can fail to yield this good. The measure of the institution is therefore the real instruction, character and service it makes possible, assessed through surviving documents and reports rather than an imagined direct descent from one archetypal form.

A student at an endowed college could receive a stipend, lodging and instruction tied to the founder's stipulations. The property yield might provide a regular stipend, which made years of study feasible; a founder's stipulations might also determine which legal school occupied the teaching position. A respected scholar might then receive students because of the office, because of established expertise or because both reinforced one another. Chamberlain's Damascus evidence is important because it permits those mechanisms to be separated. Endowments, appointments, scholarly households and personal teaching relationships shaped who could study and who obtained authority to teach. Teachers and students also disagreed over whether paid office and public prestige encouraged learning, competition or vanity. It cannot be answered by assuming every patron was either altruistic or self-interested.

### 30. Translation, theological controversy and the uses of reason

**Matn**

As Muslims developed different sciences, they also argued over what kinds of things could be known and what counted as proof. Debates about human action, divine attributes and the Qurʾān occupied theologians; translators brought logical and natural-philosophical writings into Arabic; jurists continued to reason about the grounds of obligation. The same educated person might enter several of these inquiries, yet each asked a different question.

A demonstration seeks conclusions warranted by its premises, a legal judgment considers authoritative sources and applicable circumstances, and spiritual discipline concerns the transformation of the knower. These modes can cooperate in a person's life without any one method becoming competent to decide every question. Trouble arises when the valid instrument of one inquiry is made sovereign over the rest.

Arguments also entered courts and palaces. During the miḥnah, state officials demanded conformity to a theological position about the Qurʾān. A controversy about doctrine thereby acquired the force of public examination and punishment. The power to compel an answer became visible as something different from the intellectual or religious warrant for the answer itself.

The rise of logic and kalām thus belonged both to the inner work of distinguishing kinds of knowledge and to the outer history of teaching and authority. Their lasting contributions depended on learned persons able to form judgments and on communities willing to contest error by appropriate means.

**Sharḥ**

Philosophers and theologians disputed whether created causes act with inviolable necessity and how such causation relates to miracles and divine agency. Different answers presuppose different accounts of nature, regularity and possibility. It would be a mistake to summarize all Ashʿarīs as holding a single identical philosophy of causation or to equate the whole of falsafah with denial of miracles. The problem is debated in al-Ghazālī's seventeenth discussion of the *Tahāfut*, but it had a longer prehistory in kalām and philosophical accounts of nature. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's 'Occasionalism' entry gives a useful map of disagreement even about how to read al-Ghazālī's ultimate position.

Arguments about God's attributes, moral obligation and human action also arise from different sources. Early Muʿtazilī positions are not interchangeable, and Sunni Ashʿarī and Māturīdī schools later developed their own internal debates. Ibn Sīnā's distinction between essence and existence and his proof of the necessary existent entered many later theological treatises, despite strong criticism of other Avicennan doctrines. The crucial historical change is not that 'Greek reason defeated' or 'Islam rejected reason.' Methods of inquiry moved across disciplines, were adapted to particular problems, and became objects of criticism by new authors. Primary controls include al-Ashʿarī's *Maqālāt*, al-Māturīdī's *Kitāb al-Tawḥīd*, al-Fārābī's works on the sciences, Ibn Sīnā's *Shifāʾ* and al-Ghazālī's *Tahāfut*. Differences in authorship, date and formulation can change the meaning of a philosophical proposition and the objections later commentators raise against it.

The plurality of proofs requires a corresponding plurality of trained judgments. A theologian's conclusion about an attribute of God, a philosopher's demonstration about nature and a jurist's ruling about a contract may all concern one person in his effort to live truthfully, yet the evidence and inference appropriate to each differ. The comprehensive knower can see how they relate without asserting that a legal precedent is a geometrical proof or that a spiritual experience entails the validity of a syllogism. The two eyes here signify attention to the ultimate orientation of knowledge and exactness about the object under investigation. Intellectual limitation becomes damaging when a legitimate method is treated as competent in every domain.

The miḥnah makes the distinction public. An official's power to demand a theological declaration, the arguments offered for the doctrine and the conscience of the person questioned are separate realities, even when they meet in one interrogation. Coercive success is not a criterion of truth. A scholar's virtue may strengthen willingness to defend a conviction while leaving the conviction itself answerable to evidence. Through repeated study and argument, logic and kalām form intellectual habits that allow later readers to distinguish premises, implications and scope. Their place in the larger inheritance is valuable because they perform this work well, not because they imitate the whole prophetic mission.

The *miḥnah* offers an early case of theological language becoming an instrument of public authority. During the *miḥnah*, caliphal officials demanded declarations about the Qurʾān's createdness, making conformity to a theological position a condition of public service. Muʿtazilī theologians developed arguments about divine justice and unity beyond the court's coercive policies; later Sunni theologians also employed rigorous dialectical argument. Later Sunni theologians used intricate logical methods while rejecting the imposition of a doctrine by a caliph. The controversy involved distinct claims about divine speech, legitimate proof and the ruler's authority to enforce a doctrinal declaration. These are distinct but interacting processes.

### 31. Al-Ghazālī, al-Rāzī and the remaking of philosophical theology

**Matn**

The expansion of learned disciplines made disputes over the boundaries of reason increasingly precise. Al-Ghazālī studied law, theology and philosophy and examined the claims of philosophers where they appeared to contradict authoritative religious teaching. He also pursued the purification of intention and the relation between acquired knowledge and a rightly ordered life. His work entered a network of teachers and readers who did not agree on every conclusion.

Ibn Rushd later answered some of al-Ghazālī's criticisms, while Ibn Sīnā's account of being, knowledge and causation continued to be discussed in commentaries. Al-Rāzī pressed objections to philosophical demonstrations; al-Ṭūsī developed replies that helped establish new paths of inquiry. The arguments passed through books and classrooms where exact distinctions mattered, including the relation between understanding what a thing is and judging that it exists.

These controversies were also exercises of distinct human powers. A thinker can discern a logical inconsistency while remaining morally vain; a spiritually sincere person can still make an invalid inference. The perfection of knowledge calls for demonstrative care where demonstration applies, fidelity to revelation where its authority governs, and cultivation of character in the person who studies.

The learned world inherited from the Prophetic life more than a catalogue of statements. It inherited the responsibility to give different kinds of understanding their rightful scope and to form people capable of using them together. The history of philosophical theology records how that task was repeatedly disputed and renewed.

**Sharḥ**

These are two interacting but distinct intellectual histories. In *Tahāfut al-falāsifah*, al-Ghazālī objects to identifiable claims associated with the philosophers, including an argument about causation and miracle; Ibn Rushd's *Tahāfut al-tahāfut* responds to the critique. The disagreement cannot be reconstructed adequately by the bare sentence 'reason and revelation were reconciled' or by assigning one universal causal theory to al-Ghazālī. His *Iḥyāʾ* is another undertaking, concerned with the ordering of worship, everyday action, destructive motives and saving virtues. Al-Ghazālī's critique of philosophical theses and his treatment of worship and character pursued related concerns within different genres and arguments.

A second history develops through the criticism of Ibn Sīnā's *al-Ishārāt*. Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī uses detailed commentary to expose problems in essence, existence, definition and demonstration; al-Ṭūsī's replies help establish a continuing debate. The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry on al-Rāzī documents particular positions and the care needed in inferring his considered conclusions from dialectical writings. Their commentaries preserved technical disputes about essence, existence and demonstration through successive generations of study. Commentaries became intellectual instruments: by setting out a lemma, objection, response and further objection, they required students to inhabit the logic of a question before accepting a resolution. That is relevant to the larger history of formation because the discipline of judgment can be cultivated through argument as well as through repeated moral actions. The achievements and failures of those pedagogic practices must still be judged from actual texts and institutions, not an idealization of scholasticism.

Philosophical theology stages an inquiry into the difference between a thing's intelligible meaning, the judgment that it exists and the ultimate ground of existence. The fact that a definition is understood does not in itself establish an existent instance; proofs about causation and necessity require their own premises. Contemplation of God as the Source of all being introduces a further order of metaphysical question. These distinctions matter for comparing Avicennan, Ashʿarī and Akbarian formulations: a difference of aspect within one consideration is not automatically an ontological division into several existents, and the language of Being must not be used as a substitute for the Absolute beyond determination.

The controversy also concerns the formation of the one who knows. Al-Ghazālī's criticism of philosophical theses, al-Rāzī's searching questions and al-Ṭūsī's replies each demand precision about the argument being made. Spiritual discipline can restrain vanity and expand receptivity, but a sound inference still depends on exact terms and valid reasoning. Conversely, demonstrative skill does not guarantee just treatment of students or sincerity in worship. The complete life joins these real perfections through action while preserving their distinctive methods. This provides a more rigorous horizon for studying the relation of discursive knowledge, spiritual tasting and the ethical cultivation of the scholar.

The dispute over essence and existence turned on what definitions establish about things outside the mind. Ibn Sīnā argues that a thing's essence can be understood without thereby affirming its existence; al-Rāzī questions what such a distinction establishes about real beings and whether a definition yields knowledge of an external essence. The reply cannot be assessed by saying merely that the later thinker 'synthesized' theology and philosophy. Al-Rāzī's dialectical writings sometimes test a thesis without endorsing it, so particular lemmas and objections determine which position he actually adopts. The later classroom practice of commenting line by line preserved these difficulties for students. It also introduced a risk of treating subtle distinctions as verbal contests detached from demonstration; whether that happened is a question about particular curricula and commentaries, not a verdict on late scholasticism as a whole.

### 32. The work of the heart becomes a teachable discipline

**Matn**

Believers could perform visible obligations while remaining troubled by envy, anger, pride or attachment to reputation. Teachers of the heart sought to make attention to intention and conduct a sustained discipline. They drew upon Qurʾānic commands, reports of the first generations and practical exercises in generosity, restraint, remembrance and service.

Al-Ghazālī, a jurist and theological author who also wrote profoundly about the life of the heart, later arranged a major work around worship, everyday customs, destructive dispositions and saving virtues. That arrangement made teachable the relation between outward obligation and inner cultivation without treating them as the same competence. The preservation of accurate instruction still depended upon teachers who knew how to correct prayer, transactions and conduct in the different settings where their students lived.

Repeated action shaped capacities. Someone who regularly returns property to its owner becomes more ready to act truthfully; a person who trains attention in prayer becomes better able to recognize distraction and recover focus. Such habits are learned through a body, a household and relationships that supply both difficulties and correction. A teacher's guidance becomes effective through practice rather than mere admiration.

Spiritual writers also described modes of knowing and presence whose meaning exceeds acquired habit. A virtuous disposition can be cultivated deliberately; deeper realization is received and understood through a distinct spiritual inquiry. Their relation does not authorize a claim that every visible act of discipline proves an inward state. People remain responsible for the concrete rights of others whatever experiences they report.

The emerging disciplines of purification carried one aspect of prophetic guidance into a teachable form. Their historical strength depended on trustworthy persons who embodied the virtues they taught, families and institutions that supported continuing practice, and vigilance against turning an inward path into public self-importance.

**Sharḥ**

Al-Ghazālī's *Iḥyāʾ* supplies an unusually clear architecture of the question: devotional acts, habits of ordinary living, qualities that destroy and qualities that save. Qurʾān 91:7–10 concerns purification of the soul, 107:4–7 rebukes ostentatious worship, and the ḥadīth of intention relates actions to their purposes. A jurist might determine whether a required donation was validly made, while a teacher of the heart could ask whether the giver sought recognition and became hostile when unnoticed. They need not issue contradictory verdicts: validity, motive and character are different objects of examination. Al-Muḥāsibī's *al-Riʿāyah* pursues self-monitoring, and al-Qushayrī's *Risālah* records a vocabulary of states and stations. Al-Muḥāsibī, al-Qushayrī and al-Ghazālī developed different analyses of intention, spiritual states and disciplined conduct in their respective works.

The category of *malakah* can help explain the historical pedagogy, provided it is not attributed indiscriminately to the first Companions. Repeated action can establish ease and stability, but a habit formed through vanity is not the same thing as a virtue. The spiritual author therefore asks about knowledge, intention, repeated practice and the social circumstances that reinforce or undermine a disposition. This bridges the Sīrah's early concern with prayer and mutual obligation to later manuals without pretending that the manuals already existed in the Prophet's lifetime. Prayer, self-examination and companionship developed distinctive vocabularies as teachers addressed recurring problems of intention, action and conduct.

The discipline of self-purification connects acquired virtues with a more comprehensive receptivity without erasing their difference. A person repeatedly exercises patience, truthful speech or self-examination until the act becomes less impeded by contrary appetite. This is takhalluq in the sense of cultivating character, and its durability can be understood through malakāt. The resulting capacity is real: it changes how the person responds to a spouse, subordinate or stranger. Yet a perfected outward habit is not itself evidence of every claimed state of unveiling or realization. Tahaqquq concerns a different depth of presence and understanding whose recognition requires a careful spiritual framework.

The two eyes have a daily application here. The worshipper relates conduct to God and simultaneously perceives the particular claim of the person before him. The judge resists pride not by abandoning evidence but by hearing more accurately; the merchant relinquishes greed while still calculating the fair price; the parent controls anger by recognizing a child's dependence. Spiritual education becomes embodied when the return from contemplation alters these decisions. A monastery, madrasa or lodge may provide repeated practices, teachers and support, but the quality of realized action remains answerable to its actual human effects. The Prophet's comprehensive example is transmitted through these partial modes without making the disciplinary map identical with the spiritual state to which it points.

The practice of self-examination takes place under conditions that can foster or frustrate it. A wealthy person may have more opportunities to give but also more occasions for display; a judge may face a temptation to regard being right as proof of being virtuous; a scholar may confuse mastery of a text with self-knowledge. Al-Ghazālī's discussion of blameworthy traits becomes relevant to such people precisely because their ordinary roles carry moral risks. Al-Ghazālī treated prayer, trade, social duties and concealed motives as connected aspects of moral formation. Early warnings against vanity do not establish an unbroken institutional lineage to later Sufi lodges. The stronger connection is the persistence of concrete religious problems and documented practices through which teachers addressed them.

Al-Ghazālī's own preface to the *Iḥyāʾ* explains why he combines worship, ordinary dealings, destructive qualities and saving qualities: the visible action and the condition of the agent are complementary objects of *ʿilm al-muʿāmalah*. Thus a person's prayer, debt, household conduct and anger all become occasions for diagnosing the relation between knowledge, intention and action. Al-Qushayrī's discussion of *ikhlāṣ* distinguishes acting for other people's approval from admiring oneself for acting well; Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh's aphorism gives concise form to the difference between the outward configuration of an act and its animating sincerity. Their terminology is later; their texts help explain why the history of religious practice generated disciplines of moral formation. ### 33. From the teacher's company to enduring Sufi networks

**Matn**

Seekers gathered around teachers whose recitation, conduct and prayer gave them confidence in learning. Some remained in informal companionship; others travelled or settled in lodges sustained by gifts and endowments. Over generations, lineages of instruction acquired recognizable forms, routes and expectations. Such arrangements allowed spiritual practice to be carried beyond the life of one teacher.

Some communities traced their practice through the Qādirī and Suhrawardī lines, while in Egypt and North Africa the guidance associated with Abū al-Ḥasan al-Shādhilī reached later generations through companions and writers. The remembered teaching placed trust in God within ordinary livelihood and responsibility. Other authors investigated prophetic wisdom in a more technical metaphysical idiom. These overlapping worlds produced different forms of spiritual education, each dependent on a teacher's knowledge and students able to embody the instruction.

The company of a guide could correct faults that a solitary reader failed to see. Repetition of prayer or remembrance trained attention, while ordinary work with family and neighbours tested whether mercy, restraint and truthfulness had become stable qualities. Teachers also spoke of deeper realization in which knowledge of God was received with a transformed presence. The learned habit and the realized state belonged to related but distinct inquiries.

The comprehensive prophetic vocation supplied a measure for this differentiated inheritance. Some successors preserved reports, some learned to judge actions, others trained devotion or contemplation. Their respective attainments could meet in a person, but no lineage of teachers took possession of the Prophet's unique revelatory office. Claims about realized states required discernment within their own tradition, just as legal opinions required argument and historical genealogies required evidence.

As Sufi networks spread, their houses and persons also entered markets, courts and political conflicts. Service, endowment, patronage and contention shaped their public lives. The spiritual inheritance became historically effective through the truthfulness of particular teachers and through the contingent institutions that enabled their work.

**Sharḥ**

Alexander Knysh, in *The New Cambridge History of Islam*, and Devin DeWeese's study of Sufi organizational patterns distinguish early ascetic circles from the diverse forms of *ṭarīqah* that acquired more durable organization from the twelfth century onward. Authority could descend through personal initiation, family succession or local custom, and a lodge might serve worship, hospitality, storage or public prestige. A lineage claim is a historical claim requiring examination; a devotional chain is not automatically a documentary record of uninterrupted administrative continuity.

The Akbarian account brings the history of spiritual formation back to the comprehensive prophetic original. Ibn ʿArabī distinguishes inheritance through works, states and knowledge, as summarized by [William Chittick](https://www.persee.fr/doc/horma_0984-2616_1996_num_30_1_1371). The dimensions belong together in a perfected servant: correct activity expresses knowledge, and inward realization deepens the manner of serving actual persons. A spiritual teacher may help a disciple acquire disciplined practice and self-knowledge through companionship, while divine opening remains a gift.

The later Akbarian doctrine of prophetic inheritance provides a principial account of why spiritual life becomes differentiated. Ibn ʿArabī distinguishes inheritance through works, states and knowledge; al-Qūnawī's account of a station not limited by any one station helps describe the relation between a comprehensive spiritual centre and more restricted attainments. Farghānī's elaborations of the Muhammadan Reality and al-Mahāʾimī's distinctions among character-acquisition and realization represent later interpretive developments with their own technical claims. They should be read as forms of spiritual explanation rather than silently ascribed to the first generations. None of the later claimants inherits prophetic revelation in its unique final commission.

Companionship is a mode of mediation because a human guide can adapt a word or correction to the actual disciple before him. It joins knowledge of the spiritual end to a judgment concerning this person's temperament, family duties and present capacity. That movement resembles the barzakhic coordination of levels while remaining dependent on the guide's real competence and rectitude. Its effects must be judged in actual relationships, including the treatment of dependants and the exercise of authority. Historical Sufi lineages and lodges added means for preserving practices over time; their endowments, patronage and organization had contingent causes. The institution can transmit a work, a practice or a memory of a state without by that fact possessing the inward reality of its founder.

Al-Qūnawī's discussions of the *maqām lā maqām* concern realization that encompasses the valid modes of differentiated spiritual stations. Farghānī describes the Prophet's speech, character and bodily actions as the enacted comprehensive Qurʾān. The later saint or scholar participates according to his bestowed capacity. His true station is revealed through the responsible use of the knowledge, rights and relationships entrusted to him. The historical formation of Sufi teaching networks requires its own dates, people, endowments, texts and transmission routes; these form the material conditions through which companionship and instruction remain available.

The Shādhilī teachers addressed remembrance and reliance upon God in the circumstances of earning, family life and human relationships. Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh's *al-Ḥikam* and *Laṭāʾif al-minan* do not simply prescribe withdrawal: they address the intentions and misconceptions of people who must act, earn and encounter others. They treated ordinary duties as occasions for testing intention, reliance and remembrance. Ibn ʿArabī's *Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam*, by contrast, assigns distinctive wisdoms to prophets and asks about the relation between divine self-disclosure and human realization; Qūnawī and Farghānī develop this speculative vocabulary further. The Akbarian discussions of Muhammadan reality concern prophetic wisdom, metaphysical disclosure and spiritual realization, not merely commendable behaviour. Spiritual transmission had become a set of institutions and an intellectual tradition; their historical emergence and the truth of their metaphysical claims are distinguishable questions. Sufi teaching lineages developed historically through identifiable teachers, while Akbarian texts elaborated metaphysical accounts whose claims require separate philosophical argument.

A second distinction keeps the history accurate. Spiritual practices such as remembrance and self-accounting, the institutional form of a later *ṭarīqah*, and a metaphysical doctrine of *walāyah* are not interchangeable. Ibn ʿArabī discusses command, station and witnessing within a theory of realization; Qūnawī's *Miftāḥ al-ghayb* develops an ontological treatment of *wujūd*; Farghānī later systematizes the Muhammadan Reality in his commentary on Ibn al-Fāriḍ. The relevant sequence is a documented development of teaching and concepts, not evidence that every early community member used the later terms. [Hakim on *walāyah*](https://ibnarabisociety.org/the-way-of-walaya-souad-hakim/); [Chittick's translation of Qūnawī](https://ibnarabisociety.org/qunawi-on-the-one-wujud-william-chittick/); [SEP on Ibn ʿArabī](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ibn-arabi/).

## Book VI — An international civilization with many centres

### 34. Cairo, Damascus and Jerusalem: government, learning and the Crusades

**Matn**

Cairo, Damascus and Jerusalem lay within overlapping worlds of learning, commerce and armed conflict. The Fāṭimids founded Cairo in 969 and supported institutions connected with their religious and dynastic claims. Later rulers in Syria and Egypt confronted the Latin crusading states while managing rival courts, soldiers, revenues and cities with Muslim, Christian and Jewish inhabitants. Saladin's campaigns and the recapture of Jerusalem in 1187 became part of a larger political and devotional memory.

Nūr al-Dīn, the ruler who developed power in Syria before Saladin's ascendancy, supported institutions of learning and public administration alongside campaigns against competing states. Saladin later assembled military and fiscal resources across Egypt and Syria. Their decisions were received through armies, advisers, officials and urban communities. The restoration or protection of a city required the outward means of rule, while the teaching of its inhabitants continued through persons whose authority rested upon education and trust.

A city could receive a scholar from another state and still remain under a ruler with different interests. Teachers, judges, physicians and merchants moved through routes that fighting sometimes interrupted and sometimes left open. Schools and endowments supported the acquisition of knowledge, while armies needed revenue, equipment and disciplined command. The duties of worship and justice were fulfilled under these changing material conditions.

Religious unity was enacted through shared practices and learned texts even where sovereign power was divided. That fellowship did not erase differences among legal doctrines, confessions or local customs; it provided occasions on which particular people recognized one another's claims. Spiritual teaching could cross a frontier without transferring political allegiance.

The history of these cities shows several forms of authority acting at once. Rule protected or coerced; learning persuaded and corrected; institutions supplied food, teaching and charity; households maintained life across upheaval. The adequacy of a public order was tested in how these relations were governed together.

**Sharḥ**

Muslim and Christian rulers fought, negotiated truces, exchanged envoys and traded with one another; neither population acted as one undifferentiated political bloc. The Latin principalities interacted with diverse eastern Christian communities and with Muslim dynasties that themselves fought rivals. Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn's military success depended on prior Syrian consolidation, control of Egypt's resources and treaties with local powers. Ibn al-Athīr, Ibn Shaddād and Latin chroniclers preserve accounts of the campaigns from differing political and religious positions. No single court chronicle is a transparent record of all participants.

This case also clarifies what an 'international Islamic society' meant. A traveler passing from Cairo to Damascus encountered different rulers but could find Arabic books, legal teachers, markets, hospitals and spiritual guides across the frontier. Political fragmentation sometimes increased patronage competition. Fāṭimid Ismāʿīlī institutions and Sunni endowments promoted different understandings of religious authority while sharing some urban and administrative forms. The coexistence of disagreement and common social practices needs explanation, not rhetorical celebration. Crusading warfare shaped taxation, fortification, migration and religious preaching; it did not create the scholarly networks from nothing. For orientation consult the *New Cambridge History of Islam*, volumes 2 and 3, and specialist biographies of Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn.

The city is a useful scale for applying the distinction between comprehensive orientation and specialized institutions. Cairo's religious colleges, a Damascene court, a military garrison and a hospital might all serve people who worshipped and understood themselves as part of a wider community. They nevertheless operated through different methods of certification, payment and decision. A judge's ruling does not settle a physician's diagnosis, and a sultan's sponsorship does not establish a teacher's credibility. The integration of such competences requires a public order attentive to the goods they actually accomplish for persons and households. The return from spiritual ideals to civic action thus takes the form of budgets, schools, roads, hospitals and rights defended through reliable procedure.

The conflicts surrounding the Crusades also warn against treating religious identity as a sufficient explanation of military behaviour. Commanders negotiated truces and alliances amid divergent strategic interests; different Christian and Muslim populations had their own local histories. Prophetic principles of fidelity and justice can illuminate an appraisal of conduct, but the historian must establish what was promised, breached or defended through appropriate sources. Shared worship and scholarship linked multiple Muslim centres even without a single sovereign will. The historical unity of a civilization is therefore a nexus of learned practices, trade, devotional relations and institutions rather than an empire imagined as one undifferentiated person.

Al-Azhar's institutional history began in Fatimid Cairo and changed under subsequent governments and scholarly communities. Founded in Fatimid Cairo, the mosque became associated with a particular ruling dynasty's religious and educational project; its subsequent life passed through different political and doctrinal settings. It would be inaccurate to describe every later Egyptian scholarly institution as simply a continuation of Fatimid propaganda, yet a building's history can register changes in authority that a list of dynasties conceals. Similarly, military and charitable endowments under Nūr al-Dīn and Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn tied urban property to public aims, while actual teachers and students used those places in diverse ways. The wars shaped what cities could finance and whom they could protect. Endowed institutions financed teaching under differing patrons, just as individual campaigns depended on particular alliances, revenues and military aims.

### 35. Al-Andalus and the Maghrib: cities, scholarship and the loss of a political world

**Matn**

A survivor of the Umayyad overthrow established a ruling house in Córdoba in 756. Over centuries al-Andalus became a western centre of government, agriculture, crafts and scholarship, linked to cities of the Maghrib across the strait. Courts sponsored building and learning, while households and teachers carried religious practice through periods of political stability and division. The western caliphate later broke into competing principalities.

Western Muslim society passed through the rule of the Almoravids and Almohads, as well as the earlier Andalusian dynasties, each with different religious and administrative projects. Legal traditions, especially Mālikī learning, moved between towns of North Africa and the Iberian Peninsula through students and books. A learned form could thus travel across changing political borders while the particular communities and languages that had sustained it in one place remained exposed to violence and loss.

Power passed through new dynasties and alliances, and Christian kingdoms gradually took more territory. Granada fell in 1492, followed by coercive measures that altered and often destroyed the conditions for Muslim and Jewish communal life in Iberia. Families faced conversion, displacement or clandestine practice. Books, knowledge and customs travelled with those able to leave, while other forms of life were lost.

A jurist's authority over a question of worship did not require the possession of a Muslim throne, though it did require students and opportunities to teach. The transmission of language, ritual and skilled crafts could survive particular governments through households and networks, and it could also fail under sustained coercion. The difference between a spiritual form and its political shelter became painfully visible.

Western Muslim civilization developed through real cooperation among rulers, workers, merchants and scholars. Its cultural and intellectual attainments were not the property of one office. Their continuities and losses must be understood through the actual people and institutions that carried them.

**Sharḥ**

The institutional trajectory is more informative than a rise-and-fall label. Umayyad rule in Córdoba was initially an emirate and only later a caliphate; the caliphal order ended in 1031, after which different *ṭawāʾif* competed for revenues, military support and legitimacy. Almoravid and Almohad movements emerged within North African conditions and intervened in Iberian politics, bringing their own doctrines and ruling practices. The Nasrid regime at Granada persisted for more than two centuries while balancing warfare, diplomacy, taxation and economic relations with its neighbours. María Viguera-Molins describes the collapse of the Cordoban caliphate in 1031 and the subsequent competing kingdoms, while archaeological studies document the continuing urban and agricultural life of al-Andalus.

Learning followed related but distinct pathways. The *Muwaṭṭaʾ* circulated in versions associated with western transmitters; Mālikī jurists acquired durable authority in the Maghrib and al-Andalus. Scholars could write Arabic commentaries and teach across the straits even when the regions were politically divided. Ibn Rushd's philosophical and legal writings belong to this western history, while Ibn ʿArabī's early life in al-Andalus and later movement eastward illustrates a transregional scholarly-spiritual trajectory. Ibn Rushd and Ibn ʿArabī pursued distinct philosophical and spiritual projects within the changing political and scholarly conditions of al-Andalus.

The destruction of a political shelter and the survival of some forms of guidance can occur together. A learned family expelled from Granada might preserve recitation, law or Arabic texts in another city; a house remaining under coercive rule might sustain prayer privately for a time. These are real continuities carried through formed persons, not evidence that loss was illusory. Physical displacement can also sever teaching, erase manuscripts and impoverish future generations. The formal distinction between a spiritual inheritance and the institution that protects it helps identify both enduring possibilities and actual destruction.

The western case also gives the three practical orders historical scale. Political rule determines public security and sometimes imposes coercion; households protect dependants and transmit practices through generations; persons train conscience and scholarship in circumstances partly outside their choice. Their relationships change under each dynasty. A jurisprudential tradition may travel through manuscripts and teachers across the strait, but specific connections require evidence from dates, licenses, copied works and legal practice. The spiritual horizon can explain why living knowledge is valued through these changes without making the varied fortunes of al-Andalus a simple allegory of rise and decline.

Christian, Jewish and Muslim relations varied across cities, institutions and centuries. The concept *convivencia* is a subject of historiographical debate, not a license to erase coercion or shared daily life. The forced conversions imposed after Granada and the expulsion of Moriscos during 1609–14 are historically distinct from the conquest of 1492 itself. Communities and books displaced toward the Maghrib could survive political collapse, yet the material cost to families was severe. Archaeology, local documents, Muslim and Christian chronicles and scholarly biographies preserve different evidence for political change, religious practice and learning in the western Mediterranean.

### 36. Persianate language, commerce and the circulation of books

**Matn**

Arabic served as the language of revelation and much scholarly work across Muslim societies. Persian also became a great language of literature, ethics, administration and spiritual teaching in Iran and Central Asia. Secretaries, poets, scholars and travellers carried texts among courts and households; merchants moved across the same regions with goods, letters and news. Different languages allowed meanings to reach audiences through distinct forms of expression.

In Mughal India, the prince Dārā Shikūh cultivated conversations across Persianate and Indian learned worlds and sponsored translations associated with the Upaniṣads. Such work required interpreters trained in more than one intellectual vocabulary and belonged to a particular courtly setting. It illustrates both the possibilities of encounter and the difficulty of determining when terms in different traditions truly name comparable things. The existence of a translation is evidence of a meeting of languages; the philosophical equivalence of the translated doctrines remains an argument to be made.

A work copied in a Persian-speaking city might be read in India by someone who knew both Persian and Arabic, taught to students and eventually joined to local practices. Such a journey required books, teachers, transport and continued interest. A resemblance between two teachings could invite comparison, but documented transmission depended on owners, citations and known routes of copying.

Poetry and narrative could cultivate attention to justice, beauty, loyalty or the dangers of pride. Their practical effects depended on interpretation and the conduct of those who learned from them. Philosophical arguments, devotional verse and juristic formulas had different warrants even when they appeared together in the education of one person.

The growth of a Persianate learned world enlarged the ways religious and moral reflection could be expressed. Its unity was sustained through recognizable ends and relationships among traditions of learning, while its variety followed the histories of languages, regions, patrons and audiences.

**Sharḥ**

The historian Marshall Hodgson distinguishes Islam as a religion from the broader Islamicate culture of poetry, commerce and royal architecture; some authors nonetheless understood those cultural practices as means of religious realization. His categories themselves require qualification, especially where authors understood philosophy or poetry as an integral way of realizing religious truth. Shahab Ahmed argues that many Muslim authors understood philosophy, poetry and other Islamicate practices as integral to their religious lives.

Manuscript colophons, ownership records, citations and teaching certificates document particular routes by which texts passed among scholars. Niẓāmī's Persian narratives, Saʿdī's ethical works, or the legal and Sufi texts carried to South Asia are possible case studies, but their particular routes cannot be assumed from later popularity. Manuscript dates, ownership notes, colophons, reading certificates and library inventories provide stronger evidence. The intellectual consequence of bilingual learning also deserves attention: a technical Arabic discipline could be explained in Persian and eventually local vernaculars, changing who could enter a tradition without necessarily changing the tradition's formal terms. Such processes explain the civilization's adaptability more concretely than the phrase 'cultural synthesis.'

Translation between Arabic, Persian and later South Asian languages makes the relation of meaning and form visible through a strictly human medium. A concept can receive new vocabulary and literary surroundings while retaining some of its intelligibility; other features may be lost or transformed. The responsible reader must distinguish genuine shared meaning from a misleading lexical resemblance. An ethical poem may move desire and imagination; a legal text makes determinate claims about action; a metaphysical treatise argues for a view of reality. Their different powers are not convertible into one uniform category called 'culture.'

The work of reception occurs through actual persons. A scholar must know the languages, a student must be formed to ask appropriate questions, and a teacher may have to reconcile a new text with existing duties and institutions. The one person's intellectual life is potentially comprehensive precisely because these capacities remain distinct while being coordinated. A ruler's patronage or a merchant's transport can aid this work without proving the truth of any text carried. Provenance, copying notes, citations and teaching certificates must establish the historical routes through which Persianate materials entered new contexts. The spiritual claim of unity and the empirical claim of transmission belong to related but different orders of explanation.

One route of inquiry is through the reconstruction of a book's movement. A Persian text copied at one court might reach India with a secretary, be read in a learned household and be cited in a later ethical manual. A documented textual route requires dated manuscripts, ownership notes, biographical notices or explicit citations; similar vocabulary alone does not establish transmission. Literary Persian also provided an intermediary language for local translations and commentaries. Students who learned Arabic legal terminology through Persian explanations could participate in a wider scholarly community while continuing to speak vernacular languages at home. Persian also mediated technical learning in administration, law and spiritual instruction among multilingual communities. It is about the practical mediation by which technical learning became teachable across regions without requiring every learner to begin with the same mother tongue.

### 37. Mongol conquest, conversion and the survival of scholarly worlds

**Matn**

Mongol armies overran extensive parts of Central and Western Asia in the thirteenth century, destroying cities and displacing people. Baghdad fell in 1258, and the Abbasid court there came to an end. These events shattered political structures and damaged libraries, irrigated lands and learned households. Other centres continued to teach, while rulers in Mongol successor states later entered Islam through distinct personal and dynastic histories.

Ghāzān, who ruled the Ilkhanate near the end of the thirteenth century, publicly adopted Islam and supported reforms and institutions associated with the new religious orientation of his court. Earlier Mongol rulers and soldiers had already entered Islam through different routes. The histories of rulers, military households and settled populations therefore had to be followed separately: change at the court could make patronage available without having immediately formed the habits or convictions of every village.

The conversion of a ruler did not mean that all his subjects acquired the same practices overnight. Teachers, merchants and Sufi communities participated in the formation of new religious relations, while state patronage supported some forms of instruction. The reconstruction of a mosque or college depended on resources and labour, and the restoration of trust took longer than an accession proclamation.

Knowledge survived through copied books, students, family memory and endowments where these remained effective. Some transmissions ceased through death and destruction, leaving genuine losses that no later narrative of renewal could undo. In other places, new networks formed and learning travelled along altered political routes.

The distinction between spiritual continuity and political survival becomes clear in this history. A religious practice can outlive its former protector through the work of living persons, but its endurance remains vulnerable to the material world in which those persons must teach, travel and earn their bread.

**Sharḥ**

The difference between a dynasty's conversion and a population's religious transformation is essential. The historian Peter Jackson's *The Mongols and the Islamic World* and the *Encyclopaedia Iranica* account of the Mongols trace the varied trajectories of the Ilkhanids, Jochids and Central Asian khanates. Aḥmad Tegüder had already ruled as a Muslim Ilkhan; Ghāzān's accession later established a more durable public Islamic orientation at the court. Rashīd al-Dīn wrote under Ilkhanid patronage, while Mamluk and other chroniclers recorded the same conflicts through different political allegiances. Motives given to a conqueror by an official chronicler are evidence for political representation as much as for private conviction.

The mechanism of institutional persistence is visible in Cairo and Iran. A *waqf* could support a school across changes of dynasty, but endowments might be ruined or reallocated; a jurist's students could copy his work even when his patron was defeated. Mongol administrators also adopted Persian fiscal and literary practices for practical reasons. An Ibn Khaldūnian model can illuminate the transition from conqueror solidarity to the difficulties of administering settled societies, while local documents decide how far the analogy holds. After the Mongol wars, scholars continued to produce works in theology, logic, astronomy and Sufi thought, although warfare altered their centres, resources and patrons.

The Mongol age shows the difference between a public conversion, the spiritual formation of persons and the rebuilding of institutions. A ruler's declaration can alter the official language of power, the distribution of patronage and public ritual at court. The household and village still acquire practices through teachers and actual repeated worship, while a ruined college requires land, materials and administrators before students can meet again. These changes have different temporal scales. The realized inward state of an individual is also not a statistical consequence of the religious affiliation of a ruling house.

The idea of prophetic inheritance clarifies why learning survived in some places despite political destruction: transmission can be sustained by a student, a copyist, a learned family or a spiritual companion when imperial office collapses. It also clarifies what was lost: books, teachers and opportunities are necessary mediating forms for people who live under created conditions. Historical inquiry must document particular lines of survival; a metaphysical assertion about the permanence of truth does not demonstrate the physical survival of any manuscript or the innocence of those who destroyed it. In this setting the return from higher orientation to service meant feeding refugees, restoring teaching and establishing justice amid the real aftermath of violence.

A later chronicler who says that Ghāzān's accession brought Islam to the Ilkhanate is reporting a major change in public royal identity, not the conversion of everyone under his rule. Aḥmad Tegüder and Muslim Mongol soldiers preceded Ghāzān's accession and therefore demonstrate earlier Islamic affiliations within Ilkhanid society. Later Mongol rulers' patronage of scholars, foundations and monuments also served political purposes, but religious conviction cannot be disproved merely by identifying those interests. The contrasting Mamluk and Ilkhanid reports should therefore be compared as partisan accounts of a contested transformation. Courts, colleges, manuscripts and commercial routes carried learned and devotional practices through changing dynastic conditions. Mongol campaigns devastated cities and scholarly households, while surviving people and institutions later rebuilt teaching and textual circulation under new rulers.

### 38. The Middle Niger: markets, kings and independent scholars

**Matn**

Across the Sahara, merchants carried salt, textiles and other goods between North Africa and the communities of the Middle Niger. The rulers of Mali and Songhay acquired different relationships with Islamic learning, trade and local ritual life. Towns such as Timbuktu drew teachers, judges, merchants and families whose interests sometimes met and sometimes diverged. Religious life developed through the work of these people rather than through one imperial command.

The pilgrimage of the ruler Mansa Mūsā in 1324 brought Mali's political wealth and religious connections to audiences far beyond the Niger region. Later Songhay rulers, including Askia Muḥammad, cultivated their own relationships with Muslim learning. The remembered careers of these rulers must be distinguished from the writings of scholars in Timbuktu who acquired authority through study, teaching and the settlement of actual disputes.

A merchant's trust could make a journey possible; a scholar's teaching required language, books and a recognized competence; a family maintained prayer and care through its own routines. Patrons and endowments supplied some learned households with resources, while rulers could protect or threaten them. A legal judgment was effective only where parties accepted its authority or public institutions could enforce it.

When Moroccan forces intervened at the end of the sixteenth century, learned people experienced arrest, displacement and new political demands. The scholar Aḥmad Bābā's life and writings bear witness to the interaction of independence, learning and coercion. The history of manuscripts and teaching circles discloses links with wider Muslim intellectual life while preserving the particular forms of West African society.

Islamic civilization here had its own centres and histories. Its growth cannot be measured solely by the decisions of an eastern caliph or a trans-Saharan ruler; it took shape through the cultivated abilities of particular persons and the enduring institutions they sustained.

**Sharḥ**

The *Tārīkh al-Sūdān*, learned biographies, manuscripts, archaeological evidence and reports by travellers such as Ibn Baṭṭūṭah preserve different aspects of the Middle Niger's history. Zachary Wright traces West African scholarly authority through teaching communities and inherited learned relationships, which did not depend wholly on appointment by royal courts. Northwestern University's West African Arabic manuscript collections and catalogues also make local textual production accessible.

Timbuktu supported learned families, libraries and teaching circles whose organization differed from modern universities; surviving manuscript inventories vary greatly in age and provenance. The age, provenance and content of particular libraries need separate examination. A study of 31 private libraries found variation in the age and provenance of collections, limiting claims of uninterrupted scholarly transmission across every household. The surviving libraries preserve evidence of local scholarship but differ in the periods and networks they document. Trade supported towns and literacy; rulers patronized institutions; scholars organized teaching and defended authority within those changing conditions. West African scholars used Arabic and Mālikī learning within local commercial, political and educational arrangements, adapting inherited disciplines to their own institutions.

The Middle Niger gives an account of Islamic participation that cannot be reduced to a centre transmitting ready-made institutions to passive margins. A local scholar acquired knowledge through defined teachers, mastered texts and gave opinions responsive to the rights of people around him. A merchant negotiated trust across linguistic and political boundaries; a ruler exercised power over security and revenue; a parent raised children within household responsibilities. Each activity could take guidance from a shared religious orientation while having its own practical method and regional history. Their cooperation generated real learned communities capable of producing original scholarship.

The case of Aḥmad Bābā must be read in the context of the Moroccan occupation of Songhay territories and the coercion experienced by scholars. His writings and movement bear witness to an intellectual life that could contest political power rather than simply derive authority from it. Manuscript ownership, copying and correspondence can demonstrate links among West African and North African networks, while a generalized image of 'Islamization through trade' cannot establish what an individual community believed or when it adopted a practice. Spiritual continuity here was mediated by actual habits, words, books and relations of trust under changing local governments.

Aḥmad Bābā (d. 1627) wrote on Mālikī learning and scholarly biographies, and his career was disrupted by Moroccan intervention in Timbuktu. His works on Mālikī scholarship and learned biographies belong to a world that maintained ties with North Africa; his career was also affected by Moroccan intervention in Songhay and the removal of scholars from Timbuktu. Aḥmad Bābā's scholarly career and forced removal after Moroccan intervention connected the independence of learned households with struggles over political power. The surviving works document learned circles and libraries in particular towns; they do not establish Cairo-style colleges everywhere or a uniform date for the manuscript collections. Their ownership and contents vary. Dated manuscripts and biographical records document particular West African scholars and libraries without establishing identical educational institutions in every town.

### 39. Across the monsoon seas: ports, households and the Malay world

**Matn**

Seasonal winds linked ports in the Red Sea, East Africa, India and Southeast Asia long before European companies acquired control of particular harbours. Muslim merchants sailed among people of many religions and languages, relying on pilots, ships, credit and agreements with local rulers. Some settled abroad and entered households whose children grew up within several linguistic and commercial worlds.

Harbour officers in Melaka, often designated by the title syahbandar, helped visiting traders navigate customs obligations, storage and protection. Their offices served a public commercial need, while resident teachers and families sustained different forms of continuity. A port could accommodate merchants of many religions and languages without instantly becoming a unified religious community. Long-term settlement and the education of children gave such places histories deeper than one exchange of goods.

A trading town required weights, storage, customs collection and protection from violence. In Melaka, harbour officials dealt with visiting merchants while local families maintained places of worship and instruction. Trust in a partner could secure a shipment, but the education of a child or settlement of a family dispute required different relations. Teachers and religious specialists moved through the same network without simply becoming agents of trade.

The adoption of Islamic practices across the Malay world followed distinct local histories. Court patronage, family life, scholarly travel and devotional communities contributed in different measures; no single merchant's journey establishes the origin of an entire population's faith. The exchange of books and people made broader learning available while local institutions determined how it was received.

Ports reveal the practical relation between a common religious orientation and diverse forms of life. The same coastal settlement could be a commercial market, a home and a place of prayer; each had its own conditions, and their ordered cooperation made enduring communities possible.

**Sharḥ**

Kenneth Hall's study of the eastern Indian Ocean documents Melaka's warehouses, resident merchants, harbour offices and rules for customs and commercial protection. A. C. S. Peacock's 2024 study adds Arabic, Persian and Turkish accounts, inscriptions and navigational sources that connect the port to wider Muslim geographical knowledge. These witnesses do not settle the exact conversion dates of early rulers; the evidence for that sequence is famously inconsistent, as Christopher Wake noted. Muslim, Hindu, Buddhist and other merchant communities participated in Indian Ocean commerce; Muslim merchants did not monopolize its ports.

Indian Ocean port communities linked mercantile trust, resident families, worship and instruction through sustained settlement. Trust among commercial partners is not identical to faith, but people who lived together could build prayer spaces, commission teachers and sustain scholarly ties. A port administration that protected foreigners did not by that fact produce Muslim conviction; it furnished conditions in which a community could reproduce itself. Malay literary and legal manuscripts record Islamic teaching and court practice in forms that differed among ports and ruling houses. The transition from an Arabic book to Jawi instruction was likewise mediated by language, translators, scribes and patrons. Local rulers, merchants, scribes and teachers shaped how Islamic practice entered Malay institutions and vernacular texts.

A port is a powerful example of practical unity formed through distinct competences. Navigation requires meteorological and maritime skill; a merchant contract requires enforceable expectations and trust; the care of families requires continuing relations; religious worship requires knowledge and places of assembly. One ship's arrival does not perform all of these tasks, but it may set them into relation. The person formed by Islam can participate responsibly in several roles, while a harbour government coordinates a public subset of them. This is a horizontal expression of the formal unity of action: the parts retain proper functions while serving the community's life.

The history of Melaka especially needs evidence of residence and mediation. An officer's customs rule governs visiting traders; a mosque, a teacher or a household cultivates a different form of attachment to the place. Commercial reliability may create opportunities for religious exchange but is not identical with assent to a creed. Linguistic translation and long-term marriage can carry practices across generations; the details differ among ports. The spiritual and the economic are joined in lived communities through actual means, not by asserting that profitable commerce automatically produces a religious civilization.

Melaka's *syahbandar* officers, customs regulations and arrangements for visiting merchants make an excellent causal case. They helped reduce uncertainty about lodging, weighing, protection and market transactions for temporary residents. Some of these residents formed more durable households and communities in which religious learning could continue between trading seasons. Harbour privileges supported trade and settlement, but dated inscriptions, legal texts and local chronicles give differing evidence for the conversion of particular rulers and communities. Peacock's Arabic and Persian material locates Melaka within broader Muslim geographical knowledge, while Malay and Portuguese accounts supply different perspectives. Holding these kinds of evidence apart prevents commerce from being treated either as proof of spiritual transmission or as an explanation that makes spiritual teaching unnecessary.

### 40. Bengal: fields, settlements and changing religious communities

**Matn**

The eastern Bengal delta changed through the work of rivers, cultivation and settlement. Forested and marshy areas were opened over generations, while waterways shifted and new villages arose. Military conquest did not immediately determine the religious life of all who inhabited this region. Households, cultivators, local chiefs, teachers and revered figures participated in the creation of its rural communities.

A settlement needed land rights, food, shared labour and places for assembly. Religious instruction often arrived within these practical arrangements. A land grant could support cultivation and a place of worship; a teacher might help a family learn prayer while a village negotiated access to water or revenue obligations. The meanings of such activities were joined in lived society but established through different sources of evidence.

The material record preserves uneven traces: inscriptions, monuments, administrative documents and local narratives. A remembered saint's influence must be related to the date of a settlement and the surviving documentation rather than assumed to be the cause of all later devotion in its neighbourhood. The person who taught and the people who cultivated had distinct roles in forming a durable community.

Bengal's history thus gives the wider account a local scale. Religious life grew through ordinary acts of work, learning and relationship, while ecology and political authority shaped which forms of settlement could endure.

**Sharḥ**

The historian Richard Eaton's *The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier, 1204–1760* is the key test case. He uses inscriptions, monuments, administrative texts and local literary records to relate political, agrarian and religious frontiers without collapsing one into the others. A later essay clarifies four overlapping processes, adding the eastward movement of Sanskritic institutions to political, agrarian and religious change. These distinctions are historical hypotheses supported in particular places and periods, not a universal theory of conversion.

For a particular village or shrine, the available evidence can include a land grant, a dated inscription and later settlement patterns. A saint's remembered career may or may not be independently datable; the location of followers' fields and the timing of a later literary account can show how memories of a community took shape. The spiritual question is inseparable from this context but not reducible to it: why did people regard particular teachers and places as bearers of blessing and trustworthy guidance? The archive may establish their social position without proving or disproving their inner spiritual realization. In parts of Bengal, agriculture, land grants, shrines and religious instruction developed through overlapping local settlements rather than one uniform sequence.

A Bengal land grant gathers several causes whose separation is essential for historical explanation. The ecological possibility of cultivating a site depends upon rivers, soils and labour. A grant establishes a claim to land or revenue under a political authority. A local religious foundation can give that settlement a communal centre, while the teaching and formation of persons occurs through households and learned guides. These aspects may reinforce one another but cannot be proven identical from the presence of a shrine or an administrative inscription. The material circumstances in which people come to practise Islam are properly studied as a local history of settlement.

This case allows the three scales of practical wisdom to be read together. A family seeks provision and security; a village negotiates access, responsibility and disputes; persons learn the habits and worship through which religious life is reproduced. The aim of guidance is not to convert the delta into a symbol for inner realization; it is lived through choices by those who settle, teach, care and govern. The metaphysical account of the human vocation lends an orientation toward right use of received goods, while Eaton's historical method requires inscriptions, changing agrarian boundaries and local narratives to establish how particular settlements actually formed.

A land grant associated with a religious figure in Bengal can carry several meanings at once: authorization to cultivate, a claim to revenue, support for a place of worship and later memory of a revered founder. Eaton's historical method asks which meanings can actually be dated from the document or its material setting. If cultivation moves into a forest frontier, the establishment of a village may alter the resources and social ties through which a religious community reproduces itself. Yet neither the clearing of land nor the grant proves a person's inward conversion. Land grants and mosque foundations document settlement and patronage but seldom disclose the private convictions of every household involved. A genuine integrated history resists the equal and opposite temptations of reducing Islam to economics or treating agricultural conditions as irrelevant to its local life.

### 41. The Ottoman settlement: courts, colleges and a growing imperial household

**Matn**

Ottoman rulers developed an Anatolian principality into an empire extending through the Balkans, Anatolia and the Arab provinces. The capture of Constantinople in 1453 gave the dynasty a capital at the meeting of several routes and jurisdictions. Soldiers, palace households, provincial officials and tax collectors sustained imperial rule, while local judges, teachers, guilds and religious communities carried out other forms of social order.

The learned hierarchy often called the ʿilmiyye developed over time through appointments to teaching and judicial posts, and its later form should not be projected unchanged upon the dynasty's earliest centuries. Provincial communities also inherited distinct legal customs and spiritual lineages when Ottoman rule entered the Arab lands. Their courts and schools negotiated these relations rather than receiving an entirely new religious order through one decree.

In an Ottoman town, a family disputing an inheritance might appear before a qāḍī whose training followed a recognized legal school. The judge heard claims and witnesses within a procedure; government regulations concerning taxation or public order operated alongside juristic practice. A college supplied teachers and students, and a waqf could maintain a mosque, school, kitchen or water supply. These institutions answered different needs and depended on different kinds of authority.

The ruler's command could give a court power to act, but a judgment still required knowledge, fairness and evidence. A spiritual guide might correct an intention without holding a governmental appointment; a charitable foundation might feed people long after its founder died. Their work could cooperate with rule or expose its failures. Public life depended on such differentiated forms of competence.

The Ottoman settlement joined an extensive political order to learned and devotional networks whose histories reached beyond one dynasty. Its lasting institutions show how a shared religious orientation could acquire powerful forms of social transmission while remaining open to contest over justice and authority.

**Sharḥ**

The Ottoman *ʿilmiyye* offered a relatively structured hierarchy of appointments but cannot be equated with a single centralized clergy. Gilles Veinstein's chapter in *The Cambridge History of Turkey* documents how judges, mosques, Friday sermons, colleges and pious foundations became part of Ottoman governance from its early period, while scholars were originally recruited from other regions as well. Later institutions changed over time; do not project the sixteenth-century hierarchy back onto the fourteenth century.

A judge in an Ottoman town faced questions raised by actual families and markets, while sultanic *qānūn* regulated matters such as tax and administration. The relationship between juristic tradition and imperial rule varied by subject and province; a law code is not evidence that everyone obeyed it. Endowments supported services but also elite households. Sufi lodges and the pilgrimage connected people across frontiers, including Arab provinces with established scholarly traditions. Rulers patronized colleges and courts, but jurists, household teachers and spiritual lineages retained forms of learning that governments did not wholly control. Ordinary subjects encountered the empire through courts, markets, taxes, village administration and worship, not solely through the decrees of Constantinople.

Ottoman legal life brings normative determination and temporal execution into close contact. A qāḍī adjudicates a claim through recognizable legal procedures; the litigants bring evidence and sometimes negotiate an agreement; the sultan's government supplies a jurisdiction and may impose administrative regulations. Their work relates to justice under different formal aspects. A judge needs knowledge of the law and the persons before him, yet he cannot simply substitute a spiritual conviction for evidence or a correct legal text for an inquiry into the facts. Sultanic qānūn, juristic doctrine and local court practice must be compared as distinct materials.

The larger empire also shows that authority can be differentiated without disintegration. Courts, endowed colleges, hospices, fiscal offices and spiritual teachers sustained different capabilities. A comprehensive public purpose becomes effective when each is accountable to its own end and cooperates with the others where duties overlap. A charitable foundation needs competent accounting; a school requires trained instruction; a just ruler must listen to informed judges. These are practical relations among finite human agents, not a claim that any sultan embodied the completed Muhammadan station. Court registers, waqf records and appointments permit historians to study how the relation actually worked at given places and times.

An Ottoman court register provides a different view of the state than an imperial legal code. In a register, litigants present claims about inheritance, debts or property, and the qāḍī records settlements within a social world in which witnesses, custom and political order matter. The imperial law code reveals how the ruler intended to regulate some activities. These documents may agree or differ without either being irrelevant. A madrasa certificate, meanwhile, records a different kind of authority, that of learned transmission. The empire's durability required all three—imperial command, local adjudication and teaching—but their relations varied. Ottoman court records, the *ʿilmiyye* hierarchy, endowments and regional Sufi institutions document distinct and sometimes competing forms of authority.

### 42. Safavid Iran and Mughal India: rival confessional and imperial settlements

**Matn**

During the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, Safavid Iran and Mughal India developed distinct arrangements between scholarship, confessional identity and public power. The Safavid rulers supported Twelver Shiʿi institutions as they reshaped the religious life of Iran over generations. Mughal emperors governed regions with many languages and communities, relying on provincial revenue systems, Persianate administration and changing relations with religious teachers.

An imperial programme travelled through actual agents. A jurist summoned to teach needed students and a livelihood; a governor required officials able to record land revenue; a household decided how to worship and educate children. In the Mughal world, Akbar sponsored courtly religious discussions and policies of inclusion, while under Aurangzeb scholars compiled a major Ḥanafī legal collection. These policies expressed different approaches to authority and did not produce identical conduct in every province.

In Safavid cities philosophers, jurists and spiritual teachers disputed questions of knowledge and religious legitimacy even under rulers who promoted one confession. Their discussions had their own grounds of proof, while public recognition affected where and how they were taught. Indian learned networks likewise combined law, hadith, logic and spiritual discipline through families and schools.

These parallel histories show several forms of participation in Muslim intellectual and religious life. Their unity cannot be drawn from an identical imperial plan; it arose through actual persons ordering duties and knowledge within different political worlds.

**Sharḥ**

The Safavid adoption of Twelver Shiʿism was a state project with long and uneven social consequences, not an instant change in the convictions of all Iran's inhabitants in 1501. The arrival and support of scholars from regions with established Imāmī learning are part of that history. The relation of rulers to jurists also changed between Ismāʿīl I and later Safavid reigns. Mughal policies toward religious communities, revenue and scholarship differed among rulers and provinces, and changed across time. Akbar's courtly debates, revenue administration and imperial household were not interchangeable institutions; the *Fatāwā-yi ʿĀlamgīrī* is a major compilation whose practical reception demands separate research.

Safavid Isfahan supported philosophers and theologians including Mullā Ṣadrā, while Indian scholarly networks developed curricula in law, ḥadīth, logic and spiritual discipline. Philosophers, jurists and spiritual authors within each empire disputed one another's positions rather than expressing a uniform imperial doctrine. Shāh Walī Allāh's eighteenth-century work later connected South Asian hadith, law and spiritual concerns in a setting shaped by Mughal fragmentation. Comparative studies of the three empires should account for revenue systems, military organization, confessional policy and local communities before making large claims about decline or renewal. Safavid patronage of Twelver Shiʿi scholarship gave Iran institutions different from those sustained by Ottoman Sunni and Mughal scholarly networks.

Safavid and Mughal histories make the distinction between religious authority, personal realization and imperial policy unusually clear. The Safavid state's promotion of Imāmī institutions affected who could teach, judge and receive support; it did not decide every dispute among philosophers, jurists and spiritual masters. Mughal patronage of learning and governance altered the opportunities for those engaged in those arts while leaving regional households with their own commitments. Different confessional claims should be represented on their own doctrinal grounds rather than compressed into one general category of Muslim statecraft.

Knowledge and practice moved through the three practical scales. An individual's character determines how he exercises a learned role; family and local community sustain education and property; imperial government creates or limits the public forms available to them. A legal compilation such as the Fatāwā-yi ʿĀlamgīrī can express detailed normative determinations, yet its impact requires evidence of reception in courts and households. The Akbarian image of comprehensiveness can supply a conceptual horizon for the ordered coexistence of disciplines, but cannot establish whether a particular ruler or doctrine realized it. The different histories of Iran and India therefore have to be reconstructed through their own institutions and people.

## Book VII — Modern change in the conditions of religious life

### 43. Oceans, finance and the changing balance of power

**Matn**

From the late fifteenth century, Portuguese fleets entered an Indian Ocean already traversed by Asian and African merchants. Dutch and English companies later built fortified settlements and acquired financial and military capabilities that changed particular routes and ports. These newcomers encountered sophisticated markets with existing credit relationships, maritime knowledge and political authorities.

Surat in western India illustrates the continuing strength of Asian commercial networks after Portuguese ships reached the Indian Ocean. Merchant families financed voyages and used credit across communities, while competing European companies gradually sought trade privileges, fortified sites and political authority. The balance changed through specific acquisitions of force and jurisdiction; the meeting of the trading partners remained a world of personal promise, calculation and the real needs of distant households.

Over succeeding centuries, commercial companies and expanding European states joined shipping, borrowing, taxation and military force in new ways. A trading monopoly, a harbour fort and the administration of an inland province were different kinds of power, acquired through distinct arrangements. Industrial production and faster communication later altered the scale on which states could mobilize resources and transmit orders.

A merchant once recognized through personal reputation could increasingly be assessed through contracts, accounts and distant institutions. Such instruments extended cooperation while also making it possible for decisions to be taken by people who had never met those affected. Households and religious organizations experienced these changes through altered property rights, employment, taxation and schooling.

The forms of knowledge appropriate to commerce and administration became increasingly specialized and quantitative. They retained genuine technical value, yet a public order still needed to consider what its calculations were for and whose claims they served. This problem linked changes in world economy to the later transformation of law, education and the practical conditions of spiritual life.

**Sharḥ**

The historian Marshall Hodgson's 'Great Western Transmutation' describes changes in technology, communication, production and social organization that accompanied and exceeded Europe's overseas conquests. Atlantic credit, maritime trade and continuing Asian commercial networks developed together, challenging claims of a sudden and universal European monopoly. The familiar claim that Europeans simply monopolized the Indian Ocean from 1500 is false; studies of Gujarat and the Gulf document cosmopolitan communities and continuing Asian participation into the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Nor should a single Ottoman defeat be made the beginning of a uniform Muslim 'decline.'

Material change mattered to intellectual and religious life through identifiable intermediaries. A state unable to finance armies might reform taxes, borrow money or reorganize offices. A trading company that acquired territorial power could alter courts and property claims. Printed books and eventually telegraphy changed the speed and scale at which arguments moved. These were uneven transformations: an endowment-supported teacher in Cairo, a village jurist in India and a merchant family on the Swahili coast experienced them differently. Guénon criticized modern conceptions of knowledge and quantity, a metaphysical interpretation distinct from documentary evidence about finance, commerce and administration.

The expansion of finance and measurable administration changes the medium through which one person acts upon another's life. Credit instruments connect distant parties; accounts express resources in comparable numbers; a company may pursue profit through officers remote from the labourers or cultivators whose conditions it changes. Quantification is an indispensable technique for many genuine goods. The moral danger lies in allowing the numerical description to become a total account of what is valuable. An ability to calculate an efficient rate does not itself decide whether the underlying contract distributes risks justly. The two eyes require a truthful grasp both of the measurable particulars and of the persons whose obligations are involved.

A traditionalist criticism of the 'reign of quantity' identifies a metaphysical and cultural disorder in the elevation of measurement to an exclusive standard. It must be distinguished from Hodgson's historical account of changes in production, capital, communication and public institutions. The former can guide an assessment of ends; the latter asks which forces produced a particular change. Their conjunction is fruitful when a case is specified: a shipping company, a court's property rule or a state's borrowing arrangements. These supply records by which the relation between technical competence, public power and individual or household welfare can be examined rather than merely lamented.

Surat's maritime trade demonstrates why European arrival did not produce instant European control. Merchants of different religions and regions financed vessels, exchanged credit and relied on commercial partnerships; European companies entered these existing networks while later acquiring territorial power in different ports. Political control over a harbour, a monopoly over a commodity and control of an inland agrarian region are three different achievements. In Europe itself, changing relations among taxation, debt and military expenditure affected how states competed. Hodgson's 'transmutation' and traditionalist critiques of modern civilization address different questions: the former seeks historical mechanisms of change; the latter evaluates the intellectual and spiritual meaning of modern modes of life. Their conclusions can be compared only after the subject of each has been specified.

### 44. Colonial courts and the transformation of Islamic law

**Matn**

In British-ruled parts of India, courts gradually acquired powers previously exercised across a more varied field of Muslim judges, muftis, legal scholars and local practices. Officials translated texts and classified doctrines within new procedures; separate state codes increasingly governed criminal and commercial affairs, while selected matters of family and property were designated Muslim personal law. The same religious vocabulary therefore entered an altered system of authority.

The Sylhet endowment had been executed in 1868 by two brothers, Abdur Rahman and Abdul Kadir, who assigned property to a family arrangement with charitable provisions. Their descendants and creditors later disputed its effect. In December 1894 the Privy Council ruled the arrangement invalid as a charitable waqf under its view of the applicable law. The 1913 validating legislation addressed family waqfs prospectively; the 1930 Act extended that validation retrospectively. These steps changed legal enforceability under colonial public authority without erasing the juristic arguments that had framed the earlier practice.

A dispute over family waqf made the change visible. The older exemplary act concerned ʿUmar's land at Khaybar: the asset was kept and its fruits directed toward beneficiaries under conditions intended to endure. Muslim jurists subsequently developed rules about valid dedication, family interests, management and ultimate charitable purposes. In Sylhet, members of a family arranged property for descendants and for charitable benefit. When their deed reached the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council, the 1894 decision treated the charitable benefit as too remote to validate the arrangement.

The judgment changed what could be secured through that form of deed under colonial courts, despite the continuing force of different juristic understandings among Muslims. Legislation in 1913, followed by further legislation in 1930, addressed the effect of the earlier ruling. The consequences depended on precise legal texts and the jurisdictions in which they operated; the original gift, the jurists' distinctions, the colonial procedure and the later statute were different steps in one traceable history.

The movement from a landowner's question to an endowment, a legal school, a family deed and a colonial appeal shows how a moral purpose acquires and sometimes loses effective institutional form. Spiritual intention, legal validity and public enforcement must be understood in their particular relation, rather than assuming that an inherited norm supplies an unchanged institutional arrangement for every age.

**Sharḥ**

Colonial authorities reorganized courts and legal procedures differently in India, North Africa and other Muslim regions. British Indian 'Anglo-Muhammadan law' applied selected doctrines under colonial procedures and later concentrated much Muslim law in personal status; the University of Exeter's Privy Council Papers historical guide gives an accessible account of the changing scope. The legal historian Wael Hallaq argues that colonial state structures transformed juristic authority; local court records and studies of codification document how these changes operated in particular jurisdictions. The scholar of Islamic law Sohaira Siddiqui examines changes in legal theory and authority under colonial rule alongside the translation and codification of particular legal manuals.

Colonial courts transferred parts of legal classification and procedure from older juristic networks to officials working within new state institutions. A juristic ruling had once emerged in a field populated by teachers, muftis, judges, litigants, rulers and local practice; colonial government could retain the vocabulary of Muslim law while relocating final authority over procedure and legal classification. That shift did not erase all juristic agency, and different courts followed different trajectories. It did, however, produce new educational problems. Madrasas might strengthen instruction in the inherited texts, while new colleges trained students for changed administrative professions. The divergence between moral formation, scholarly qualification and public jurisdiction became acute. Al-Attas later argued that education should order knowledge according to its proper purposes, while colonial officials and modern colleges changed the institutional settings in which law and learning acquired authority.

The endowment trajectory permits the new explanatory framework to be tested across a complete sequence of transformations. In the original Khaybar episode ʿUmar seeks guidance concerning property; the continuing substance and distributed yield are differentiated within one charitable action. Later jurists formulate rules governing validity, descendants, management and ultimate beneficiaries. A Sylhet family then executes a deed within its particular social and legal setting. The Privy Council applies a colonial conception of charitable purpose in December 1894, affecting the legal security of family waqf under that jurisdiction; the 1913 legislation and its 1930 extension form further public interventions. These are successive and partly different acts, connected by a legally intelligible problem. Their doctrinal and procedural differences must be established through the actual texts.

The three practical orders explain why a single waqf raises multiple questions. Ethical cultivation concerns generosity and trust; household governance concerns the care of descendants; civic ordering concerns the legal form under which property and beneficiaries can be protected. Fiqh decides what the norm permits and requires; the court's jurisdiction determines how a deed will be treated; the legislature may change the public enforceability of family settlements. The vertical principle of right orientation does not write a colonial statute. Its historical expression follows a chain of human authors, interpreters and judges whose acts are limited and open to criticism. Here the distinction between an enduring purpose, a contingent institution and a truly effective decision prevents the mistaken treatment of fiqh as an unchanging administrative blueprint.

The family *waqf* dispute in *Abul Fata Mahomed Ishak v. Russomoy Dhur Chowdhry* makes the transformation of legal authority concrete. In 1868, two brothers in Sylhet established property arrangements supporting descendants and eventually the poor. The [published Privy Council decision of 15 December 1894](https://courtkutchehry.com/judgments/abul-fata-mahomed-ishak-and-others-appellant-hash-russomoy-dhur-chowdhry-and-others-respondent) rejected the deed as a valid charitable endowment because the benefit to charity was judged too remote. The [Mussalman Wakf Validating Act 1913](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1852964/) and a [1930 Act](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/192467222/) subsequently altered the applicable legal treatment, the latter with qualified retrospective application.

The episode connects a household's decision, juristic categories, advocates, provincial judges, an imperial appellate court and later legislation. Each institution acts through a different competence and form of power. A family may seek to discharge obligations through an endowment; a jurist may interpret the law of charitable purpose; a court controls what evidence and authority govern its judgment. The Prophet's integrated guidance supplies an ultimate standard of truthfulness and justice, while the actual legal right depends on the applicable sources, contracts, jurisdiction and proof. The historical change lies in the redistribution of the competence to decide, explain and enforce inherited legal obligations.

The Bengal judicial reforms associated with Warren Hastings in 1772 provide a dated institutional point of entry. Officials attempted to retain some Hindu and Muslim legal doctrines while placing their application inside a new hierarchy of Company courts. Over the nineteenth century, codes, appeals and professional practices altered the role of classical juristic reasoning in public judgments. The effect cannot be summarized as the abolition of the Sharīʿah on one day. In other places, such as Egypt and Malaya, colonial and local arrangements developed differently. The source method here is to choose one class of cases—for example inheritance—and compare its doctrinal treatment in a juristic text, the translated rules used by officials and a particular court judgment. Only then can one assess which parts of interpretation and jurisdiction changed.

### 45. Renewal through teaching, reform and print

**Matn**

Colonial administration and new educational opportunities altered the audiences and resources of Muslim teachers. Printing brought commentaries, legal manuals, devotional books and controversy to people outside established circles. Railways and steamships widened travel, while examinations and state schools changed the means through which competence was publicly recognized. A person could possess a printed text without sharing the years of study through which a teacher had learned to explain it.

The educational projects had identifiable dates and programmes. The school at Aligarh began in 1875 and developed into the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College in 1877. At Deoband, scholars organized the madrasa during the 1860s, with institutional and scholarly sources differing in whether the foundation is dated 1866 or 1867. Their languages of instruction, funding, textbooks and aspirations for graduates mattered to what they could transmit. Print widened access, while trained teachers remained necessary to turn reading into dependable judgment.

In India, the madrasa associated with Deoband took shape in the 1860s around preservation and renewal of scholarly disciplines. Aligarh developed another programme through English-language and modern education. Their teachers and supporters answered distinct questions about the work students should be prepared to perform. Similar debates unfolded in Cairo, Ottoman lands and elsewhere, with considerable variety in approaches to law, spiritual practice and modern sciences.

Learning continued to involve the formation of persons. Recitation required repeated correction, legal reasoning required practice with sources and cases, and moral judgement matured through duties undertaken among others. A school structure could assist these achievements through disciplined time and recognized curricula; it could also reward the performance of an examination more readily than a durable virtue.

The expansion of print and schooling multiplied means of transmission while sharpening questions about their ends. The test of renewal lay in the knowledge people actually gained, the capacity to use it well and the human relationships through which it became responsible action.

**Sharḥ**

Reformers disagreed over inherited curricula, state schooling, legal authority and new sciences; their programmes crossed any simple traditional–modern division. Deoband, Aligarh, reformist writers in Cairo, Ottoman educational institutions and West African learned networks pursued different objectives. Some expanded hadith study; some sought a reorganization of legal authority; some defended established Sufi instruction; some integrated administrative or scientific subjects. Printed editions also altered textual practice: a student's stable printed page differed from a manuscript exemplar annotated through personal reading, but neither medium guaranteed correct understanding.

The founding chronology of Deoband is reported as 1866 in institutional histories and 1867 in several scholarly reference works. The exact Hijrī/Gregorian conversion and the distinction between foundation and formal opening need direct comparison. Aligarh's initial school opened in 1875 and the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College followed in 1877. These were actual institutional experiments supported through different donors, teachers, languages, curricula and aspirations. Their success is best assessed through the competencies students acquired, the work they subsequently performed and the forms of religious life each helped sustain.

Teaching is a form of human mediation because the learner's understanding grows through more than exposure to information. A student learns a legal text by distinguishing cases and arguments under correction; a reader of Qurʾānic language needs grammar, context and recitation; a person acquiring good character needs repeated practice among others. These are forms of takhalluq and learned malakah with different objects and tests of proficiency. Print greatly enlarges access to expressions of knowledge, but it does not by itself ensure the formed capacities through which those expressions are understood and applied. An examination may measure part of that acquisition while leaving other parts unobserved.

Different educational reforms should therefore be compared by the goods they tried to transmit. A madrasa sustaining chains of legal and hadith study and a college preparing students for new professions answer partially different social demands. The relation between inherited learning and civic work becomes explicit in curricula, teacher appointments, student lives and the institutions that employ graduates. A metaphysical account of ordered knowledge supplies a measure for asking whether the disciplines serve the whole person; historical assessment needs evidence about actual teaching, social access and competence. The challenge of renewal is to coordinate those valid partial excellences without confusing one certificate, ideological programme or reading medium with realized wisdom.

Al-Attas connects education to *adab* and the ordering of knowledge; Guénon criticizes the modern priority given to quantity. Their arguments interpret modernity at a different level from the institutional histories of particular schools. Al-Attas and traditionalist authors later offered distinct critiques of knowledge and modernity; neither expressed the intentions of every nineteenth-century Muslim teacher. The historical evidence involves school registers, syllabi, printed editions, biographies, correspondence and institutional records. The better question is which students each new arrangement served, what it enabled them to do, and what forms of knowledge became easier or harder to transmit. The answer will vary across classes, languages, genders and regions. Print, mass schooling and institutional examinations changed how teachers explained texts, corrected mistakes and assessed students' understanding.

Deoband and Aligarh represented distinct institutional responses among many forms of South Asian Muslim education. Deoband emerged through a specific scholarly and financial arrangement, while the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College developed its own residential and institutional aspirations. Women, merchant communities, village teachers, Sufi networks and other regional schools also shaped religious learning. Print made a legal opinion or devotional text more widely available, yet public circulation could intensify disputes over wording, authority and context. Examinations, lessons and student writings reveal differences between memorizing formulas, explaining a text and applying its reasoning to unfamiliar cases. This links the formation of *malakāt* to actual instructional practice without pretending that one curriculum was uniformly successful.

### 46. Nation-states, migration and the new scale of Muslim life

**Matn**

The twentieth century transformed the political and social conditions under which Muslim communities lived. Ottoman institutions ended, colonial empires retreated unevenly and new states emerged through partition, independence and revolution. Families moved across new borders and settled in societies with different laws. Mass education reached many more people, while radio, television and later digital networks allowed religious words to circulate far beyond their first audiences.

Partition in South Asia in 1947 created new states and transformed family networks, property and educational institutions, while Indonesian independence followed a different series of struggles across 1945–49. Migrants to Europe and the Americas founded mosques, associations and schools within still other legal settings. The shared rites of Islam remained recognizable while the institutions required for education, public rights and family life followed new histories of statehood and movement.

A Muslim in a new city might learn prayer at home, study law through a university course, hear sermons from another country and seek spiritual instruction from a local guide. Each source supplies access to a different kind of competence. A searchable text helps locate evidence; the capacity to weigh it demands languages, context and training; the habit of acting upon it requires relationships and repeated effort. The speed of a message has no necessary relation to the depth with which it is understood.

Public institutions also carry more specialized authority. States license some schools and mosques, courts decide particular family matters, private organizations provide relief, and families form dispositions that no ministry can simply command into being. Their cooperation or tension affects whether a principle becomes lived justice for a particular child, worker, student or neighbour.

The enduring pattern of prophetic guidance remains concrete. Revelation is received and remembered; right action is learned; knowledge is interpreted in its rightful field; persons are formed; public arrangements secure obligations where they can. Contemporary renewal asks how those acts can be joined responsibly under new conditions of power, movement and technological mediation.

**Sharḥ**

Muslim-majority states, minority communities and religious institutions experienced distinct political and educational developments; no single pattern describes them all. Independence in India and Pakistan in 1947, Indonesian independence during 1945–49, the dissolution of Ottoman political institutions and distinct African decolonizations took place under different conditions. State ministries, traditional schools, Sufi networks, universities and migrant associations cannot be assigned one universal effect. The transformation of broadcast and digital communication also has contrary possibilities: access to large textual corpora expands, while the authority of a circulating quotation can become detached from the circumstances of its transmission.

In Madinah, believers developed arrangements for shared obligations and adjudication; later institutions addressed related problems through different legal and communicative structures. In seventh-century Madinah, a new community needed ways to place trust in reports, resolve disputes and act together; later hadith, legal and spiritual traditions elaborated forms of authority that could travel beyond their first setting. Today the material conditions of access and repetition have changed again. A transmitted text, its juristic interpretation, its practice in daily life and later spiritual explanations remain distinct even when one person engages all four. Digital circulation expands access to religious material without guaranteeing correct interpretation or lasting habits. It yields concrete research questions: what does a student know after repeated short videos, what can they independently verify, who corrects their misunderstanding, and how does learning change conduct over time? A student's ability to identify sources, explain their meaning and apply what they learn supplies concrete evidence of understanding.

Contemporary religious life distributes responsibility among people and media at an unprecedented scale. A digital archive stores words; an editor establishes a text; a hadith critic investigates attribution; a jurist reasons about applicability; a teacher corrects understanding; a family gives daily form to the obligation; a state may enforce a part of it. These functions are related without being identical. Their proper coordination is the horizontal problem of transmitting a comprehensive guidance through finite institutions. Access to an archive is a condition for some kinds of study, but the actuality of knowledge appears in competent judgement and responsible action.

The movement from ascent to return supplies an enduring measure for contemporary spiritual formation. Prayer and remembrance are carried back into work, parenting, study and public duty. An inward experience claiming comprehensive significance has to be assessed against the person's treatment of actual others and the normative standards of the tradition. The three practical orders are all present in a modern migrant household: the cultivation of virtue, care for dependants and participation in the law and common life of a new polity. The task of renewal is therefore neither to recreate the institutions of seventh-century Madinah unchanged nor to abandon their guiding ends. It is to determine which practices and offices now truthfully transmit knowledge, justice and formation under changed material conditions.

Online archives lower the cost of finding religious texts, but understanding them still requires linguistic, historical and interpretive skills. A scanned manuscript, a search result from a hadith database, a short devotional clip and a university lecture each give different access to evidence and guidance. Digital texts and short lectures often circulate without their original context; checking the source, language and commentary remains necessary for accurate interpretation. Teachers still identify errors that access to a document alone cannot correct. Mass media and searchable digital texts expanded access to religious information, while interpretation and durable habits still required sustained practice and correction. Digital archives broaden access to sources while placing greater demands on independent verification and sustained study.
