# A family waqf before the Privy Council: the 1894 Sylhet appeal
## Primary case judgment, family papers, juristic disagreement and legislation

**Sīrah Chapter 44 source audit · 12 November 2026**

### Primary witness now directly inspected

**Abul Fata Mahomed Ishak and others v Russomoy Dhur Chowdhry and others**, Privy Council, decision 15 December 1894, (1894) LR 22 Indian Appeals 76; ILR 22 Calcutta 619. [Published judgment text](https://courtkutchehry.com/judgments/abul-fata-mahomed-ishak-and-others-appellant-hash-russomoy-dhur-chowdhry-and-others-respondent), reporting the opinion delivered by Lord Hobhouse. [University of Exeter's Privy Council Papers research site](https://privycouncilpapers.exeter.ac.uk/the-judicial-committee/full-text-printed-papers/) separately lists the case among six for which it preserves extended printed appeal papers. The original underlying family deed and the complete lower-court transcript **have not** been independently collated here.

This primary text closes a previous gap: the case was previously supported chiefly through Gregory Kozlowski's account and Nandini Chatterjee's study of the household archive. The reported judgment is now accessible and has been checked for its actual legal reasoning and material facts.

### What happened according to the judgment

A deed dated **21 December 1868** was executed by two brothers, Abdur Rahman and Abdool Kadir, in the Sylhet district. The family claimed it created a valid *waqf*. Its principal immediate benefits went to the brothers' descendants and relatives. The deed named the poor, widows and orphans of Sylhet as potential ultimate beneficiaries **after** the donors' bloodline had become extinct. The settlors themselves remained managers (*mutawallis*); they fixed allowances to family members, and the settlement restricted beneficiaries' capacity to demand accounts or alienate interests.

A subordinate judge in Sylhet initially treated the waqf as valid; the Calcutta High Court reversed. The Privy Council heard an appeal, not original live witness testimony. It declined to adjudicate every disagreement over individual parcels: the decisive question was whether the settlement's provisions could count as a true charitable dedication under the principles of Muslim law as that tribunal understood them.

The Board held the charitable interest too remote and uncertain. Its argument was not that the words *waqf* or *God* were forbidden in the document. It was that the arrangement's substantive beneficiaries were the family indefinitely, while the stated ultimate benefit to the poor was illusory in practice. The judges discussed contrary arguments developed by Muslim jurist and judge **Amīr ʿAlī**, including a different understanding of earlier Muslim precedents and the charitable value of maintaining a household. They did not adopt that interpretation.

**Research distinction:** The Board's interpretation is a contested judicial application of Muslim law by a colonial appellate tribunal, not an exhaustive proof that Muslim jurists had always rejected family waqf. The existence of a later *wakf alal aulad* statute itself reflects sustained dispute over the status of familial benefit as a charitable or pious purpose.

### The statutory response: two dates, not one

The [Mussalman Wakf Validating Act, 1913](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1852964/), ss. 3–4, permitted family waqfs satisfying Muslim-law requirements where ultimate benefit was reserved for the poor or another permanent charitable purpose. Section 4 explicitly addressed a charitable benefit postponed until family descent came to an end.

**The 1913 Act was not initially retrospective.** The [Mussalman Wakf Validating Act, 1930](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/192467222/), s. 2, extended the 1913 Act to earlier waqfs but preserved rights and liabilities already accrued before the enactment. This is confirmed in the published 1919 judicial discussion of the Act's prospective effect ([Nawab Khaseh Habibullah Saheb v Khajeh Soleman Quader](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/547618/)) and in the [Indian Law Commission's 250th report](https://www.legislative.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/06/010a4ec28e488b994c3d036a4353275a.pdf).

Therefore the correct sequence is **1868 deed → lower-court dispute → 1894 Privy Council judgment → 1913 prospective statutory correction → 1930 qualified retrospective validation**.

### The micro-to-macro relation made historically explicit

The example now supplies a documented chain of connections rather than a speculative analogy. Two brothers organized family property through a deed; descendants and parties dealing with the property litigated claims; courts reclassified the arrangement through their own rules of evidence and legal interpretation; a court in London ruled on a family institution in Sylhet; Muslim jurists and legislators contested the outcome; statutes changed the class of lawful endowments.

Some details of family papers and litigants' motivations still require the extended printed appeal papers. But the court's own argument and the statute's text establish that *preserving an Arabic legal label is not the same as preserving the legal and social relationships through which an institution functions*.

The Akbarian analogy of the “partial trace” can be used only after this history is established. It does not decide whether the wakf deed was valid in Muslim jurisprudence. The relevant historical fact is the shift of interpretive and enforcement power, including a **contested** distinction between family support and public charity under a colonial court.
