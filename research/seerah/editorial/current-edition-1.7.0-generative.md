# Book I — The world into which the mission came

## 01. Prophetic beginnings: guidance addressed to human life

**Matn.** God created Ādam, taught him the names and entrusted humanity with a life on earth in which knowledge and obedience would be required. Iblīs refused God's command to bow to Ādam, claiming that his creation from fire made him superior to one created from clay. Ādam and his wife were given a place in the Garden and forbidden to approach a particular tree. When they disobeyed, they acknowledged their wrong and sought God's forgiveness. God accepted their repentance and sent them to earth with the promise of guidance. On earth, Ādam and his descendants would receive guidance and remain responsible for how they answered it.

Nūḥ called his people to worship God and continued his mission despite their rejection. He addressed them openly and privately and refused to dismiss the believers whom their leaders looked down upon. At God's command he built the Ark while his opponents mocked him. When the flood came, he called to his son to board, but his son sought refuge on a mountain and was drowned. Nūḥ then appealed to God on his son's behalf. God explained that the son who had refused His call was not among the family members promised deliverance. Nūḥ sought refuge from asking about what he did not know and asked for mercy.

Hūd likewise called the people of ʿĀd to worship God alone. He challenged their pride in strength and impressive buildings and warned them against oppression. Ṣāliḥ addressed Thamūd, whose prosperity and carved dwellings did not free them from obedience to God. The she-camel was given to them as a sign, with an allotted share of water, but they violated the command concerning her. Neither power nor abundant provision could save a community determined to reject its messenger.

Lūṭ faced the people of his town when they threatened the guests who had come to him. He tried to protect them, while the visitors—angels sent by God—announced the approaching judgment and instructed him to depart with his household, except those excluded from deliverance. Shuʿayb called his people to worship God and to deal honestly in trade. He condemned the dishonest use of weights and measures and declared that he would not practise what he forbade others to do.

Ibrāhīm rejected idolatry and remained faithful to God despite opposition and difficult trials. He asked about the future of his descendants, and God made clear that His covenant did not extend to wrongdoers merely because of their descent. With Ismāʿīl, Ibrāhīm raised the foundations of the Sacred House and prayed for a community devoted to God. Ismāʿīl is remembered for keeping his promise and instructing his household in prayer and giving. Through Isḥāq and Yaʿqūb, guidance continued in other branches of the family.

Yūsuf was separated from his father by his brothers' betrayal, resisted temptation and was imprisoned before being vindicated. His interpretation of the ruler's dream led to measures for storing grain against years of famine. When his brothers came to Egypt, the story moved toward recognition, forgiveness and the reunion of a divided family. Mūsā grew up under a ruler who oppressed the Israelites. After leaving Egypt and living in Madyan, he received the divine call and was sent back to confront Pharaoh. The liberation of his people was followed by the harder work of keeping the covenant, worshipping faithfully and living together under God's guidance.

Dāwūd was given kingship, wisdom and the responsibility to judge justly; he is also remembered for praise of God and skilled work. Sulaymān received extensive rule and particular understanding, yet both kings remained answerable to the God who had granted their gifts.

Ayyūb endured severe affliction, prayed for relief and received healing. Yūnus called upon God in the darkness after leaving his people; God delivered him, and his people are remembered for repenting and being spared the threatened punishment. Zakariyyā prayed for a successor, and Yaḥyā was granted wisdom and tenderness. Maryam was sustained by God through extraordinary trials and gave birth to ʿĪsā, who called people to God and performed signs only by His permission.

The Qurʾān recounts these prophets to instruct Muḥammad ﷺ and his community, as well as to remember their earlier missions. Their missions unfolded among different peoples and circumstances, yet all called to worship of God and faithful action. The Prophet ﷺ received the final revelation and lived its guidance among his family, companions and wider community. His example would remain authoritative after his death, while later generations sought to understand and practise it in societies very different from the one in which he lived.

**Sharḥ.** Ādam: knowledge, responsibility and repentance.

The divine Names explain the world’s differentiated perfections; human comprehensiveness explains the capacity to know their relations. Comprehensiveness is not a mere inventory of faculties. It becomes effective when knowledge distinguishes the nature of things, their capacities, and what is due to them. Government without these distinctions cannot establish justice. The metaphysical vocation therefore reaches directly into knowledge of creatures, practical arts and the ordering of responsibilities.

In Qurʾān 2:31–33, God teaches Ādam the names and asks the angels to name what has been shown to them. Their inability to answer and Ādam's reply make his received knowledge central to the account. The exegetes differ over what the names encompass and how their teaching is understood; the passage does not present Ādam's knowledge as a reward earned through a prior quantity of worship.

Ādam's naming of creatures concerns received knowledge before it concerns dominion. The Qurʾānic account joins learning, responsibility, disobedience, repentance and the promise of further guidance (2:30–38). These relations furnish the theological opening for later accounts of human arts, judgment and government without making those institutions contemporaneous with Ādam. In the Akbarian interpretation, the comprehensive human is capable of relating differentiated realities without confusing their natures; the Muhammadan perfection later gathers prophetic excellences under an incomparable final commission. The relation is typological and principial, not a historical claim that all subsequent sciences descended by direct institutional transmission from the first prophet.

The lapse, grace and continuing formation.

Ādam’s individual history and the phases of primordial humanity represented through him occupy different interpretive levels. A spiritual cycle is not a dated biography, and the teaching of the Names does not by itself settle the timing of prophetic appointment.

After the transgression, Ādam receives words from God and repents (2:37). The Qurʾān describes both his recognition of wrongdoing and God's mercy toward him. Repentance is therefore neither the denial of the lapse nor a loss of the human dignity granted at creation. It restores his obedience and shows that a creature remains dependent upon divine guidance.

Unlike Ādam, Iblīs refuses to acknowledge his wrong. He argues from the difference between fire and clay to justify disobeying God's command (7:12; 38:76). His reasoning seizes upon one feature of the created beings involved and treats it as sufficient to overrule a divine command. Later spiritual commentators discuss the limits of such partial knowledge, but God's permission for the event does not excuse Iblīs's refusal.

Gift, trial and the return to means.

The Qurʾān presents prophetic election as God's gift, not an office gained through completing a prescribed sequence of hardships. The prophets undergo trials and sometimes receive new instructions within them. These events disclose the demands of their missions to those who witness or hear about them; they do not imply that God needed the trial to learn what He did not know.

The movement toward God also need not terminate in withdrawal from ordinary action. A servant can pass from dependence upon means, through recognition of their insufficiency, to renewed use of them without inward dependence upon them. Return to action then expresses a stronger integration, not a diminished contemplation.

Maryam receives provision without having to procure it in the account of her sanctuary (3:37). During childbirth she is instructed to shake the palm trunk so that dates fall to her (19:25). Later spiritual commentators have read this change as an education in reliance upon God while using ordinary means. The Qurʾān itself does not explicitly rank the two episodes as successive spiritual stations; that ranking belongs to interpretation. Immediate human distress also need not destroy patience; sustained rebellion against the trial differs from its first bodily and emotional impact.

Work and ordinary bodily needs belong to human life, including the lives of the prophets. Spiritual detachment concerns the manner in which created means are used and trusted, not their simple abandonment. A farmer cultivates the land and a ruler must judge disputes; neither activity by itself determines the person's spiritual standing.

Nūḥ: worship, steadfastness and the claims of kinship.

Nūḥ's principal summons is clear in Sūrat Nūḥ (71:1–10): worship God, seek His forgiveness and heed His messenger. He speaks to his people over a long period, first openly and then privately, but their resistance persists. The chiefs' objection that only people of low standing have followed him (11:27–31; 26:111–115) is one aspect of that resistance. Nūḥ refuses to dismiss believing followers to satisfy the demands of the powerful. Their social rank cannot decide the truth of the message.

God commands Nūḥ to build the Ark (11:37–40), and he carries out the work while his opponents mock him. The vessel is a real means of rescue, but its construction and use occur under divine instruction. The Qurʾān then describes Nūḥ calling his son to board, the son's confidence that a mountain would protect him, and the flood that separates them (11:42–43). The son does not perish because Nūḥ loved him, but because he refused the guidance that had been offered.

Nūḥ's prayer after the drowning is especially delicate (11:45–47). He recalls that his son belongs to his family and that God's promise is true. God clarifies that the son is not among the family promised deliverance and warns Nūḥ against asking about what he does not know. Nūḥ immediately seeks divine forgiveness and mercy. Exegetes differ about what Nūḥ knew of his son's inward condition, and the readings of 11:46 are significant; the secure point is that biological descent does not entitle a person to salvation apart from faithful response to God.

The passage does not condemn a father's concern for his child. It distinguishes that concern from the authority of God's judgment, and it shows Nūḥ turning to God even after receiving correction. Some later Sufi and Akbarian commentaries explore the spiritual meanings of the flood and of those who drowned. Such interpretations must be attributed to their authors; they cannot reverse the Qurʾānic account of Nūḥ's summons, his son's refusal and God's answer.

Hūd and Shuʿayb: the call to worship and the demands of justice.

Hūd calls ʿĀd to worship God and to abandon its arrogance. Qurʾān 26:128–130 refers to impressive constructions and the harsh exercise of power. The criticism falls upon boasting, oppression and the false security that people derive from strength, not upon building as such. Commentators offer different explanations of *maṣāniʿ*, including substantial buildings or works of water management; the passage does not identify one archaeologically verified site.

Shuʿayb's people question whether his prayer gives him grounds to challenge their worship and their use of wealth (11:87). He requires honest weights and measures, fair dealing and the abandonment of corruption. His answer (11:88) makes the demand personal: he will not do behind their backs what he has forbidden them to do. His trust in God is joined to a willingness to live by the guidance he proclaims.

Reform here requires integrity of action as well as an accurate rule. Neither prophetic authority nor private piety grants an exemption from the standard proclaimed. Lawful provision remains good; its sufficiency challenges the claim that every profitable means is therefore legitimate. Dependence on divine enabling also limits the reformer’s self-claim: he can strive and teach, but cannot manufacture another person’s willing response.

Ṣāliḥ: the sign, the water share, and collective violence.

Ṣāliḥ's people possess gardens, water, crops and houses carved from mountains (26:146–149). The Qurʾān does not condemn their material capacities. It challenges their belief that security and abundance release them from an obligation to respect God's sign and the limits placed upon their use of water. The she-camel has a specified turn to drink; they have another (26:155). The shared source introduces a real, temporal restraint: their entitlement is not the only entitlement. The sign therefore enters the rhythms of provision rather than remaining an abstract spectacle.

A powerful minority resists this imposed limit. The narrative of nine corrupting groups conspiring against Ṣāliḥ and his household (27:48–50) distinguishes private envy, coordinated violence and the attempt to conceal responsibility under an oath. The corruption is not the fact that people need water, but the conversion of a legitimate resource interest into the destruction of a protected claim. The Qurʾānic verses do not identify the exact archaeological mechanism by which the camel appeared; reports of emergence from a rock belong to the exegetical tradition. The Ṣāliḥic *Fuṣūṣ* interprets the opening of rock through the wisdom of *fatḥ*, the disclosure of an unforeseen possibility, but that metaphysical reading does not establish a physical history not supplied by the Qurʾān.

The she-camel was a sign of God, and the allotted access to water was one condition the people of Thamūd were commanded to respect. Their refusal was part of their rejection of Ṣāliḥ's message, not merely a disagreement over water.

Lūṭ: the protection of guests and God's rescue.

The arrival of guests exposes a community's disorder in the concrete obligations of hospitality and protection (11:77–81). Lūṭ strives to shield those entrusted to him while facing people he cannot repel by his own force. His wish for strength or a protective clan identifies an actual horizontal constraint: righteous intention does not itself supply the means of defence. The commentaries distinguish the ordinary sense of the *rukn shadīd*, a powerful kin group or stronghold, from the Prophetic reminder that God's support is the ultimate refuge.

The Lūṭic *Fuṣūṣ* connects *malakiyya* with force and strength, making the episode a meditation on the limits of creaturely power. But divine protection is not a justification for neglecting the duty to resist harm within one's actual capacity. The guests and kinship status matter precisely because a messenger must act among people through means that may be insufficient. Divine rescue does not retroactively make their threatened assault a legitimate use of force.

Lūṭ's concern for his guests and his wish for help arise from a responsibility he cannot fulfil through his own strength alone. God's rescue does not make the prophet's concern pointless; it brings the threatening encounter to an end through a power beyond his own.


Ibrāhīm: intimacy, tested leadership and the House.

Ibrāhīm’s wisdom begins in *khulla*: an intimacy in which attachment to the divine permeates the person rather than remaining one interest among others. Its comprehensiveness is not physical mixture with God. It concerns the depth to which divine knowledge and love govern the servant’s reality. Trial makes that allegiance effective amid relationships and possessions that rightly matter to him.

The tests do not inform an ignorant God. They disclose fidelity in action and qualify the person’s leadership before others. The covenant therefore does not pass automatically through descent; injustice contradicts its demand. The near-sacrifice must not become praise of killing as such: surrender concerns the beloved attachment, while the ransom prevents surrender from being identified simply with the child’s death.

The sanctuary gives this allegiance durable social form. The stated purpose of the settlement is the establishment of prayer; the attraction of hearts and arrival of provisions sustain it. Return to the House, protection, pilgrimage and livelihood become related obligations rather than unrelated advantages. Its custodians cannot convert the goods supporting worship into a property claim against worship’s demands.

The sacred centre joins distinct lives to a common orientation without containing God or erasing their differences. The petition for a future messenger completes the connection between place and formation: recitation, teaching and purification must make the sanctuary’s purpose living in its people. A sacred building alone cannot do their knowing and acting for them.

Ismāʿīl: the covenant becomes household fidelity.

Ibrāhīm's sanctuary cannot secure its end through stone or descent alone. The Qurʾān independently honours Ismāʿīl for truthfulness to his promise and instruction of his household in prayer and *zakāt* (19:54–55). His agency is not reducible to standing beside his father at the House. The shared building becomes a recurring human task: promises bind conduct across time; household teaching habituates bodies and desires; giving prevents possessions from enclosing the family within private interest. Al-Qushayrī connects the promise to sacrificial trial and joins bodily and financial devotion. Ibn ʿĀshūr emphasizes the passage's independent treatment of Ismāʿīl.

Ibn ʿArabī's treatment of Ismāʿīl in the *Fuṣūṣ* concerns a spiritual station and the servant's dependence upon God. The Qurʾānic praise of Ismāʿīl for keeping his promise and instructing his household (19:54–55) also presents fidelity through acts sustained over time. The Qurʾānic praise of Ismāʿīl and Ibn ʿArabī's later metaphysical discussion address different questions; the latter does not replace the former.


Yūsuf: the light of interpretation and the subtle government of events.

Yūsuf's interpretation of dreams illustrates knowledge conveyed through signs whose meaning is not obvious to the person who sees them. The ruler's dream leads to Yūsuf's explanation and to practical preparations for scarcity. Ibn ʿArabī's treatment of Yūsufic wisdom associates dreams with the imaginal form of knowledge, which is a further metaphysical interpretation rather than a description of the report's historical mechanics.

The narrative also displays divine subtlety: betrayal, displacement and confinement do not disclose the whole course to their participants. The opening vision and its eventual realization give intervening events a relation that cannot be read from immediate advantage alone. This does not vindicate the brothers’ wrong; providence exceeds their intention rather than making their intention righteous.

The approach to office has a public threshold. Yūsuf seeks clarification of the accusation before accepting release, protecting the trust on which later service will depend. Spiritual integrity is not merely private self-certainty. It can require truthful public acknowledgment when an unresolved charge would compromise the entrusted work.

Interpretation then becomes practical counsel. Harvest remains in its ears, consumption leaves reserves, and future scarcity qualifies present abundance. The physical property of stored grain belongs inside the explanation: knowledge must address how food actually survives. Trustworthiness governs the beneficiaries; competence secures the means.

The conclusion returns authority to the household. Yūsuf speaks of release from prison rather than making the brothers’ violence the centre of the reunion. Reconciliation does not deny the wrong; it prevents regained power from making revenge its end. The narrative joins symbolic discernment, moral endurance, public credibility, temporal foresight and forgiving action.

Mūsā: divine formation, intimacy and public commission.

Mūsā’s mission begins before his confrontation with Pharaoh. The infancy narrative shows preservation through what appears most dangerous: water, separation and reception into an enemy’s household. Later flight and life in Midian enter a formation already held within divine care. The commentary reads subtle favour beneath overpowering circumstances; it does not claim that violence becomes righteous because God frustrates its purpose.

The declaration that God has chosen Mūsā for Himself leads directly to his being sent to Pharaoh. Intimacy does not culminate in keeping the messenger away from the world. It equips him to return without making worldly acceptance the source of his authority. His request for assistance and his brother’s participation further distinguish received mission from self-sufficient individual power.

Deliverance then changes the community’s task. Freedom from a ruler does not automatically form truthful judgment, stable worship or ordered desire. Covenant gives freedom a governing end. The tablets’ comprehensive guidance must not be redescribed as a merely external law awaiting a later religion to supply inwardness.

The al-Khiḍr narrative preserves a further distinction between spiritual dignity and the particular knowledge God discloses to a person. A limited presentation need not yield the whole reason for an act. This does not give ordinary agents authority to set revealed obligations aside through unverified claims to inward knowledge. It places judgment within its actual evidentiary and vocational limits.

Ayyūb: dependence, patience, and the right to ask for relief.

Suffering is not self-interpreting. It can result from several causes and does not prove a prior moral fault. The detailed accounts of Ayyūb's losses and bodily condition vary widely; accusations that he deserved punishment through a specific hidden wrongdoing are neither established by the Qurʾān nor required for its lesson. The spiritual commentary prefers the possibility of affliction that raises rank without preceding fault.

Ayyūb's invocation combines acknowledgment of harm with recognition of divine mercy (21:83). His statement does not annul patience: complaint **to** God differs from accusation **against** God. His steadfastness belongs to continuing worship and trust rather than to a refusal to ask. The response includes a striking bodily act: strike the ground with the foot, then wash and drink (38:42). A restorative divine gift arrives through created water and an obedient movement. Healing neither proves that suffering was a moral failure nor that using means marks lesser realization.

The oath incident (38:44) adds the relationship between firmness and lenience. The Qurʾān commands a bundle and speaks of not breaking an oath; the surrounding story involving Ayyūb's wife has several disputed details. Its juridical applications have also been contested. The limited but secure point is that the text joins fidelity to a commanded concession rather than equating seriousness with maximum severity.

In the Ayyūbic chapter of the *Fuṣūṣ*, Ibn ʿArabī reflects on suffering and the limits of human knowledge of its causes. The Qurʾānic Ayyūb prays to God, acknowledges his affliction and receives relief. Neither the Qurʾānic account nor the later metaphysical interpretation justifies claiming that every misfortune follows from a concealed personal fault.

Yūnus: the limits of prophetic expectation and the mercy of reprieve.

Yūnus's mission reveals a community that responds after a warning, avoiding the fate of destroyed peoples (10:98). The commentaries debate whether repentance occurred after impending signs or after punishment had begun, and compare it to Pharaoh's final response. Those chronologies are not interchangeable. The Qurʾānic result is decisive: God removes the threatened worldly punishment after the community believes. The people's future cannot be read exhaustively from the messenger's expectation of their present refusal.

The messenger's departure and the ordeal of the fish establish another difference. The phrase *lan naqdira ʿalayhi* (21:87) admits readings involving constriction or punishment; it must not be made to assert that a prophet doubted God's omnipotence. Ibn ʿAjība distinguishes the general possibility of withdrawing from hostile people from the specific authorization appropriate to this particular mission. Yūnus recognizes his wrong and calls out from darkness, and God saves him. The spiritual readings describe relief as *tanfīs*: constriction is opened through confession and mercy, without turning the initial impatience into righteousness.

Qurʾān 68:48 directly instructs Muḥammad ﷺ to remain patient and recalls the experience of the companion of the fish. The prohibition concerns impatient departure, not the excellent act of calling upon God. The Prophet's reported prohibition on claims of superiority over Yūnus also forbids using the episode to discredit a messenger.

Ibn ʿArabī's Yūnus chapter of the *Fuṣūṣ* develops its own arguments about the dignity of created human life and the limits of human judgment. The Qurʾān recounts the repentance of Yūnus's people and God's decision to spare them (10:98). Those are distinct texts and claims; a later spiritual reading does not replace the account of what the messenger and his people did.


Dāwūd and Sulaymān: gift, particular understanding and the uses of dominion.

Prophetic kingship gives vicegerency an outward jurisdiction; it does not produce prophecy through political success. The Davidic discussion emphasizes divine bestowal. The gifts establish gratitude and accountability rather than a claim that the recipient earned an independent power.

The damaged-crop judgment makes that accountability concrete. Scripture affirms that God gave both prophets judgment and knowledge while granting Sulaymān a particular understanding. In a widely transmitted explanation, his settlement restores the crop while temporarily compensating its owner through the flock’s benefits, then returns the respective properties. This reported detail illustrates a concern for restoration and both parties’ continuing means; it is not itself the full wording of the Qurʾān.

The different judgment does not reduce Dāwūd to an ignorant or spiritually deficient figure. The exegetical debate concerns particular discernment, prophetic judgment and divine correction. The wisdom already bestowed on both remains explicit.

Their relations to the cosmos also differ. Mountains and birds praise with Dāwūd; wind and other powers serve Sulaymān’s command. Praise, craft and ordered action express different relations of the same received gift. Armour-making joins inspired instruction to bodily protection, so technical work belongs within gratitude rather than beneath prophetic dignity.

The arrival of the Queen’s throne first tests Sulaymān: will extraordinary power produce gratitude or self-attribution? The Queen’s recognition matters, but it should not conceal this prior trial of the king. His extraordinary command increases his obligation to acknowledge the Giver; it does not suspend that obligation.

Zakariyyā and Yaḥyā: continuity without possession of the future.

Zakariyyā asks God for a successor while acknowledging his advanced age and his wife's barrenness. The birth of Yaḥyā is presented as an extraordinary gift rather than the expected outcome of their ordinary circumstances. Yet he ends by acknowledging God as the best of inheritors (21:89). His desire for continuity therefore does not authorize control of the gift. Interpretations of *yarithunī* (19:6) differ—wealth, knowledge, religious office, prophetic vocation, or combinations—and must not be reduced to a single supposedly obvious meaning.

Yaḥyā receives *ḥukm* in youth, takes hold of the Book with firmness and receives tenderness, purity and kindness to his parents (19:12–15). Commentators disagree about the exact timing and whether *ḥukm* here means early prophethood or wisdom and understanding. Neither reading makes strength and mercy rivals. A received capacity for firm judgment is perfected through gentle conduct and the absence of tyranny.

The linked conclusion of Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ identifies the prophets as people who hasten **in** good works, call upon God through hope and fear, and remain humble (21:90). This is an explicit scriptural counterweight to the one-sided spiritual fixation we studied in Iblīs. The different modes of response do not flatten into a fixed sentiment; they remain available to the command appropriate to the occasion. Neither family continuity nor spiritual fervour is complete unless it becomes receptive action.


Maryam and ʿĪsā: creative Word, means and servanthood.

Maryam receives provision in the sanctuary (3:37), and during childbirth is told to shake the trunk of a palm so that dates may fall (19:25). Later spiritual writers interpret these episodes in relation to reliance on God and the use of ordinary means. The Qurʾān itself does not explicitly rank the two scenes as successive spiritual stations; that ranking belongs to interpretation.

ʿĪsā’s conception discloses the creative command without an ordinary paternal cause. His signs likewise reveal divine life-giving through a created messenger. The repeated qualification of divine permission maintains the distinction between the instrument and the Source, while the form of the sign makes that Source conspicuous.

The mission does not stop at extraordinary effects. Recognition that God is his Lord and theirs leads to worship; confirmation of the Torah and the alteration of certain prohibitions connect revealed knowledge with the community’s conduct. The inward and outward are not successive competitors but coordinated requirements of response.

His final address concerning his followers further joins compassion to discernment. ʿĪsā entrusts the judgment of his followers to God, acknowledging that both punishment and forgiveness belong to Him (5:118). The divine Names of might and wisdom close the appeal, retaining the right of judgment even within the possibility of forgiveness. The passage admits different exegetical explanations of its scope; it does not independently establish an unrestricted doctrine of universal pardon.

The fuller relation among the episodes.

The Qurʾān recalls prophets who remained faithful under very different conditions. Ṣāliḥ called his people to respect a divine sign, Lūṭ sought protection for his guests, Ismāʿīl kept his promise, Ayyūb prayed during affliction, Yūnus turned to God after his departure, Zakariyyā sought a successor, and Yaḥyā received wisdom and tenderness. Their circumstances differ, but all remain servants of the same God.

The Qurʾān recounts these episodes for the instruction of the Prophet ﷺ and those who receive the final revelation. They offer different examples of fidelity, repentance, perseverance and judgment, without suggesting that each earlier prophet possessed only one virtue.

Muhammadan comprehensiveness and prophetic conduct.

The prophets share worship of God and differ in their missions, laws, circumstances and characteristic disclosures. The differentiation includes gratitude amid sovereignty, endurance under suffering, fidelity through changing fortune, and renunciation of self-importance. No prophet lacks every excellence not especially prominent in his story. Distinct emphasis is not exclusive possession.

Muhammadan gathering joins these modes of servanthood without cancelling their proper differences. Following the earlier guidance cannot mean simultaneously enacting incompatible practical laws. The commentators disagree about the precise authority of preceding laws, but the contrast between common guidance and differentiated legal provisions remains essential.

The Akbarian account explains the gathering through the comprehensive Name and the relation between Muhammadan speech and the realities deposited in Adamic humanity. It affirms the earlier prophets’ perfection while distinguishing modes of its manifestation. Principial precedence is not an earthly chronology placing the historical Prophet before Ādam.

The distinction between the principial reality and the historical person requires greater precision. In Farghānī's *Muntahā al-madārik*, fourth foundation (Beirut 2007, pp. 103 and 110–116), the world is read as an articulated Book, and the revealed Qurʾān as the comprehensive verbal expression of realities manifested through the Prophet's corporeal temperament, heart, words and character. Al-Mahāʾimī (in the working Gnamma translation, MSH-055–058, pp. 67–70) distinguishes bodily, religious/legal, luminous spiritual and inward heart-centred modalities of Muhammadan inheritance while expressly keeping the historical Prophet within creaturely servanthood. These are later Akbarian accounts, not propositions stated verbatim by every early narrator. The divine Names remain God's; their created manifestation does not constitute incarnation or an independent second source of creation.

The *insān kāmil* as comprehensive *barzakh* is neither an aggregate of numerous isolated virtues nor simply an agent with unusually flexible practical judgment. In Ibn ʿArabī and Qūnawī the *maqām lā maqām* denotes realization not confined by one particular station or Name. Its ethical significance may involve perception of different rightful claims within a particular human encounter, but its ontological and spiritual meaning exceeds that consequence. Chittick's [study of Qūnawī and the Akbarian school](https://ibnarabisociety.org/qunawi-in-the-school-of-ibn-arabi-william-chittick/) provides a useful comparison; the Gnamma *al-Nuṣūṣ*, Text Four, relates particular tastings to a claimed comprehensive witnessing. A transmitted prophetic action may genuinely express that integration without granting the historian unmediated access to the Prophet's whole inner state.

The metaphysical comparison with Logos also requires distinctions of level. Schuon's *Understanding Islam* relates the Muhammadan Spirit to Logos while rejecting literal divine incarnation; Guénon's *Buddhi/Mahat* belongs to formless manifestation and is not automatically equivalent to the first principial determination used by certain Akbarian authors. Neither a simple identity of Sanskrit and Arabic terms nor a literal higher-dimensional physics of the Prophet follows. These comparisons concern the differential apprehension of an integrated original through necessarily partial conceptual perspectives.

The final mission’s historical forms give this account determinate content. Recitation presents the signs; instruction teaches revelation and practical wisdom; purification removes the habits that obstruct a truthful response. The address includes people beyond the first recipients. Its continuance involves teachers and practices that communicate inherited guidance, not new claimants to prophetic revelation.

The prophetic stories continue to form their hearers.

The Qurʾān’s earlier prophets are not merely a genealogy before Muḥammad. Their stories strengthen him in his actual mission and instruct his hearers in endurance, confidence and response. Their narration belongs to the divine act of guidance. The history they relate and the formation they effect are connected but distinguishable functions.

Recitation alone does not exhaust this formation. The revealed word accommodates human reception through letters and speech, while its purpose includes understanding and action. Attention, comprehension and transformation therefore remain distinct. One can preserve the wording while failing to let it govern conduct.

The same passage may awaken fear, hope, gratitude, restraint or resolve according to its meaning. Comprehensiveness lies in a responsive heart and rightly ordered action, not in a permanent mood or the repetition of one favoured spiritual vocabulary. Each revealed address demands a response appropriate to its meaning, not conformity to a preferred spiritual mood.

Later generations preserved the prophets' guidance through recited scripture, teaching, worship and the practices of religious communities. Those forms could sustain learning long after a prophet's death, but their effectiveness depended upon people who understood and lived what they transmitted.

## 02. Land, settlement and the political worlds of the Near East

**Matn.** Human life depends upon land, water, plants, animals and the labour of others. People use knowledge and practical skill to cultivate, herd and transform the resources on which they depend. Cultivation requires attention to seasons; storage makes the harvest of one year available to another; craft transforms natural materials into instruments; and exchange carries goods beyond the places where they arise. These activities require cooperation and give people responsibilities toward those whose work makes their livelihoods possible.

Along the Nile, the annual flood sustained cultivation whose rhythms joined households, villages and successive political authorities. In southern Mesopotamia, channels of the Tigris–Euphrates system supported fields and settlements requiring continuing maintenance and decisions about water. Neither landscape produced one inevitable political order. Local cooperation preceded and persisted alongside the enlargement of temples, palaces and royal administrations. The concentration of harvests supported artisans, soldiers, scribes, worship and public works; it also made possible heavier exactions, compulsory labour and the accumulation of power. The records used to distribute food to workers could also be used to demand taxes and labour from farmers.

Writing and accounting made obligations durable beyond personal recollection. Records distinguished quantities, recipients, stores and transfers; contracts and correspondence extended cooperation across distance and time. Yet a record establishes what has been entered, not whether every demand recorded is just. Kingship similarly brought powers of protection, adjudication and coordination that could be exercised for the common good or converted into domination. The growth of civilization therefore enlarged both the possibilities of human service and the consequences of moral disorder.

Other communities lived through mobility rather than continuous settlement. Herding joined access to pasture and water with seasonal movement, while merchants connected places whose inhabitants could not produce all that they required. Caravan journeys depended upon animals, guides, credit, hosts and agreements governing passage. Town and countryside, cultivator and pastoralist, merchant and producer were not successive stages of a single development but differently organized participants in wider systems of livelihood. Their relations could be cooperative, unequal or openly hostile according to circumstances and conduct.

Between Egypt, Mesopotamia and Anatolia, the Levant contained coastal ports, cultivated plains, upland settlements and routes into Arabia. Its rulers negotiated with stronger kingdoms while households continued the recurrent work of farming, herding and exchange. During the second millennium BCE, Egyptian authority extended through networks of dependent city rulers, soldiers and tribute. The disruption of several Late Bronze Age political and commercial systems around 1200 BCE did not destroy every settlement or practice. Older centres survived, others declined, and new political configurations arose, including the Phoenician coastal cities, Aramaean kingdoms and the emerging communities of Israel and Judah. Political forms changed unevenly while the obligations of provision, protection and judgment remained.

Arabia presented further variations. Oases sustained cultivation through the protection and management of scarce water, while movement across arid country required knowledge of routes and reliable arrangements with those controlling places of rest. Settled and mobile lives interacted without one being reducible to the other. The later sanctuary-centred order of Makkah would possess its own history and obligations; its significance cannot be deduced from the political institutions of Egypt or Mesopotamia.

The prophets addressed people who cultivated land, exchanged goods, raised families and lived under rulers. They taught that wealth, ancestry and political power did not release anyone from responsibility to God. The obligation to worship God therefore bears upon the use of property, the conduct of exchange, the treatment of dependants and the exercise of judgment. Powerful institutions could support worship and justice, but could also be used to oppress. Religious communities sometimes preserved their teaching after losing the kingdoms that had once protected them. In Israel and Judah, the loss of royal power and sanctuary would test this distinction: the covenant could not be identified simply with the survival of a king, a territory or a building.

**Sharḥ.** Provision, embodied action and the purpose of earthly knowledge.

The human body lives through conditions that it does not originate. Rain precedes harvest, fertile soil precedes cultivation, animals and plants possess forms and capacities independent of human choice, and no one person's abilities suffice for every stage between seed and meal. The Qurʾān directs the human being to consider food and then names rain, the splitting of the earth, grain, fruit and pasture (80:24–32). Food is neither simply a product of human mastery nor a miraculous gift that dispenses with intermediaries. Cultivation and cooking require work upon things whose existence and efficacy remain received. Al-Rāzī distinguishes the conditions through which food comes into being from the processes through which it nourishes the body: attention to provision must trace actual intermediate causes rather than halt at the consumed object. 

The command to inhabit and work the earth does not confer independent dominion over it. In the address of Ṣāliḥ, God has brought human beings forth from the earth and *istaʿmarakum fīhā* (11:61). Al-Ṭabarī explains the latter as causing people to dwell in or inhabit it. The verse joins the created setting of human life with worship and repentance, not with a particular constitution for the state or an unconditional mandate to appropriate whatever land can be seized. Human labour becomes an exercise of knowledge when it takes account of the properties of things, the claims of other people and the dependence of every result upon more than the agent's will. 

A cultivator who studies soil and seasons exercises genuinely valid knowledge of the world; an administrator who allocates irrigation waters must also judge competing claims. These are related but not identical excellences. The Qurʾān's description of human settlement in the earth (11:61) gives neither an unqualified title to exploit it nor a technical manual for reservoirs. Human beings act through materials whose properties they must learn, other persons whose rights they must respect, and ends that order their choices. The later Muhammadan example makes this earthly work relevant to worship without dissolving agriculture, medicine or building into purely religious metaphors. The connection is explanatory: sound attention to material reality makes responsible action possible.

Interdependence is neither equality of functions nor a warrant for domination.

Households require many unlike activities: some cultivate, some preserve, carry, grind, cook, record, heal, construct or judge. Specialization can extend the effectiveness of each activity because it permits learning through repetition and cooperation across persons. It also creates asymmetries of information and power. The person who controls a store may determine access to food; the one who assesses a harvest may misrepresent its yield; the person whose labour is indispensable may nevertheless lack the power to negotiate its terms. Each advantage needs an ordering principle beyond the mere fact of being advantageous.

Qurʾān 43:32 speaks of differentiated livelihoods and some people making use of the services of others. The immediate argument rejects the attempt by powerful Meccans to nominate the kind of person upon whom revelation ought to descend. Al-Rāzī observes that the opponents mistook wealth and public eminence for the relevant measure of prophetic excellence. The verse recognizes dependence within worldly life while simultaneously denying that possession of worldly advantage authorizes a claim to divine election. Its description of economic disparity is not a moral endorsement of every hierarchy, contract or regime of labour that happens to exist. 

An administration is therefore answerable to a dual test. It must know enough to preserve the material process for which it is responsible, and it must exercise its knowledge according to the obligations actually owed. A ruler unable to anticipate scarcity may fail through incompetence; a ruler who anticipates scarcity and withholds stores to coerce dependants may fail through injustice. Practical intelligence and right disposition belong together, but one does not substitute for the other. This conjunction will later appear in Yūsuf's claim to be both trustworthy and knowledgeable concerning the storehouses (12:55).

The Nile: the flood's rhythm and the limits of centralization.

Egyptian agriculture depended on the Nile's annual inundation and the recession that left fields ready for sowing. The level and timing of the flood varied; cultivators needed to adjust seed, labour and the protection of stores to the land actually available. Much ancient cultivation used basins that received floodwater and were then drained or allowed to recede. Coordination occurred at the scale of adjacent fields, communities and local districts. It did not require every farmer's irrigation decision to be issued from the royal court. Extensive perennial irrigation, in the later sense of year-round regulated water, must not be projected without qualification into the early pharaonic periods. 

A successful village could maintain embankments and organize work without owning the entire river. Royal authority could nevertheless tax the yield, mobilize workers and command transport over distances exceeding the reach of a single village. These are different kinds of power. A central government might survey fields, supervise levies or maintain strategic works while local groups controlled the immediate distribution of labour and water. The possibility of taxation does not prove central origination of irrigation, and the existence of local cooperation does not eliminate royal coercion. 

Storage separates the time of production from the time of consumption. A granary can sustain workers who are building, travelling or unable to cultivate; it can preserve grain against an uncertain harvest and support people whose crafts serve others. It also creates a point at which access becomes conditional upon an office, a seal, a command or an imposed debt. The farmer bears the first risk of a failed crop while an authority may possess the means to transfer the burden to another district. The actual outcome depends on assessment, collection, transport and distribution. A general statement that the state either 'provided prosperity' or 'extracted surplus' conceals the work by which either result occurs.

The Nile was thus neither an automatic engine of despotism nor a sufficient explanation of Egyptian kingship. Collective discipline, local expertise, courtly religion, military power and established social relations combined in historically changing ways. Since prosperity and extraction could employ the same channels and stores, judging an institution requires knowledge of the persons protected, the burdens imposed and the purpose for which resources were directed.

Mesopotamia: channels, city growth and the changing scale of obligation.

The lower Tigris–Euphrates plain presented another combination of possibilities and constraints. Rivers and distributaries shifted their courses; fields were supplied through natural levee openings, distributary systems and, increasingly, artificial channels. Early arrangements could be maintained by relatively small communities. More developed canal networks involved agreements about excavation, water timing, land access and maintenance. By the third millennium BCE, cities and royal or temple administrations could coordinate works extending beyond the immediate settlement, but the resulting power did not extinguish every smaller authority. 

An upstream diversion illustrates the difference between a technical capacity and a legitimate claim. A channel may improve the crops of one district while reducing the share or raising the maintenance burden of another. Its proper construction requires knowledge of gradients, soils and water flow; its rightful administration requires determining whose fields are served, who performs the work, and who bears the cost. Technical improvement and justice cannot be read off the same fact. Neither can every dispute be attributed to a uniquely evil intention: scarcity can produce competing genuine needs that remain to be judged.

Urban life intensified the effects of decisions made at such junctions. By the late fourth millennium BCE, Uruk had become one of the principal large cities of southern Mesopotamia. Concentrated populations could support scribes, potters, metalworkers and administrators who did not directly cultivate the fields supplying them. The arrangement was not a single indivisible 'temple economy'. Temples and palaces possessed estates and administrative offices, while households, local authorities and private economic actors retained roles that differed by place and century. Surviving institutional archives are disproportionately rich evidence for official activity; they cannot by themselves establish that all production and exchange took place within institutions whose documents survived. 

The city made recurrent obligations both more numerous and more durable. A worker could receive rations rather than harvest grain personally; an official could be accountable for stores he had never grown; a ruler could command resources drawn from people he might never meet. Such distance enlarges the demand for reliable measures and recognized duties. It also increases the opportunities for a powerful office to conceal the persons whose labour makes its decisions effective.

The account: what the earliest writing could—and could not—secure.

A clay tablet from southern Mesopotamia dating to approximately 3300–3100 BCE records the allocation of rations. A further Uruk-period tablet of the late fourth or early third millennium represents an administrative account of products such as barley and malt. These objects preserve signs for quantities, commodities and transfers from a time before fully developed cuneiform became suitable for extended literary composition. The first documents were not primarily abstract statements of law or theology. They grew amid procedures through which goods were received, allocated and remembered. 

A memory held outside an individual gives other persons access to a decision after the decision-maker has departed. A ration list can show which worker is owed food, reveal a discrepancy between receipts and stores or preserve the terms of a transaction across an interval. It enables greater precision of coordination and supplies evidence with which to question an officer. Its use also presupposes persons able to make and interpret the marks. Those who lack such access may find their own entitlement represented by an authority who controls the document.

The distinction between a record and a just account is exact. A scribe may enter the amount ordered rather than the amount fairly due. The storage register may be accurate while the allocation it records is oppressive. Conversely, the ability to reconstruct a lost obligation can protect a weaker claimant against the forgetfulness or denial of a stronger one. Written evidence thus enlarges human power without specifying its moral direction. The record is an instrument of truth when answerable to the reality it represents, not merely because it is written.

Writing later served letters, contracts, school exercises, ritual, law and literary remembrance. Some systems of verbal transmission also preserved matters that an archive did not contain. No simple contrast between 'written administration' and 'oral religion' describes these societies. Priests and scribes belonged to religious institutions; families handed on obligations through practice and speech; and the same document could have legal, economic and sacred implications. Yet a fiscal register anchored to one estate and a teaching deliberately reproduced across households face different conditions of survival. Their distinction becomes especially consequential when a kingdom or temple ceases to function.

Pastoral mobility, households and trading corridors.

Seasonal mobility answers to the spatial distribution of pasture, water and risk. A flock cannot always graze the same ground without depletion; an animal may make use of land unsuitable for cultivation; a household may maintain relationships with settlements in which grain or tools can be obtained. Mobile groups therefore neither simply precede cities nor exist wholly outside their economies. Herding, farming and trade can be joined within a single family network, across successive seasons or through exchanges between different communities. Political authority may follow a group of people rather than apply uniformly to a bounded tract of land.

A journey introduces requirements that a local harvest cannot meet. The caravan needs animals, fodder, repair, secure halts, trustworthy information, guides and agreements with persons capable of obstructing passage. Credit permits goods to move before their final sale, but spreads vulnerability across partners who may be separated for months. In the Old Assyrian trading networks of the early second millennium BCE, merchants from Ashur carried tin and textiles to Anatolia and obtained metals in return. The Kültepe/Kanesh tablets record consignments, loans, household correspondence, disputes and transaction costs. They attest a commercial organization that depended on both settled scribal practice and repeated travel, and on households as well as itinerant merchants. 

A route is therefore not a physical line alone. Its viability depends upon continuing assurances: the donkey and its load need food and water, the merchant needs safe passage and a forum to settle a debt, and the buyer needs confidence that goods and information are what they claim to be. Local hospitality and protection become economic and moral relations rather than optional sentiment. A powerful group can profit by guaranteeing passage; it can also turn control of a well or narrow crossing into extortion. In neither case does the desert determine the morality of the arrangement.

The contrast between a canal and a caravan is consequently relative, not absolute. Both involve material infrastructures, accumulated knowledge, specialized labour and enforceable understandings. A canal directs water along a constrained course; a caravan moves commodities between separated centres. They differ in the kind of territory and personnel through which decisions take effect. Rivers could carry boats, and desert towns could possess settled archives. No division between 'bureaucratic peoples' and 'tribal peoples' follows from the geographical distinction.

The Levant under competing powers.

The Levant linked northern Syrian cities and Anatolia with the Nile region, the Mediterranean and the approaches to Arabia. Coastal ports had access to shipping and imported materials; valleys and uplands supported different combinations of cultivation, herding and settlement. A kingdom controlling one prosperous city did not thereby administer the countryside continuously or command every route used by neighbouring communities. Its effective authority depended on surrounding villages, local chiefs, armed personnel and the willingness or inability of greater rulers to intervene.

The fourteenth-century BCE Amarna correspondence makes the structure unusually tangible. Vassal rulers in Canaan wrote to the Egyptian court about rival rulers, security, service, trade and tribute. Their professions of submission did not eliminate their local conflicts; subordination to Pharaoh could coexist with attempts to attract his support against a rival. A demand imposed through the imperial system became a burden carried by particular inhabitants, even when the written exchange records only the negotiations of their rulers. The letters are invaluable for political relations, but their elite perspective cannot be treated as a complete account of every village or household. 

An imperial change altered the practical conditions of local life without always replacing its people. A new overlord might demand tribute, station soldiers, appoint or confirm a local ruler, interfere with disputes or direct goods along a different road. Traders might gain protected access to a market while cultivators faced increased assessment; a town might be spared destruction but lose the autonomy of its decisions. The effects of empire must be followed through these mechanisms rather than expressed solely as a succession of dynastic names.

Destruction and renewal at the end of the Bronze Age.

Around 1200 BCE several interconnected palace-centred regimes of the eastern Mediterranean were disrupted. Some sites experienced destruction or abandonment; the Hittite imperial system and Ugarit's palace order ceased in their earlier forms; trading patterns and political alliances were reorganized. Yet the processes differed sharply by region. Coastal communities, especially those later identified as Phoenician, preserved substantial continuities, and other settlements adapted or developed under changed conditions. There was no single uniform day of collapse and no secure explanation assigning every regional change to one climate event, invading people or political failure. 

This unevenness matters because institutions survive in different pieces. A royal court may disappear while a harbour continues to operate; a long-range political alliance may end while village fields remain cultivated; a temple may lose an endowment while households continue older rites. Destruction can interrupt knowledge, but it may also redistribute the persons who carry it. Neither outcome is determined solely by the former power of the institution. The later Iron Age involved the formation of new regional configurations, including the Aramaean kingdoms and the small kingdoms of Israel and Judah, alongside enduring cities of the coast. These developments must be dated and reconstructed from their own sources rather than made consequences of one generalized civilizational catastrophe. 

Political plurality also changed the settings in which allegiance could be expressed. A community dependent upon a patron king may face obligations to him and to an external empire; a local religious law may command conduct that the existing government does not reward. Such conditions do not establish that one religious community was uniformly more faithful than another. They give concrete form to the distinction between the validity of an obligation and the coercive capacity of the institution ordinarily associated with it.

Arabia's oases and the coexistence of different forms of life.

Arid lands concentrate settlement at places where groundwater, springs or episodic flows permit dependable cultivation. Protection of a well or cultivated enclosure can be as important to a household as possession of a field. Agreements over water affect planting, animal use, hospitality and the possibility of passage. Oases are not merely temporary stopping places for mobile groups: they can support durable households, specialized production, written communication and fortifications.

Archaeological work in northwestern Arabia identifies long-standing walled oasis settlements, including Taymāʾ and Qurayyah, with roots in the Bronze Age; the chronology and function of individual walls remain under research. Excavations at Dadan in the al-ʿUlā valley provide safely dated evidence of local date-palm cultivation in the twelfth–eleventh centuries BCE and greater oasis development in the first millennium BCE. These finds support distinct, long-lived forms of Arabian settlement rather than a picture of an undifferentiated desert inhabited only by nomads. 

Oases did not make mobile exchange unnecessary. The cultivator needed tools or goods absent from the locality; moving groups needed water, provisions and sometimes markets; towns and travellers depended upon arrangements able to withstand disputes. The resulting interdependence could be stabilizing or exploitative. A fortified wall may protect crops and stores or mark the coercive reach of whoever controls them. The evidence does not permit an unbroken institutional genealogy from Bronze Age Taymāʾ or Dadan to the Quraysh of sixth-century Makkah. Makkah's specific sanctity, local power and seasonal trading practices require their own historically dated reconstruction.

Sacred places and political places: related but not identical.

Palace, sanctuary, market and household may share a locality while exercising different claims upon people. A temple may possess an estate or receive goods, but its rites do not reduce to the management of property. A king may protect a sanctuary and still be answerable to obligations that his protection does not create. A market can operate within political guarantees without receiving its standards of fairness from the mere will of the guarantor. A holy centre may draw people who owe political obedience elsewhere. Its religious role does not require it to function as the capital of the largest neighbouring state.

Each institution has both a proper service and a characteristic deformation. Storage becomes hoarding when goods needed for a legitimate purpose are withheld without right. Protection becomes monopoly when a guardian treats those protected as possessions. Sacred stewardship becomes appropriation when custodians present access to worship as an exclusive entitlement of their lineage or office. Kingship becomes tyranny when coordination and judgment are subordinated to the ruler's independent appetite for power. These are differences of action and relation, not conclusions drawn from the size or outward splendour of an institution.

A religious centre can unite people whose territories, languages and trades remain diverse. The Abrahamic House will give this possibility a particular revealed form. Its role cannot be deduced from the shapes of earlier Near Eastern temples, nor does its sacredness erase the material acts through which visitors are fed, defended and received. The contrast between service to a sanctuary and possession of its privileges later becomes acute in the Meccan Sīrah.

Prophetic judgment enters the actual arrangements of provision.

The prophetic stories name several misdirections of genuine capacities rather than condemning the capacities themselves. Hūd's people use construction and strength; the reproach falls upon imagined permanence and oppressive force (26:128–130). Ṣāliḥ's people cultivate, build and use a common water source; their test concerns the specified share owed to the divine sign (26:146–158). Shuʿayb confronts the use of commercial ingenuity to falsify measure and diminish the claims of others (11:84–88). Yūsuf's interpretation becomes a public discipline of cultivation, preservation and distribution (12:47–49). Mūsā confronts a ruler who has made the ordering of a population into a means of humiliation and violence (28:4). The Qurʾān's particular acts prevent 'civilization' from becoming an empty praise or an equally empty accusation.

The material conditions must be specified before the moral judgment can be understood. An accurate measure has a physical relation to the goods measured; withholding part of an agreed quantity injures the recipient rather than merely displaying an unattractive disposition. A water schedule is implemented at a place and time; destroying the protected claimant changes what others can actually receive. A grain reserve sustains life only when protected against deterioration and allocated when needed. A ruler's instruction becomes oppression through officials, categories, exactions and acts imposed upon bodies. Right worship reaches these transactions because they involve the rights of persons and creatures whose reality does not originate in the powerful agent's desire.

Providence does not absolve an unjust actor by bringing an unexpected good from his action. A great city can preserve art and knowledge while committing wrongs against those who build it. Nor does material success establish that a people have accepted guidance. The proper relation between inward recognition and outward discipline must be enacted through practices and institutions capable of responding to actual claims.

Knowledge and obligations beyond the loss of sovereignty.

Material records and religious transmissions have different vulnerabilities. A field survey may retain its precision only while the same boundaries and units remain in force; a temple archive may become obsolete when its estate is confiscated. An injunction, remembered account, prayer or legal teaching may be repeated in a different place, although language, instruction and stable communal practice are still necessary for its survival. It would be mistaken to oppose a territorially fixed state to a wholly immaterial religion. Teaching depends on teachers and households, texts on copying and preservation, rites on places and times, and justice on some means of applying judgments.

The distinction is between the obligation itself and the political apparatus that can protect or enforce it. A community may preserve an inherited command after losing its king; it may also preserve a formula while forgetting the disposition or understanding through which that formula is rightly lived. Conversely, the survival of a crown or building is not evidence that the duty it once served remains effective. The forms of continuity are therefore multiple: textual, pedagogical, familial, liturgical, judicial and political. Each can support the others without being identical to them.

The histories of Israel and Judah embody both the strength and the difficulty of this differentiation. Kings, territory and the Jerusalem sanctuary became powerful supports for communal life. Defeat and displacement transformed those supports without simply abolishing the possibility of teaching, worship or covenantal identity. The historical question is not whether a people can dispense with every institution, but which institutions can carry an obligation when its former political setting no longer exists and which practices prevent that obligation from becoming only a name.

## 03. Israel and Judah: kingship, destruction and life beyond a kingdom

**Matn.** In the lands of Israel and Judah, kingship brought together the care of sanctuaries, military defence, the administration of land and the settlement of disputes. The Qurʾān remembers Dāwūd and Sulaymān as prophets entrusted with judgment and dominion, yet political authority, even when legitimate, did not replace the obligation of ruler and subject to answer to God. Rulers and families alike remained responsible for their conduct. A kingdom could defend a sanctuary and administer justice, but political power alone could not secure fidelity to God.

The Assyrian capture of Samaria in 722/721 BCE broke the northern kingdom's political autonomy. Babylon subsequently imposed its authority upon Judah, deported some of its inhabitants in 597, and destroyed Jerusalem's temple in 587/586 BCE; other inhabitants remained. The experience of conquest, deprivation and dispersal altered the means by which a community maintained worship and teaching. Kinship, transmitted instruction, sacred calendars, remembered judgments and the practices of households could continue where a dynasty and sanctuary could no longer function as before. Persian conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE permitted a return and the reconstruction of temple worship, though Jewish life remained distributed among several regions and under foreign rule.

Over subsequent centuries, Jewish communities lived across Mesopotamia, the Levant and the Mediterranean and eventually in parts of Arabia. By the Prophet's lifetime, Jewish groups in Yathrib cultivated land and participated in alliances while preserving scriptural identities. Their exact origins were diverse and cannot all be traced to the Babylonian deportations. Jewish religious life continued even where independent Jewish kingship had ended, sustained by families, teachers, communal worship and written traditions. Later Muslim communities would face related questions about preserving religious life under changing rulers, though the two histories followed different paths.

**Sharḥ.** Royal power and prophetic guidance must be distinguished first at the level of their objects. A ruler has authority over certain affairs of protection, administration and judgment; the divine command addresses the just exercise of those offices and also the relations of people not occupying an office. The Qurʾānic accounts of Dāwūd's judgment (38:20–26) and Sulaymān's understanding in the case of the cultivated field (21:78–79) join practical discrimination of particular claims to knowledge bestowed by God. They do not license a simple equivalence between victorious rule and divine approval. Different narrations of the judgments must be evaluated, and the Qurʾān does not establish every procedural detail supplied in later reports.

After the destruction of the kingdoms of Israel and Judah, families and teachers continued to transmit worship, law and remembered identity under changing rulers. The loss of sovereignty did not terminate every practice, although it exposed communities to exile, coercion and differences in what could be taught publicly. The Qurʾānic memories of Dāwūd and Sulaymān bring together prophecy and kingship without equating royal power with all divine guidance. In the later Muhammadan order, public authority, learned transmission and household teaching would likewise be distinguishable, even though their ultimate religious orientation could be shared. The historical connection is comparative rather than a claim of a directly copied Jewish institutional model.

Exile was a military and political displacement, not a literal downward passage through the ontological grades. The episode poses a concrete problem of *formal continuity under a change of matter and power*: the functions once supported by monarch, land and sanctuary acquire new patterns of household, scripture and communal instruction. The principial distinction is between a received end and the contingent institutions that serve it. The historical causes of exile remain Assyrian and Babylonian conquest and related political processes, not a symbolism deduced from the divine Names.

This also limits easy comparisons with later Muslim institutions. A medieval madrasa, an early Jewish scribal network and a family gathering for prayer are not the same social formation. What recurs is the problem of forming persons capable of carrying teachings across changes of ruler, language and territory. The specific relationship between Judean history and Arabian Jewish settlement remains a separate historical inquiry.

The transition from monarchy to dispersion changes the central historical problem. When the temple could no longer function and the king no longer secured the territory, continuity depended more heavily on scribal teaching, kin relations, calendar, prayer, law and ritual practice. The return under Persian supremacy restored a centre but not a self-sufficient sovereign state. This helps explain how identifiable Jewish communities could persist through Greek, Roman and Sasanian changes of rule. Jewish communities maintained religious institutions in Arabia, but the surviving evidence does not trace every family's ancestry to the Babylonian exile.

The changing medium of religious continuity is the decisive bridge. Royal patronage can maintain a central sanctuary; a dispersed community must teach children, preserve forms of worship, settle questions internally and recognize people of the same religious tradition elsewhere. Some of these activities developed over long periods, and their exact early chronology is contested. The conceptual connection to Islam's later history is nevertheless concrete: after the caliphate fragmented, Muslim communities too relied upon learning, law and travel beyond the reach of one political centre. Judean communities transmitted scripture through priests and scribes; Islamic madrasas formed much later under different political and educational conditions.

The Arabian connection must remain deliberately modest. Evidence that Jews were established in Yathrib in the Prophet's lifetime is not evidence identifying the specific route by which each family reached it. Jewish communities established roots in Arabia over different periods, but surviving sources do not establish one ancestry or migration date for all of them. The stronger line of explanation is how religious observance and distinctive communal law could exist under governments not controlled by those communities.

The Qurʾān recounts the missions of Mūsā, Dāwūd and Sulaymān (2:47–61; 21:78–82; 34:10–14); Kings, Chronicles and Ezra–Nehemiah preserve related but distinct histories, while Near Eastern evidence helps date the conquests of 722, 597 and 587/586 BCE. The Qurʾān and the biblical books recount some shared prophetic figures but differ in emphases, narrative structure and theological claims. Late-antique Jewish communities could be connected by learning and ritual despite local variations in language, social hierarchy and political status. This will later matter in Madinah, where the Prophet did not encounter an abstract “Judaism” but households and groups with land, alliances and particular obligations. Before that, the Persian and Greek worlds changed the languages and intellectual settings in which such communities lived.

## 04. Religious disciplines and the changing world of the first millennium BCE

**Matn.** In the centuries after the great Near Eastern kingdoms lost their earlier forms, communities increasingly sustained learning across political and linguistic boundaries. Persian kings linked widely separated provinces; Greek-speaking dynasties inherited much of this territory after Alexander; and scriptural traditions, philosophical arguments and forms of religious discipline moved through courts, cities, households and teaching communities. Jews preserved and interpreted Torah in different languages; Greek philosophical schools investigated the intelligibility of nature, political life and the good; other teachers cultivated rites, moral training and contemplative disciplines.

In northern India, several traditions debated ritual, renunciation and release; in China, teachers examined human cultivation and the obligations of ruler and family. Their histories did not unfold according to one common timetable. Some intellectual contacts were mediated by identifiable institutions and routes, but similarities between doctrines do not themselves prove a journey of teachers or texts. Each of these traditions developed through its own teachers, texts and social conditions.

Communities distinguished ritual, speculative inquiry, ethical formation, jurisprudence and contemplative knowledge according to different inherited purposes. Sometimes their cultivation preserved an integral religious life; elsewhere their practitioners disputed who possessed legitimate authority. In the eastern Mediterranean and western Asia, translation and schooling contributed to the intellectual worlds encountered by Jewish and Christian communities under Greek and then Roman rule. Muslims would later encounter these earlier traditions through specific texts, translations and communities of learning. Similar ideas across distant regions do not, by themselves, establish that those peoples had been in direct contact.

**Sharḥ.** Different teachings address different deficiencies and human possibilities. A rite disciplines the body and shared time; an ethical regimen trains desire and deliberate action; philosophical inquiry distinguishes arguments and principles; contemplation seeks knowledge not exhausted by ordinary discursive descriptions. Yet these modes cannot be positioned automatically on a single ladder of spiritual superiority. Their actual aims and relations depend upon the tradition and text under examination. The later Islamic distinctions among *fiqh*, *adab*, *kalām* and *taṣawwuf* offer an illuminating comparison only when the historical and doctrinal differences are preserved.

The philosophical schools, rituals and spiritual disciplines that appeared across different regions cannot be fitted onto one documented global chronology of revelation. Their particular objects nevertheless clarify a principial issue: knowledge of nature, moral formation, contemplation and political order require different kinds of attention. Ibn ʿArabī's later language of the Muhammadan *maqām lā maqām* does not describe a generic mixture of cultures; according to Chittick, it concerns realization that is not confined to any one particular spiritual station. Cross-cultural comparison may disclose analogous questions, but it does not make all doctrinal answers equivalent or prove contact where the sources record none. The historical institutions must remain distinct from the metaphysical analogy.

In the Mediterranean, administrative authority, urban roads, schools, translation and the movement of merchants provide concrete mechanisms through which intellectual practices could be transmitted. The Septuagint arose through Jewish engagement with Greek language; later Christian use of Greek and Syriac unfolded in different social circumstances. These are specific acts of linguistic mediation, with real agents and divergent results. The existence of a comparable idea in India or China does not establish an analogous institutional transfer without independent evidence.

Guénon's cyclic reading can still function as a question about the historical predominance of differentiated disciplines and the obscuration of direct principial knowledge. It cannot function as a substitute for dating the emergence of an Indian teaching, a Greek argument or a Chinese school. Nor should it make institutional differentiation inherently corrupt: durable and specialized instruction may also help preserve higher purposes within changing societies.

Karl Jaspers later grouped several developments under the label “Axial Age”; their different dates and settings do not establish one common historical cause. The dates of the Buddha, Zoroaster, Laozi and individual early Greek teachers are disputed to varying degrees. Connections must therefore be shown individually: an imperial road or shared administrative language can support movement of people and texts, whereas similarity between two doctrines does not by itself prove borrowing. Persia's administrative reach and the Hellenistic spread of Greek give stronger grounds for certain western Eurasian contacts than for a single transformation spanning India, China and Greece.

Guénon's argument also requires a correction often missing in its popular retellings. He considered the sixth century a threshold of what he called ordinary historical accessibility, but surviving Mesopotamian, Egyptian and other sources allow detailed chronology much earlier. His historical premise cannot be accepted without qualification, and his judgment of Buddhism or philosophy reflects his own conception of orthodoxy and intellectual hierarchy. The intellectually fruitful question is narrower: did some traditions respond to changing social conditions by differentiating ritual, practical discipline and contemplative knowledge? That can be studied separately in each tradition. It matters for the later Islamic account because philosophy, law, revelation and spiritual realization also came to be distinguishable modes of teaching, though their boundaries were drawn differently.

Guénon's metaphysical interpretation of sacred cycles and the dated history of individual teachers address different kinds of claims. Achaemenid government, Hellenistic cities and Greek scriptural translations created identifiable political and linguistic links across the western Near East. For China and India, the comparisons concern developing traditions of moral and contemplative training; their presence in one paragraph is not an argument that they directly shaped first-century Christianity or the seventh-century Ḥijāz. Guénon's sixth-century claim is a metaphysical-periodizing judgment with its own disputed historical assumptions, not an empirical explanation of the co-occurrence of named founders.

René Guénon's *The Crisis of the Modern World*, chapter 1, gives a different reading: he associates transformations around the sixth century BCE with an increasingly differentiated and outward historical phase of traditional civilizations. His claim concerns spiritual cycles and transformations in the accessibility of principial knowledge, not simply a chronology of founders. Guénon interprets the changes through spiritual cycles; the available dates do not demonstrate that a single rupture occurred across Eurasia. The Prophet's contemporaries inherited a world of earlier scriptural communities, philosophical traditions and rival institutions; none of those inheritances alone explains the emergence of his message. The immediate lineage leading to the seventh-century Near East passes through Hellenistic and Roman Judaea, Christian churches and the imperial frontiers.

## 05. Jesus and the communities that carried his message

**Matn.** In Roman Judaea, Yaḥyā called people to repentance and ʿĪsā ibn Maryam proclaimed worship of God, righteousness and mercy among a people whose life was shaped by Jerusalem's sanctuary, the Law and competing authorities. The Qurʾān describes ʿĪsā as a word from God and a spirit from Him and recounts his healing and life-giving signs, performed only by God's permission. His call concerned the worship of God and the conduct of people in their daily lives.

His followers carried teachings across households and cities in the eastern Mediterranean. Letters, remembered accounts, recitation, worship and mutual assistance developed in communities facing questions about authority, inherited Jewish practice and the reception of non-Jews. The destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE transformed Jewish religious life, while Christian congregations multiplied in Greek-speaking and other regions. When imperial patronage grew after Constantine, bishops and councils addressed controversies concerning Christ's person and the authority of competing interpretations. Their conclusions did not erase the distinct histories of Syriac, Coptic, Greek, Armenian and other Christian traditions.

Monasteries, churches, schools and households sustained prayer and learning, sometimes across frontiers that divided political rulers. Before the Muhammadan mission, Arabia was connected with Christian worlds through trade, migration, diplomacy and neighbouring kingdoms. By the Prophet's lifetime, Christian communities near Arabia differed in language, worship and theological allegiance. The Qurʾānic engagement with Christians took place in a world where those differences mattered.

**Sharḥ.** The Qurʾānic designation of ʿĪsā as God's Word and a spirit from Him (4:171), together with the emphatic qualification “by My permission” in the accounts of his signs (5:110), requires both recognition of the extraordinary and preservation of servanthood. The Akbarian tradition develops a distinctive Christic wisdom concerning spirit, breath and life-giving manifestation; but its symbolic and metaphysical explanation must not be imported as a complete account of first-century events. The Christian accounts of Christ's person and the Qurʾānic account differ substantively; fair comparison requires their claims to be stated independently.

In Qurʾān 5:110, ʿĪsā's healing and extraordinary signs occur by God's permission. Their recipient is a living person in bodily need, not merely an object offered as evidence in an abstract controversy. The act of healing, the recipient's response, the prophet's divine dependence and the later transmission of reports can be separately studied without recreating the fullness of prophetic wisdom. Ibn ʿArabī's Christic *Fuṣūṣ* offers a later account of spirit, breath and manifestation, but cannot substitute for the first-century evidence or settle the Christian–Muslim doctrinal difference. This differentiated prophetic excellence is important to the Akbarian account of Muhammadan comprehensiveness: gathering distinct wisdoms does not erase their real differences.

The later movement from personal teaching to Christian institutions had several mediations: hearing and memorization; instruction within households; organization of meals and worship; transmission of letters and narratives; assignment of authority to bishops, monks and teachers; the policing of doctrine through councils under political conditions. These mechanisms do not amount to one simple story of the “institutionalization of an original spirituality.” Communities could preserve genuine faith and help their members grow while engaging in disputes and sometimes coercion.

Christian teaching and Syriac medical or philosophical learning later entered Arabic through identifiable translators, patrons and educational settings. That exchange should be documented separately from the theological encounter in the Prophet's lifetime. The distinction matters to ʿumrān: a community may inherit the same names and sacred stories through very different forms of political support, linguistic competence and instruction.

The Islamic account of ʿĪsā and Maryam belongs to revealed history (notably Qurʾān 3:33–55; 5:110–120; 19:16–36). The earliest recoverable Christian histories rest on a different kind of evidence: first-century letters, Gospel narratives, later patristic writings and documents of councils. Christian creeds developed through councils and theological disputes; their later formulations cannot automatically be attributed to the earliest disciples. The controversy surrounding Nicaea (325), Ephesus (431) and Chalcedon (451) needs to be understood as sustained argument among Christian communities, not as a single moment when Christianity became homogeneous.

Christianity also changed the geography of learning. In Greek-speaking eastern cities, philosophical and theological argument shared a vocabulary capable of crossing political boundaries. Syriac translators and teachers moved between theological, medical and philosophical work, sometimes under rulers of different religions. This made them potential mediators when Muslim patrons later sought Greek learning in Arabic. The intellectual bridge is therefore not a vague statement that Islam was 'influenced by Christianity'; it is the documented existence of bilingual scholars, texts and schools available within the regions that Muslim armies and merchants later encountered. In the Sīrah itself the immediate issues were worship, prophecy and protection, not the ninth-century translation of Aristotle.

A concrete mechanism in Christian expansion is linguistic and urban. Letters to congregations could circulate along roads and shipping routes connecting Greek-speaking cities; local assemblies could read them, dispute their meaning and transmit instruction even without a common local bishop. Later councils and imperial patronage made certain disputes politically consequential. At the Arabian frontier, however, Greek, Syriac, Coptic and Geʿez Christian lives did not form one uniform audience for Islam. Each source needs to identify the community it actually describes.

The causal thread is institutional as well as doctrinal. Greek and Syriac writing made teachings travel; churches offered regular worship and communal leadership; monasticism provided spaces for disciplined life, copying and teaching. Imperial patronage offered resources and created new occasions for controversy, since bishops and emperors could have different interests. Aksum's Christianity and Syriac-speaking communities in Persian territory meant that the Prophet's contemporaries encountered Christian practices beyond Constantinople's control. Muslims encountered Christian neighbours, envoys, protectors, clergy and monks as well as arguments about the person of Christ. Their routes of movement traversed empires and Arabian borderlands where religion, commerce and military protection intersected.

## 06. The sixth-century crossroads of Rome, Persia, Ethiopia and Arabia

**Matn.** In the sixth century, Arabia lay between several powerful states and religious communities. Roman government in Syria and Egypt, Sasanian authority in Iraq and Iran, and the Christian kingdom of Aksum across the Red Sea shaped frontiers through armies, administrators, trade and dependent rulers. Arab groups serving as allies or intermediaries possessed obligations different from those of provincial taxpayers or local sanctuary custodians. In Yemen, struggles involving Ḥimyar, Najrān, Aksum and Persia linked local religious loyalties with wider imperial aims. Persecution of Christians at Najrān and interventions across the Red Sea exposed the vulnerability of communities caught between powers.

In the Ḥijāz, settlements and routes required water, family alliances, security and organized hospitality. The Kaʿbah held religious importance, but its custodians did not operate the same bureaucracy as Constantinople or Ctesiphon. Jewish and Christian groups lived in several parts of Arabia alongside other forms of worship. Within the Ḥijāz, clan protection, household wealth and personal relationships shaped who could speak publicly and who could respond without fear.

Roman–Sasanian war began in 602 and devastated many provinces before Roman recovery during the 620s. Around 610, revelation came to Muḥammad ﷺ in Makkah, whose immediate social and religious order was not directly governed by either imperial court. The wars weakened the two empires before the later Muslim conquests. They do not explain the origin of the Prophet's revelation. The Prophet's first summons was heard in a household and clan setting; its consequences would eventually cross all these political frontiers.

**Sharḥ.** A principial reading of the Muhammadan mission cannot deduce its historical timing from the wars of surrounding empires. The universal character of the mission within Islamic revelation is a normative and spiritual claim; the war's impact on taxes, recruitment, local loyalties and military exhaustion is a historical claim supported by different evidence. They may bear upon the same eventual civilization without being one causal argument.

The sixth-century frontier worlds set the conditions within which people received the Prophet's summons, not the cause of revelation itself. A Meccan could enjoy clan protection while a client lacked it; a ruler at Najrān or Aksum could use sovereign authority in a way unavailable to an Arabian household. The divine end of guidance, the material ability to speak or travel, and the disposition to believe were consequently not the same kind of power. Their eventual conjunction in particular relationships—hosting, protection, hearing, recitation and allegiance—made the historical mission communicable. A metaphysical account of a universal prophetic reality does not explain the taxation of a Sasanian province or the exact timing of Rome's war.

Neither Rome nor Persia maintained seamless sovereign control across the Peninsula; the Ghassanid and Lakhmid worlds were systems of shifting patronage, alliance and competition. Nor does a Christian Ethiopian ruler belong to the same institutional category as a Meccan sanctuary guardian. The historical functions of military frontier defence, water provision, pilgrimage services, revenue collection and asylum require distinct descriptions. The horizontal has its own principles, which the vertical purpose does not replace.

Later Muslims entering Syria and Iraq confronted inherited systems of taxation, record keeping and provincial administration. The Prophet's first circle, by contrast, grew through household confidence, Qurʾānic recitation and interpersonal protection. The later empire and pre-prophetic Makkah had distinct modes of organization; the empire was not already fully implicit in Meccan structures. The larger historical sequence begins with human receptivity and communication and only later brings political authority into view.

An empire's presence on the frontier did not make every caravan route a branch of its administration. The Ghassanid and Lakhmid networks involved military patronage, kinship and competition, while the Red Sea introduced Ethiopian, South Arabian and Persian interests with different reach. Literary accounts of Najrān and the Aksumite invasion need comparison with epigraphic and archaeological evidence; Valentina Grasso's analysis of sixth-century South Arabia cautions against reducing every political intervention to a single religious motive. Frontier rulers mobilized military allies, collected revenues and offered varying degrees of protection to religious communities.

The battle between imperial powers also reshaped what an Arabian leader might encounter as expansion proceeded. Muslim forces moving into Syria entered territory with military roads, bishoprics and taxation already organized; movements into Iraq encountered Sasanian estates and administrative practices. The conquest did not invent those systems and could not rule without engaging them. Yet the Prophet's first listeners were not Roman subjects receiving a centrally enforced imperial creed. Meccan believers depended on personal protection and voluntary allegiance; after conquest, Muslim rulers administered the taxes and offices of former imperial provinces.

The Roman–Sasanian war weakened both empires before the Muslim conquests, but the surviving evidence does not establish that it caused the Prophetic proclamation. It did weaken both imperial systems before major Muslim conquests. Makkah's particular situation instead depended on sanctuary customs, Quraysh's clan arrangements and regional trade. Early Muslim sources place the Year of the Elephant near the Prophet's birth, but their accounts of the expedition and its dating differ. Abū Ṭālib protected Muḥammad through kinship obligations even though he did not adopt the new proclamation.

Najrān records Christian persecution and Aksumite intervention, but it supplies no direct causal evidence for Muḥammad's proclamation in Makkah. Najrān documents the presence of Christianity, regional persecution and cross-Red-Sea intervention; it does not prove that the Meccan message arose from imperial conflict. The Roman–Persian war matters differently: it transformed the military position of the two states into whose provinces Muslim forces would later move. Thus the local origin of the mission and the geopolitical environment of its later expansion have distinct histories.

# Book II — The formation of believers in Makkah

## 07. The sanctuary, the clans and the obligations of protection

**Matn.** The Kaʿbah was the centre of Makkah's religious life and drew visitors from tribes that did not always live at peace with one another. Quraysh families guarded sanctuary services, received visitors, managed trade and negotiated protections for people and journeys. The custodianship of the sanctuary brought responsibilities and prestige, but it did not prevent injustice. Clan protection could also shelter someone whose beliefs were opposed by his relatives.

Muḥammad ﷺ belonged to Banū Hāshim and lived within obligations of kinship and hospitality that neighbours recognized. His uncle Abū Ṭālib's standing would protect him through prolonged disagreement; enslaved persons, clients and unprotected visitors could not rely on the same defence. These differences shaped the reception of the Prophet's message: some could speak and worship under the protection of powerful relatives, while others risked punishment from their own households.

In Yathrib, households cultivated orchards and maintained fortified neighbourhoods; the Aws, Khazraj and Jewish groups held distinct alliances amid remembered conflict. The order of sanctuary, trade and kinship in Makkah was therefore not the only possible form of Arabian social life. The people of Yathrib would later offer the Prophet protection and a place in which the growing community could organize its common affairs.

**Sharḥ.** The sanctuary is a received trust before it is an opportunity for prestige or trade. Qurʾān 106 joins journeys with security and provision and directs worship to the Lord of the House. The distinction between serving the sacred centre and possessing its privileges makes the immediate conflict intelligible: an office can be necessary while its holder mistakes authority over its arrangements for independence from the purpose it serves. This is a normative contrast, not a psychological diagnosis of every Quraysh leader.

Before the first revelation, the Prophet's neighbours knew him within the moral life of Makkah: kinship obligations, care for those without protection, exchange and the trustworthiness of speech. Accounts of *Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl* remember a pre-prophetic agreement for protection, though the later report's details require separate historical scrutiny. A sacred precinct could bring different tribes together without producing just conduct in every encounter. This distinction would recur after the Hijrah: the correct purpose of an institution depends upon what its custodians actually do for other people. The subsequent Akbarian language of comprehensive prophetic character interprets that coherence; it is not evidence that Meccan participants possessed its later technical vocabulary.

Abū Ṭālib's protection illustrates another distinction. The justice of protecting an innocent relative cannot be measured solely by whether the protector accepts every truth proclaimed by that relative. A person may act honourably in one relation while disagreeing deeply in another. Conversely, allegiance to a clan is not a sufficient warrant to persecute an unprotected believer. Such differences are precisely why the integrated prophetic model cannot be reduced to one unvarying rule about loyalty.

The historical basis of Meccan commercial power remains debated. It would be misleading to infer from the sanctuary's sacred significance that it necessarily generated an imperial-scale market. The immediate problem of the early mission lay in actual protection and the uneven power to speak, hear and worship. Families and relationships constituted the small-scale social field from which larger collective commitments eventually arose.

Kinship and patronage determined who could challenge prevailing religious customs without immediately losing protection. A free man with an influential guardian, an enslaved person and a visitor without an alliance did not face the same risks when accepting a new religious allegiance. This social difference helps explain why early conversions followed ties of trust and household contact, and why some believers could resist pressure while others suffered violence. It would be anachronistic to describe Quraysh's sanctuary as a modern government. Tribal obligation, ritual privilege, negotiated protection and exchange could overlap without creating one centralized state. The Sīrah tradition's accounts of the Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl and the Prophet's protection by Abū Ṭālib are valuable evidence for how contemporaries remembered justice and patronage; the wording and sequence of those accounts need comparison across transmitters.

Clan protection helped the Prophet continue speaking in Makkah, while believers without powerful relatives were far more exposed to persecution. Abū Ṭālib's defence of his nephew was a recognizable kin obligation, even while religious disagreement persisted. A client or enslaved convert could lack a similarly effective protector. Accepting the new call was therefore not one uniform act of social defiance. The religion's claim of common accountability sat within highly unequal capacities to resist. Later migration did not remove the importance of kinship immediately; it placed inherited loyalties into new relations with people whose commitment rested upon worship rather than birth.

Mecca's exact commercial scale is disputed. Accounts of Quraysh's seasonal journeys record remembered patterns of commerce, whereas claims that particular Meccan families dominated long-distance luxury trade remain disputed. The protection system is more immediately relevant to the first believers: clan standing could shield a dissenting member while enslaved or client households faced coercion. That difference explains the uneven distribution of suffering without implying that the first converts belonged to one social class.

Quraysh organized commercial journeys, but historians disagree about the volume and geographical reach of Meccan trade; evidence does not establish that its clans controlled a vast trading empire. Qurʾān 106 emphasizes journeys, security and provision, while the institutional details come through later Sīrah and genealogy. A proclamation that challenged sacred objects and inherited status also threatened the loyalties through which clans maintained protection and authority. In Yathrib, meanwhile, the aftermath of conflicts such as Buʿāth provided a different political setting. The earliest instruction would begin in Muḥammad's household, where personal confidence and protection mattered before public authority existed.

## 08. Ḥirāʾ, Khadījah and the beginnings of a teaching community

**Matn.** Muḥammad ﷺ was raised by members of Banū Hāshim after the deaths of his parents. He became known for his trustworthiness and his care for relatives and those in need. His marriage to Khadījah gave her close knowledge of his character and conduct. In middle life he spent periods in seclusion at Ḥirāʾ. During one of these retreats, he received the first revelation. The angel commanded him to recite, and the revealed words named God as Creator and Teacher. He returned shaken and asked to be covered.

Khadījah sheltered and reassured him, recalling his care for relatives, truthful dealings, hospitality and help to those in need. She then took him to Waraqah ibn Nawfal, who recognized the account as resembling earlier prophetic revelation and warned that its bearer would face opposition.

Recitation, prayer, instruction and material assistance began within a small circle of people joined by different relations. ʿAlī and Zayd had lived closely with the household; Abū Bakr brought friendships beyond it. They heard the recitations, learned to pray and began to put the new teaching into practice in their dealings with others.

When the proclamation reached wider circles, these practices were tested amid the unequal protections of Quraysh. The first believers relied upon one another's instruction, friendship and protection as the opposition to the proclamation grew.

**Sharḥ.** The earliest revelation is narrated in the extended report of ʿĀʾishah in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3 ([text and Arabic](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3)). The report distinguishes true dreams before the first encounter, the love of seclusion, periods at Ḥirāʾ, the angel's demand to recite, the pressure he experienced, the revelation of the first verses of Sūrat al-ʿAlaq, his return and fear, Khadījah's reassurance and Waraqah's recognition. Its description should not be made into an entirely reconstructed diary of every day before revelation. It is a transmitted narrative with identifiable wording, while the precise dating of early verses and the sequence of first converts remain matters requiring comparison of other reports.

The initiative belongs to God. The Qurʾān speaks of teaching by the Pen and teaching the human being what he did not know (96:1–5). The received narrative does not describe the encounter as the reward mechanically produced by solitary practice, nor does it permit reducing revelation to an ordinary psychological accomplishment. At the same time, the bodily and emotional details are important: the subject of revelation is a real human being affected by what he receives and returning for shelter to someone he trusts. Prophetic election is not the annihilation of need for care and relationship.

Khadījah answered the Prophet's distress by recalling a pattern she knew from living with him: truthful speech, concern for relatives, hospitality and help to the vulnerable. In [al-Bukhārī 3](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3), that lived acquaintance differs from Waraqah's comparison with an earlier bearer of revelation and from the Prophet's own reception of revealed words. The three kinds of knowledge cooperate but cannot be exchanged for one another. The Akbarian description of prophetic comprehensiveness gives a later interpretation of their unity in one person, not an alternative account of the angelic encounter. The household's first act of shelter and recognition also became a condition for subsequent recitation, instruction and mutual trust: the earliest community formed through persons, not a pre-existing school or court.

Waraqah's response introduces remembered prophetic language. He relates the encounter to the bearer of revelation associated with Mūsā and anticipates hostility. The historian must keep Khadījah's practical certainty about the person, Waraqah's scriptural comparison, and the Prophet's reception of revealed speech as three different evidentiary or experiential operations. Their cooperation does not make them interchangeable proofs. Nor does the report warrant inventing unspoken mystical explanations given by Khadījah or Waraqah.

The Akbarian tradition supplies a deeper—but explicitly later—interpretation of this integrality. In Farghānī's Muntahā al-madārik, fourth foundation, especially the working translation of pp. 103 and 110–116, the Prophet's corporeal temperament and comprehensive heart correspond with the revealed Qurʾān's universal gathering of differentiated meanings. The Prophet's actions and character become the enacted expression of a reality articulated verbally in revelation. This is not the historical narrator's assertion of Farghānī's cosmology. It means that the reported human virtues should not be regarded as detachable modules whose sum would recreate the original prophetic subject.

The distinction helps clarify what is accessible and what is not. The account preserves speech, action and others' responses; it does not disclose with certainty the full inward relation to the divine Names at each instant. Even a person who could correctly list every kind of help Khadījah described would not thereby possess the actuality of the Prophet's entire character. In the language of Qūnawī's comprehensive standpoint, a particular description can be true under one aspect without exhausting the whole from which that aspect has been abstracted. This should never be made a pretext for discounting the accuracy or binding significance of an authentic transmitted word.

The household is therefore the first historical medium of this revelation's reception. It was a place of comfort, memory, conversation and instruction before it could be called a formal teaching institution. A home contains particular obligations, differences of age, attention and dependence. Each new member joined the early community through real persons rather than through an impersonal enrollment mechanism. The order and dates of particular early conversions differ among Sīrah reports and require separate source evaluation.

A listener learned the recitation by hearing and repeating; prayer became a recurrent practice; generosity was enacted through the transfer of actual goods; endurance required continuing trust when the prevailing order was hostile. This is the beginning of the transition from a transmitted command to acquired disposition, and from one person's action to patterned relations among companions. Repetition can cultivate malakāt, but the existence of an outward practice does not prove every practitioner achieved the intended inward perfection.

The later distinction between text, juristic judgment, spiritual companionship and realized knowledge illuminates these beginnings precisely because these offices did not yet exist as separated professions. A person might hear the Prophet's speech, see him pray, receive his correction and judge by his example within one relationship. After his death, subsequent generations would inherit different aspects through narrators, teachers, scholars and spiritual guides; the specialized forms could preserve genuine truth without equaling the comprehensive source.

A second caution concerns social power. A trustworthy household could help receive revelation, yet it could not guarantee the safety of every believer once the summons became public. Abū Ṭālib's clan standing and the vulnerability of the unprotected would affect the conditions of transmission. The move from a small circle to a public movement must be explained through these human and political relations, not by the proposition that genuine truth invariably produces immediate institutional success.

The first revelation was received by the Prophet, Khadījah reassured him through her knowledge of his character, and Waraqah related the event to earlier prophecy. Instruction then began among the people closest to him. Neither revelation nor spiritual realization is reducible to these relations. Yet the relations are genuine media through which the divine call becomes present within human life. That combination—not a simple opposition between supernatural and everyday experience—is the origin of the historical community traced through the following chapters.

## 09. The Meccan proclamation: worship, reckoning and new loyalties

**Matn.** The Qurʾānic proclamation summoned the people of Makkah to worship one God and to answer for every person's life after death. The warnings of the Last Day challenged the assumption that wealth or noble ancestry could protect a person who neglected the rights of others. The Qurʾān called for care of orphans, feeding of the poor, honest conduct and generosity alongside prayer and remembrance.

The response to this call differed according to each person's obligations and place in society. Some wealthy believers gave away possessions or freed slaves. Others received instruction while lacking resources and protection. Bilāl, members of the family of Yāsir and other vulnerable believers suffered persecution; some of their more powerful companions could help without making the entire community secure. Shared recitation, prayer and the restraint of anger formed a fellowship before it could enforce its norms through public authority.

Giving food, freeing an enslaved person or protecting an injured neighbour now belonged to the believers' religious obligations. Such acts also required them to resist greed, fear and the desire for approval. They learned their faith through what they did for one another as well as through what they recited. When some believers could no longer remain safe under Meccan protection, movement beyond its boundaries became necessary.

**Sharḥ.** Meccan passages concerning the Last Day and provision—among them 90:11–18, 92:5–21 and 107:1–7—bring the demands of worship into direct relation with property and the vulnerable. The soul's recognition that it answers to God can change how it perceives the rights of another person. This is not a bare assertion that piety causes altruism: relinquishing a possession, protecting a dependent or enduring hostility is a concrete operation under material and relational constraints.

The Meccan commands to worship God, care for dependants and remember the Last Day reached people whose resources and protections differed. Giving food and freeing a person from bondage are outward actions with material consequences; fear of God, love of justice and the refusal of ostentation concern the actor's inward formation. A legal interpreter may identify a binding duty, while a companion observes how repeated truthful conduct forms a person who can be trusted. Neither determination is false, but they answer different questions. The Qurʾānic summons was addressed to an integral human life before later disciplines distinguished legal obligation, moral character and social welfare as specialized studies.

Qurʾānic recitation shaped the daily practice of the first believers. Words remembered and spoken within a circle could reshape the judgments through which people approached trade, family and slavery. Their power was not limited to their informational content: they were recited in prayer, discussed, heard during fear and imitated by other people. The Qurʾān remains living revelation even where a specific spiritual master is absent, while companionship can help the believer understand and apply it to a particular condition.

Unequal vulnerability prevented one homogeneous experience of persecution. The lack of a powerful guardian made courage materially different from the courage of someone shielded by kin. The resulting social duty of those better placed to help others belongs to the formation of the community. Later legal and spiritual sciences would analyze such acts under separate headings; the first believers faced them together as concrete demands.

Meccan revelation joins resurrection and judgment to demands for generosity, truthfulness and care for the vulnerable. Passages such as Qurʾān 81:1–14; 90:11–18; 92:5–21 and 107:1–7 join eschatological accountability to care for the vulnerable and critique ostentatious worship. The Meccan proclamation did not prohibit wealth or commerce as such; it demanded truthfulness, generosity and accountability before God in their pursuit and use. Recurring oaths, warnings, questions and scenes of the Last Day gave believers memorable language for examining daily conduct.

Giving, emancipation, prayer and restraint affected both relations among households and the intentions and habits of the believers who practised them. A person asked to free a slave must relinquish property; a person asked to give food must resist retaining it; a person commanded to endure mockery must discipline anger. The practices do not prove that every participant attained the virtue intended, but they identify occasions in which character was tested and cultivated. When later authors discuss *malakah*, intention or purification, they supply analytical language for such processes. Later writers developed theories of habit and virtue around practices that early believers already undertook: giving, prayer, restraint and endurance.

When the proclamation made generosity and accountability obligatory, its claim reached the actual power relationships of a city. The master who owned a slave, the guardian handling an orphan's property and the rich merchant dispensing or withholding support faced newly articulated duties. The proclamation challenged exploitation, but Meccan masters and patrons continued to wield coercive power. The historical effect in Makkah was first the formation of a community that judged such conduct by different standards, often without the power to enforce them.

Later ethical writers such as al-Ghazālī describe the formation of stable dispositions through repeated action, examination of intention and correction of appetite. This offers a useful account of how practices may form character, but it is a later analytical reconstruction. Al-Ghazālī developed his technical account of moral psychology centuries after the earliest converts practised prayer, giving and patient endurance. Historically, the relevant evidence for persecution comes largely from transmitted reports about Bilāl, the family of Yāsir and other early believers; severity, chronology and the details of speeches need individual examination. Their different social protections made a common religious commitment costly in unequal ways. As persecution intensified and clan protection failed some believers, migration across the Red Sea offered an alternative source of safety.

## 10. Aksum: faith before political safety

**Matn.** In Makkah the protection of a person depended heavily upon clan and patronage. Religious allegiance did not remove these inequalities, and some believers faced dangers against which their neighbours could not effectively defend them. Groups crossed the Red Sea to Aksum, where the Christian king, known in the Arabic sources as the Negus, gave them refuge. They had left their homes and familiar protectors in order to practise their religion more safely.

Meccan envoys asked that the migrants be surrendered. In the transmitted account, Jaʿfar spoke of the religion that had called them away from injustice and invoked the Qurʾānic account of Maryam and ʿĪsā. The king listened and refused to hand the migrants over to Quraysh. The reports differ in the wording of the speeches and the sequence of the journeys, but they agree in presenting the king's protection as essential to the emigrants' safety.

Far from the Prophet ﷺ, the emigrants sustained their worship and fellowship under the Negus's protection. Makkah remained the site of the continuing proclamation, however. Their situation differed from that of believers who remained in Makkah, still exposed to Quraysh. A secure home for the community within Arabia had yet to be found.

**Sharḥ.** The migration to Aksum arose from the unequal protection available in Makkah. Some believers could rely on powerful relatives, while others faced punishment from people who controlled their livelihoods or households. The emigrants needed a ruler willing to refuse Quraysh's demand for their return. The Negus exercised that authority, and Muslim families gained a place where they could continue worship and maintain their fellowship.

**The hearing before the Negus.** Ibn Isḥāq's account, preserved by Ibn Hishām and later Sīrah writers, relates that Jaʿfar ibn Abī Ṭālib explained the moral and religious demands of the new message and recited verses concerning Maryam and ʿĪsā. The king refused the envoys' demand. These speeches survive through later transmission rather than contemporary court records; details and the sequence of the migrations differ among reports. Their common subject is the protection of emigrants who lacked the power to compel it.

**The Negus's later standing in Muslim reports.** The early encounter took place at a Christian royal court, but it is misleading to describe the Negus simply as an enduring non-Muslim protector. In [Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3877](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3877) the Prophet ﷺ announces the Negus's death, calls him a righteous man and his brother Aṣḥamah, and instructs the companions to pray for him. [Al-Bukhārī 1333](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:1333) records the funeral prayer. Muslim tradition therefore understands him as having accepted faith, even though the precise circumstances of his profession and its public visibility require separate examination. A Christian court's original protection of Muslims and the tradition concerning the king's later faith are distinct stages.

At Aksum the emigrants continued worship by relying on a ruler who possessed the political power to refuse their extradition. The Negus's original protection and the later Muslim testimony concerning his faith must be kept distinct: [al-Bukhārī 3877](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3877) records the Prophet's announcement of his death and the prayer commanded for him. Protection of vulnerable people, truthful religious witness and political jurisdiction intersected without becoming a single office held by the emigrants. The later Muhammadan inheritance includes the capacity to maintain worship when its adherents have no sovereign authority over their hosts. The circumstances of refuge, however, depend on the king's actual decision, distance from Makkah and the continued life of the migrant households.

A parallel Meccan example appears in [al-Bukhārī 3905](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3905): Abū Bakr received the protection of Ibn al-Daghinah, but his visible recitation attracted listeners and became a matter of dispute. Protection of a person's body and freedom to teach publicly need not coincide. The spatial separation of the Aksum migrants from the Prophet also required them to preserve recitation, prayer and mutual responsibility without his continual physical presence. Later spiritual interpretations of *hijrah* as departure from what separates a servant from God illuminate the experience of exile, but they do not replace the historical needs for security, shelter, family stability and permission to worship.

## 11. From loss of protection to an alliance at al-ʿAqabah

**Matn.** The deaths of Khadījah and Abū Ṭālib brought the Prophet ﷺ two grave personal losses and left him more exposed to opposition in Makkah. He lost the intimate companion whose home had first received the revelation and the relative whose standing had restrained opponents within Quraysh. Seeking a hearing beyond Makkah, he went to Ṭāʾif and encountered rejection. During pilgrimage seasons he continued addressing visitors, while people from Yathrib began receiving and carrying his recitation into their own relationships and rivalries.

The Qurʾān speaks of God's taking His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque. Reports of the Night Journey and Ascension relate the journey to Jerusalem, passage through the heavens, meetings with Ādam, ʿĪsā and Yaḥyā, Yūsuf, Idrīs, Hārūn, Mūsā and Ibrāhīm, and the giving of prayer. During the return Mūsā advised the Prophet concerning the burden of repeated worship for the community, and the prescribed daily prayers were reduced to five. The date and some details of the reports remain disputed.

The Prophet returned from the Night Journey to continue his mission among his people. The five daily prayers became a central obligation of Muslim worship. Later spiritual commentators reflected on the significance of his meeting the earlier prophets and returning to ordinary life after the ascent.

In Yathrib, Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr taught the new believers recitation and prayer. The first pledge at al-ʿAqabah concerned belief and conduct. In the later pledge, the people of Yathrib undertook to protect the Prophet and those who would join him, accepting the danger this might bring to their own households. Migrants began departing; the Prophet and Abū Bakr followed under concealment. In Yathrib the emigrants and their hosts would have to build a community capable of supporting worship, settling disputes and defending its members.

**Sharḥ.** The loss of Khadījah and Abū Ṭālib requires attention to what each relationship had actually supplied. Khadījah's home had joined affection, shelter, economic means and confidence in the Prophet's person; Abū Ṭālib's protection operated through clan standing, even while he differed from the religious proclamation. The removal of these supports altered material risks without reducing the Prophet's mission to the power of any one household or protector. Traditions of the Ṭāʾif journey preserve differing details, including the remembered rejection and later spiritual interpretations of endurance; a critical account separates its core from subsequent elaborations.

The Night Journey is explicitly stated in Qurʾān 17:1. The higher visionary passages in 53:1–18 raise additional exegetical questions about the referent of the sighting, including the relation between the vision of Gabriel and the vision of God. The sources cannot simply be flattened into one account. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 162a ([Arabic and translation](https://sunnah.com/muslim:162a)) gives a connected narrative of al-Burāq, Jerusalem, the heavenly gates, successive greetings from earlier prophets, the Lote Tree, the initial prescription of fifty prayers and the reduction of their number. Bukhārī 349 supplies a related form with differences of arrangement and emphasis. Neither source securely supplies a date connecting the event causally with the specific order of the later pledges.

The seven encounters are ordered in Muslim 162a: Ādam in the first heaven; ʿĪsā and Yaḥyā in the second; Yūsuf in the third; Idrīs in the fourth; Hārūn in the fifth; Mūsā in the sixth; and Ibrāhīm in the seventh. Each offers welcome and recognition. The report does not state that a prophet appearing at a given level has no rank or reality beyond that level. Al-Qūnawī, al-Nuṣūṣ Text Nineteen (Gnamma QUN-NU-019-G, pp. 259–260), treats these appearances as correspondences with a particular condition or attribute, rather than an exhaustive permanent ranking of the prophetic realities. His cosmological analysis is later interpretation, not a line of the canonical hadith itself.

A further distinction concerns vision. Muslim 176a transmits Ibn ʿAbbās's statement that the Prophet saw with his heart; Muslim 177a transmits ʿĀʾishah's rejection of the claim of ordinary ocular vision of God and her identification of the Qurʾānic sighting with Gabriel. The difference cannot responsibly be suppressed in the service of a smooth esoteric story. Later Akbarian accounts of inward witnessing and spiritual sight need to identify precisely what they mean and whether their claims concern the same object and mode of vision as these early reports.

The Prophet's ascent is, in the Akbarian tradition, more than a tour of cosmic geography. Ibn ʿArabī interprets it through the differentiated divine Names, forms of prophetic wisdom and degrees of inner realization. The comparison of his Futūḥāt chapter 367, Kitāb al-Isrāʾ and Risālat al-Anwār in James Morris's study (JAOS 1987–1988; [accessible prepublication translation](https://ibnarabisociety.org/wp-content/uploads/PDFs/Morris_The-spiritual-ascension.pdf)) repeatedly emphasizes the movement of **return**. The realized servant does not necessarily become publicly marked by permanent ecstatic difference; the highest orientation toward God can reappear as appropriate presence to created persons and duties.

Qūnawī's analysis of the Muhammadan station as comprehensive, and Ibn ʿArabī's maqām lā maqām, must retain their ontological and experiential depth. They do not mean simply that the Prophet was good at adapting to circumstances. Distinct prophetic wisdoms are true under their respective determinations, and the Muhammadan station, in this tradition, is not confined to one of them. This also does not authorize an editor to invent the Prophet's inward experience of a specific earthly event.

The exchange with Mūsā concerning daily prayers is unusually revealing. The canonical report relates that Mūsā urges the Prophet to ask for relief because the community will struggle with the original number. Ibn ʿArabī gives the counsel a particular spiritual interpretation: in the encounter with Mūsā in Futūḥāt 367, the knowledge at issue is *dhawq* or direct experience, a person's actual familiarity with the demands of a community. Mūsā's prophetic experience and Muḥammad's unique spiritual commission are not opposed; the dialogue concerns the correct relation of worship to real human capacity.

The Prophet's return from the Miʿrāj is indispensable to its explanatory force. The report of [Muslim 162a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:162a) relates the meeting with Mūsā, his counsel concerning the community's capacity, and the prescription of five daily prayers. In Ibn ʿArabī's later interpretation of spiritual ascent, *return* is not simply descent into an inferior life: worship resumes among real bodies, times, households and neighbours. A correct legal account establishes the prayer's duties, but does not exhaust the spiritual reality of ascension, nor does a mystical reading abolish its established ritual form. The subsequent work of teaching prayer, welcoming migrants and undertaking the pledges at al-ʿAqabah arose through separate historical actions rather than mechanically from the heavens.

Al-Sulamī's pre-Akbarian Laṭāʾif al-Miʿrāj, edited and translated by Frederick Colby, provides an earlier Sufi register of spiritual etiquette, awe, light, ineffability, love, vision, the limits of speech, and the benefit of prayer. The printed contents name specific attributed sayings on the propriety of witnessing, the secret between lovers, Moses and Muḥammad, and the purpose of the ascent. The full 56-text Arabic and translation corpus has not been directly collated for this edition; the sharḥ cannot pretend to quote those items in detail or to assign their contents solely on the basis of the contents page.

Martin Lings's The Book of Certainty, chapter II, printed pp. 24–25, treats the seven heavenly appearances as distinct characteristics of prophetic realities without claiming that those prophets' complete degrees are restricted to one sphere. His discussion of the Muhammadan Light supplies a Traditionalist contemplative interpretation. It is not a second independent early witness to the Prophet's narrated journey.

The return to Makkah also demands attention to the earthly life. The Prophet continues seeking protection, giving instruction and hearing pledges. The events involving Yathrib did not spring mechanically from the ascent: they involved households with old conflicts, visitors who returned home, Muṣʿab's teaching and a binding undertaking to defend the Prophet. These are independent historical causes and relations. The first pledge addressed worship and conduct; the second altered the hosts' military and political obligations.

The earlier migration to Aksum had shown that believers could worship under a ruler who did not share all their theological convictions. The pledges from Yathrib provided a different arrangement: the community itself would take responsibility for the Prophet's bodily safety and for the settlement of new members. Instruction came before the promise of collective defence; shared practice made common obligations recognizable without creating one homogeneous spiritual state in everyone who participated.

The Night Journey and the events surrounding the pledges at al-ʿAqabah belong to different kinds of evidence. The first concerns the Prophet's extraordinary journey and the obligation of prayer; the second concerns the people who offered him protection in Yathrib. The received narrative joins a servant's unique elevation toward God to a returned obligation of worship in time, while the migration narrative joins trustworthy individuals to a developing public community. Together they make intelligible the two directions of the Sīrah: **ascent toward the source and a more perfect carrying of human responsibilities on return**. The concrete historical transmission of this pattern remains a task of teachers, companions, households and later differentiated institutions, never a reproduction of law-giving prophethood.

# Book III — Madinah: how a religious fellowship became a public community

## 12. The Hijrah: neighbours, newcomers and a shared order

**Matn.** The emigrants arrived in Yathrib with their religious fellowship but without much of the property, livelihood and kin protection they had possessed in Makkah. Their hosts cultivated orchards, maintained households and lived among groups with separate alliances and remembered conflicts. The Prophet ﷺ established a mosque where prayer, learning and common consultation could recur. The Anṣār shared food and lodging with the newcomers, many of whom had lost their possessions in Makkah. This support relieved immediate hardship, while the emigrants sought ways to rebuild their livelihoods.

The town also needed agreements for common defence and the settlement of disputes. Agreements associated with the Prophet coordinated existing kin obligations for compensation, ransom, mutual support and defence. Named groups retained responsibilities within their own communities while undertaking wider commitments of peace and protection. Jewish groups named in the surviving agreements retained their religious distinctions while undertaking specified obligations of peace and security. Different recensions preserve different names and clauses; no single text can be assumed to bind every resident of Yathrib.

The Prophet ﷺ led prayer, taught the Qurʾān and settled disputes while helping different groups assume responsibilities toward one another. Personal generosity remained important, but agreed obligations also protected people who could not depend upon voluntary help alone. Questions of marriage, debts, inheritance and trade continued to arise as these arrangements took shape among the town's families and communities.

**Sharḥ.** In Madinah, support for individual emigrants gradually became part of a more durable arrangement of shared responsibilities. A migrant needed food and lodging; the Anṣārī who shared them also had legitimate claims belonging to his own household. Qurʾān 59:8–9 praises the hosts who preferred others despite need, but this does not mean scarcity ceased or that every family could sustain indefinite open-ended generosity. The mosque, a recurring place of worship and counsel, provided one setting for the gradual formation of recognizably shared practices.

The Medinan *kitāb*, preserved in recensions associated with Ibn Isḥāq/Ibn Hishām and Abū ʿUbayd, speaks of a single community while retaining responsibilities for blood compensation and ransom within named groups. A religious association can coordinate existing groups without dissolving all their rights, kinship or religious distinctions. The modern term *constitution* is useful only if it does not smuggle in a theory of individual equal citizenship or undivided national sovereignty absent from the transmitted text. Studies by Michael Lecker and other scholars disagree about compositional layers and participants; that dispute belongs in the dossier, not in an invented seamless narrative.

The Prophet's guidance in Madinah required more than generosity in the moment and more than a document declaring unity. Migrants had to eat and work; hosts retained obligations to their own dependants; different groups bore responsibilities concerning ransom, blood compensation and common security. The transmitted Medinan *kitāb* places these relations into public terms without making every resident a member of one modern category of citizenship. A person who recognizes another's need and a community that reliably settles an enforceable claim are accomplishing related forms of justice. The Akbarian idea of comprehensive human action can illuminate their coordination, but the actual agreement's clauses and participants must be established through textual comparison. Public ʿumrān arises through people carrying out specified obligations, not a document acting on its own.

In the Akbarian reading, the Muhammadan integral vocation relates differentiated goods without abolishing their particularity. The historical evidence does not tell us that a document originated from a mystical doctrine of comprehensiveness. The analytical value of the doctrine is to resist a false choice between communal unity and legitimate subgroup distinctions. It explains the norm toward which acts of worship and public responsibility can be ordered while separately describing material and political mechanisms. The formation of the people, not simply the drafting of an agreement, is the work through which an association becomes durable.

The key phrase in the Medinan document is the designation of a single community, *umma wāḥida*, alongside clauses retaining the emigrants and several groups of Yathrib within their existing arrangements of blood compensation and captive ransom. Ibn Hishām and Abū ʿUbayd preserve versions of the Medinan agreements; Michael Lecker compares their clauses, parties and transmission in *The Constitution of Medina* (2004, with later revisions). Saïd Amir Arjomand and other scholars argue over its composition and the distribution of its parts. The surviving recensions vary in clause sequence and participants, leaving the dates of individual provisions uncertain. The term “constitution” is convenient but potentially misleading: the transmitted text is a set of concrete obligations, not a modern theory of equal citizenship or centralized sovereignty. Participating groups assumed specific responsibilities for blood compensation, ransom of captives and contributions to common defence.

These provisions turned commitments of mutual aid into specified duties between groups that already possessed their own obligations. A believer can be generous toward a newcomer today but not tomorrow; an agreed rule about compensation and mutual aid makes some duties predictable and adjudicable. Neither law nor generosity replaces the other. Where kin groups retained responsibility for ransom and blood money, the agreement did not eliminate their internal authority; it directed their activity into a wider protected association. The Prophet was teaching worship while resolving issues that required enforceable cooperation. The Medinan community linked shared worship to concrete public duties of compensation, ransom, defence and adjudication.

Lecker's 2004 study and substantially updated 2024 edition warn against treating the transmitted *kitāb* as a modern constitution or assuming that every Jewish group in Madinah signed it. In the published text the emigrants and several Yathrib groups continue their customary blood-money and ransom responsibilities even as a wider *umma* is asserted. This combines continuity of local kin obligations with wider public coordination. The agreements retained named clan duties while placing them within wider obligations of mutual protection.

The social problem was acute. An emigrant had surrendered the protection and resources of his old household; an Anṣārī who helped him still had children, farms and obligations to relatives. Material generosity was therefore a test of allegiance, while the mosque made collective worship and teaching a regular practice. Qurʾān 59:8–9 describes the deprived emigrants and the hosts who preferred them despite their own need. The Qurʾān praises mutual assistance, while the Medinan agreements assign particular responsibilities to participating clans and allied groups. The Prophet's leadership united teaching with adjudication, but its historical forms remained responsive to local conditions. Public judgment increasingly concerned family obligations, property and markets, matters no less important to the community's development than warfare. The inner and outer are linked here in a demonstrable way: a disposition toward generosity mattered because real people needed food and shelter, while public obligations made mutual assistance more reliable than generosity alone.

The document's wording imposes a useful restraint on our terminology. A phrase designating a single community stands next to recurrent clauses retaining named clans' earlier compensation obligations. The legal relationship is one of enlarged coordination, not a documented abolition of kin groups. Equally, the Jewish clauses cannot be read as proof that every Jewish group of the oasis had accepted precisely the same terms. Lecker's study argues for narrower participation and separately negotiated arrangements; the recensions require collation before any one clause can be dated with certainty. Existing clans retained their identities but now shared responsibilities for defence, compensation and judgments that crossed earlier boundaries. [Ibn Hishām's text](https://islamweb.net/ar/library/content/58/584/); [Lecker's study](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-constitution-of-medina-muhammads-first-legal-document-2/).

## 13. Worship takes shape in the routines of a town

**Matn.** In Madinah, worship became part of the daily routines of households and neighbourhoods. Prayer gathered people who also worked, cared for children, traded, lost family members and required help. Fasting trained restraint while the poor still needed provision; the direction of prayer joined the community to the sanctuary that many emigrants had been forced to leave. Qurʾānic guidance addressed debt, marriage, the protection of dependants and the resolution of disputes at different moments as situations arose.

The Prophet ﷺ did not treat ordinary vulnerability as a distraction from God. When a child cried during congregational prayer, he shortened a recitation he had intended to prolong out of concern for the mother. At home he participated in household work and went out to pray when its time arrived. A granddaughter was carried during prayer, set down when he prostrated and lifted when he stood. To a young boy he spoke gently about a small bird. His actions occurred among real people whose different needs did not disappear because the community was engaged in worship.

Other encounters concerned the rights and responsibilities of public life. When a man urinated in the mosque, the Prophet restrained those who would interrupt him and ordered the place washed; the man's mistaken action and the bystanders' anger were both brought under correction. In the market he discovered wet grain concealed beneath the visible surface and demanded openness to buyers. A woman's question about ritual purity received additional practical explanation through ʿĀʾishah. In the cases of Māʿiz and the woman of Ghāmid, the reports describe confessions of adultery, the Prophet's questioning or initial refusal to proceed, and the eventual punishment. The woman's pregnancy and her child's need for care affected when the sentence could be carried out. Other narrations record how he corrected those who spoke with contempt about a person who had repented.

The people who accompanied the Prophet learned from more than his formal judgments. They saw how he addressed children, received questions, corrected mistakes, cared for his family and exercised public responsibility. These experiences would become part of the Sunnah transmitted to later generations.

**Sharḥ.** **The different goods of one human life.** The Qurʾānic texts on prayer direction (2:142–150), fasting (2:183–187), deferred debts (2:282), inheritance (4:7–12) and marital arbitration (4:35) address distinct acts and obligations. The people receiving them were not professional worshippers separate from professional household members and traders. They acted as complete persons under different relations. This does not mean every verse was revealed at the same time or every subsequent school rule was already set out in a Medinan manual; the sources establish varied occasions and subsequent work of interpretation.

**Prayer and the mother with the crying child.** Reports through Abū Qatādah and Anas, recorded at Bukhārī 707–710 ([compare the sequence](https://sunnah.com/bukhari/10), especially [709](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:709)), preserve the Prophet's declaration that he sometimes intended a longer prayer but shortened it when hearing a child's cry and knowing the distress of its mother. The public act remained prayer and retained its divinely directed end; the mother's circumstance affected a legitimate decision about the prayer's length. An imam's duty to worship rightly and concern for the real conditions of participants were neither interchangeable nor incompatible.

The Prophet's decision concerned a particular child and mother, not an abstract instruction to exercise kindness. A mother does not need to submit a formal petition for her vulnerability to be noticed, and the child does not cease to have bodily needs because an adult congregation has begun worship. Later fiqh asks about the legal status and limits of shortening prayer; ethical teaching considers care and perception; spiritual interpretation may see the act as responsive to the proper relation of divine and creaturely rights. These are real but partial accounts of a single action. No source gives a verbatim record of a separate inner mystical disclosure on that occasion.

**Household service and prayer.** When al-Aswad asks ʿĀʾishah what the Prophet did at home, she recalls his service to the household and his departure to prayer when its time came ([Bukhārī 676](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:676)). That relation corrects the idea that household labour must be spiritually insignificant because it is not a formal rite. The temporal sequence also avoids its inverse: domestic service does not abolish prayer. The Prophet acts within the differentiated demands of one life.

**The child carried in worship.** Abū Qatādah reports the Prophet carrying his granddaughter Umāmah, putting her down for prostration and lifting her again on standing ([Bukhārī 516](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:516)). Juristic questions about movement in prayer are properly narrower than the total meaning of the encounter; the same action can communicate practical attention to a dependent child without establishing unlimited permission to alter ritual form. The full bodily pattern matters: his form of worship and his care for the child coexist in timed gestures.

**The small bird, the kissed child and gentle authority.** In [Bukhārī 6129](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:6129), Anas remembers the Prophet speaking affectionately to his young brother Abū ʿUmayr about his bird. In [Bukhārī 5997](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:5997), the Prophet kisses al-Ḥasan and corrects al-Aqraʿ's expression of distance from his children. These cases disclose how attention, affection and recognition work within relationships shaped by unequal age and authority. The first does not establish a hidden, later emotional biography for the boy; the second does not impose one identical visible gesture in every culture. Both show that the human being is addressed as a particular person, not a statistical unit in a large social order.

**The dependent servant.** Anas reports years of service in which the Prophet refrained from impatient reproach toward him ([Muslim 2309a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:2309a)). The retrospective character of the memory matters: a sustained relation discloses a durable style of exercising authority rather than a single momentary courtesy. Whether this establishes one specific later legal protection for servants is a separate juristic and historical problem. It is more immediately evidence for a quality of close daily companionship.

**Correction without abandonment of sacred order.** Bukhārī 219–220 ([text](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:220)) recounts a Bedouin urinating within the mosque and the Prophet stopping an angry interruption, then ordering water poured over the affected place. The impurity remains wrong, and purification remains required. The person who acted in ignorance and the companions angered by the act both receive instruction. The action corrects a physical condition, a form of public behaviour and the manner in which people treat an outsider. It is not evidence that sanctity or discipline are dispensable.

**Truthfulness in exchange.** Muslim 102 ([text](https://sunnah.com/muslim:102)) reports the Prophet finding moisture hidden beneath grain offered for sale and asking why buyers could not see the defect. The marketplace is a relation between an informed seller and an otherwise less informed buyer; concealment frustrates the buyer's judgment. Legal duties of disclosure, the individual's disposition of honesty and a market's public reliability are connected without being identical. A later *ḥisba* institution needs its own documents and history; it cannot be declared historically created by this one report.

**Women's access to comprehensible instruction.** In [Bukhārī 101](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:101), women seek a distinct time for teaching and the Prophet responds; Bukhārī 314–315 ([text](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:314)) describes ʿĀʾishah clarifying ritual purity when another woman needs practical explanation. The student requires intelligible direction, while a teacher's discretion and a second teacher's detailed knowledge cooperate. Such reports show women as active bearers of religious understanding, not merely the recipients of doctrines transmitted by others. They do not establish one uniform educational institution already existing in every later Muslim region.

**Confession and judicial restraint.** Muslim 1695a ([text](https://sunnah.com/muslim:1695a)) preserves the distinct confessions of Māʿiz and the woman of Ghāmid. With Māʿiz, the Prophet questions the admission and his condition in the narrated sequence; with the woman he initially directs repentance and later defers the public penalty in recognition of pregnancy and an infant's need for nursing. The report describes death by stoning, which cannot be elided. Muslim 1696a ([text](https://sunnah.com/muslim:1696a)) transmits a separate account of a woman of Juhayna, the instruction to her guardian to treat her well, the penalty, and a funeral prayer followed by recognition of her repentance. The sources must not silently combine these into a single verbatim conversation, or imply that public confession and penal punishment are prerequisites for the forgiveness of every sin.

The cases of Māʿiz and the Ghāmidī woman in [Muslim 1695a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:1695) cannot be reduced either to a legal formula or to a sentimental account of forgiveness. The report includes repeated approaches, refusal or delay, the establishment of a confession and eventual severe punishment; in the woman's case, pregnancy and care for the infant affect the timing and arrangements. The legal question concerns proof, jurisdiction and punishment; the moral question includes repentance, the rights of a dependent child and the speech of witnesses. The Prophet's decisions address these real persons under different claims, while the source does not grant an external observer exhaustive access to their inner stations. A jurist's correct ruling preserves one indispensable aspect of guidance without reproducing the comprehensive prophetic act.

**Why the Akbarian model helps, and where it stops.** In the Akbarian teaching, the *maqām lā maqām* concerns realization not restricted to one particular station or Name, and the Perfect Human is a comprehensive *barzakh* rather than a ledger of excellent traits. These doctrines may elucidate the proper ordering of differentiated human goods, but do not belong to the direct wording of Bukhārī's or Muslim's narrators. The Prophet's complete historical person is not reconstructed by adding together all of the above disciplinary analyses. What the sources give is the plurality of actual, reliable acts in which divine servanthood and human relationship meet.

**From encounters to civilization.** The same person who is formed by household care can later become teacher, parent, seller, judge or public official. Repetition, correction and imitation can produce *malakāt*; reliable relations can become norms and institutions. But the movement is not one inevitable causal chain. One cannot claim that the institution of market inspection originated from the wet-grain incident, that a later educational college originated from the boy's bird, or that all legal punishments follow one undifferentiated rule of mercy. The historical Sīrah records the founding relationships; later chapters trace the specific persons, texts, contracts and organizations that developed them.

The continuity is not a lossless replication of the Prophet's person. His commission integrates revelation, teaching, judgment and spiritual realization in an unrepeatable way. The Qurʾān, narrated Sunnah, jurisprudence, ethical formation and spiritual companionship remain genuine but selectively mediated access to guidance. The everyday human encounters are therefore not decorations around the political Sīrah. They are primary evidence for the manner in which the Muhammadan inheritance makes human life intelligible as a field of worship, knowledge, obligation and care.

## 14. Badr: conflict tests allegiance and public judgment

**Matn.** The migration to Madinah had not removed conflict with Quraysh. The emigrants had lost property and clan security, while trading parties and alliances continued to bind Makkah to routes near the new community. In the second year after the Hijrah, conflict over a caravan led to the battle at Badr. A smaller Muslim force defeated an army sent from Makkah, killed prominent opponents and took prisoners. The victory strengthened Madinah's position, but brought difficult questions about the treatment of prisoners and the distribution of goods taken in battle.

The Muslims had fought together under the Prophet's command, but they did not agree over every claim to the spoils. Revelation addressed their disputes, their conduct in battle and the treatment of captives. Victory had not removed differences of interest among men united in faith.

Decisions about prisoners and property affected families on both sides and the future relations between Makkah and Madinah. Quraysh prepared to retaliate, and the following year the Muslims faced a new battle at Uḥud.

**Sharḥ.** Victory at Badr brought new responsibilities for the treatment of captives and the distribution of property. Qurʾān 8 treats spoils, fear, divine assistance and the treatment of captives as matters on which participants could require correction. When people attribute a success to their own superiority, the resulting claims to wealth or public rank can sever the relation of common action to its higher purpose. The divine aid asserted in the Qurʾān and the material facts of planning, numbers and tactical decisions are not rival explanatory causes in the same register.

At Badr, defeat or victory did not remove the need to judge the handling of captives and the distribution of goods. The people present had experienced fear, combat and loss; they were now responsible for persons who could not defend themselves and property over which competing claims arose. Qurʾān 8 addresses these questions within worshipful dependence upon God. Later jurists can analyze captives and spoils; moral formation concerns the passions that make victory appear to authorize unlimited personal entitlement; political history traces how such decisions were enforced. A later Akbarian comparison with comprehensive discernment is meaningful only after preserving these concrete differences. No one successful military decision can be taken as an exhaustive disclosure of the Prophet's spiritual station.

The personal dispositions of the fighters and the public consequences of their acts form one historical sequence. A fighter's resolve helps a unit maintain position; a willingness to accept a judgment helps prevent distributional conflict; mutual confidence can make repeated coordinated action possible. Ibn Khaldūn's later *ʿaṣabiyya* describes one form of social cohesion, but it cannot substitute for the community's own articulated religious motivations. Neither religious allegiance nor shared ancestry alone explains all the diverse acts at Badr.

The source register must distinguish the Qurʾānic account from the maghāzī narratives of troop numbers, individual speeches and specific encounters. The ethical interpretation cannot claim to know each person's inward station or use victory as proof of faultlessness. Uḥud would soon expose how different human impulses could arise under the very same outward allegiance.

The Qurʾān discusses Badr in passages including 3:123–127 and Sūrat al-Anfāl, which addresses fear, victory, captives, spoils and cohesion. Detailed troop movements, numbers and dialogues belong mainly to the later maghāzī tradition; they require comparison of versions, especially Ibn Isḥāq's material and relevant hadith. A battle's broad occurrence does not license treating each remembered speech as independently verified. Badr's importance as an institution-forming event is clearer when we ask who now bore responsibilities that had previously been limited to kin groups: commanders coordinated armed men from several backgrounds; the community faced captives and property disputes; alliances elsewhere in Arabia were affected by the perceived result.

Captivity offers a sharp case of institutional change. Before a public community could hold prisoners, conflicts of capture or ransom might be managed primarily through family or tribal retaliation. After Badr, the Prophet and the believers confronted prisoners from powerful Meccan households, whose treatment affected diplomacy and future exchange. Spoils raised related disputes among fighters who had taken different risks. These were not abstract problems added by later jurists: the community was learning to assign claims when common military action produced consequences beyond any one family's control. Later law systematized matters already encountered here, while the Qurʾānic address contested pride and entitlement arising from victory.

In Qurʾān 8 the aftermath of Badr is not celebrated merely as successful fighting. Disputes over spoils and the treatment of captives bring human desires into the immediate interpretation of events. That permits one to connect personal formation to public judgment without claiming that believers were uniformly free of rivalry. The Qurʾānic passages address fear, victory, captives and spoils; later maghāzī reports supply fuller speeches and troop counts whose transmission differs.

Victory confronted the fighters with decisions about spoils and captives, testing their restraint, loyalties and judgment. The participants faced fear, conflicting expectations and the temptation to read success as personal entitlement. Qurʾān 8 addresses disputes over spoils and requires obedience and collective responsibility after victory. Ibn Khaldūn's later analysis of solidarity offers one angle on how a threatened group could act collectively; revelation, shared worship and the Prophet's authority provide the actors' own grounds of commitment. Social solidarity can explain mobilization without exhausting religious conviction. Uḥud will demonstrate that collective confidence does not remove the need for disciplined judgment.

## 15. Uḥud and the Trench: discipline, siege and divided loyalties

**Matn.** Quraysh returned toward Madinah seeking revenge, and near Uḥud some Muslim archers left the positions assigned to them after a moment of apparent advantage. The resulting attack contributed to severe losses; the Prophet ﷺ was wounded and Ḥamzah was killed. The battle showed the danger of abandoning an assigned position, even when victory seemed near. The survivors returned to Madinah grieving their losses.

A larger coalition later advanced toward Madinah. Defenders dug a trench, maintained watches and endured prolonged fear, hunger and uncertainty. Collective labour depended upon people who supplied food, used tools, guarded approaches and remained at assigned tasks. The defenders had to maintain this effort over many days rather than during a single battle. Alliances and wartime agreements also came under pressure. After the siege, reports describe judgment against Banū Qurayẓah and the execution and captivity of members of that community. The accounts differ in their details, especially in the reported numbers and speeches.

Both campaigns placed great demands upon the people of Madinah. Soldiers had to follow commands, families supplied and cared for the defenders, and the Prophet faced serious questions concerning alliances and public security. The continuing threat from Quraysh would eventually lead to negotiations at al-Ḥudaybiyyah.

**Sharḥ.** The two campaigns tested different forms of endurance and obedience. Qurʾān 3:121–175 addresses Uḥud, with explicit attention to disobedience, fear and consequences; Qurʾān 33:9–27 addresses the siege and its aftermath. The actual tasks differed: archers held a limited tactical position, while trench workers and their dependants bore extended labour and deprivation. Their acts required practical understanding, obedience and endurance under different constraints.

At Uḥud, an assigned group of archers left a position; at the Trench, men and households sustained digging, watches and provisions over many days. The relevant virtues were enacted through precise circumstances: keeping an assigned station, carrying food, using tools, obeying legitimate direction and maintaining mutual confidence. Their consequences were military as well as personal. Qurʾān 3 and 33 address failure and endurance through the events themselves. The Akbarian return to outward action does not turn siege work into a symbol with no material causes. Even a true spiritual resolve cannot replace a correctly sited trench or adequate food. Later public offices and military methods inherited limited aspects of disciplined collective action without becoming the Prophet's comprehensive authority.

At the relational level, the fatigue of one family, trust in a commander and the readiness of allies all affect what a community can actually do. The collective is not an independent entity that acts without persons. Nonetheless, a trusted position or agreement can carry obligations greater than an individual's immediate desire. The wider social result arises from coordinated repeated conduct, including when no one person's virtue could produce an outcome alone.

Banū Qurayẓah requires independent critical handling. The sources differ concerning reported breach, particulars of the decision and numbers of those killed; no responsible spiritual reading may treat their suffering as decorative proof of providential harmony. The historicity and legal significance of each claim must be assessed on its own evidence. The moral requirement that acts be judged in relation to real persons and actual rights is strongest precisely where competing communal memories create the temptation to substitute a totalizing story for source criticism.

The eventual movement toward negotiation with Quraysh is a later political development with separate proximate causes. Neither Uḥud nor the siege simply “produced” the treaty as a necessary spiritual stage. They changed the actors' material constraints, alliance structures and expectations, which made different choices available.

Qurʾān 3:121–175 engages the events around Uḥud, and 33:9–27 addresses the Trench and its aftermath. These texts do not supply every detail or number in later historical accounts. Ibn Isḥāq, al-Wāqidī and later writers preserve differing materials whose relation needs criticism. The transmitted judgment of Banū Qurayẓah appears in Sunni historical and legal accounts; reports differ over particular speeches and numbers. It is unnecessary to transform this book into a monograph on the controversy, but equally unsound to present every later number as established observation.

At Uḥud some archers abandoned their positions; at the Trench the defenders maintained collective labour and vigilance during a prolonged siege. Uḥud tested whether fighters under immediate pressure would remain at assigned positions; the Trench demanded the patience of households over a sustained threat, with tasks that brought no instant victory or visible booty. Failure could arise through individual departure, exhausted supplies or divided allies. The same outward obedience therefore took different forms in battle and siege. The siege demanded vigilance, patience and restraint from its defenders; later spiritual authors examined those qualities in settings of moral discipline.

At Uḥud some archers abandoned their tactical assignment after an apparent advantage; at the Trench the defenders sustained collective labour and defence under scarcity and fear. The Banū Qurayẓah reports raise a third, distinct problem of alleged wartime breach and post-siege judgment. The later narratives of Banū Qurayẓah differ in reported numbers and speeches; those details have different levels of evidentiary support.

The two campaigns illustrate distinct social pressures. Uḥud exposes limits of discipline inside the fighting force; the Trench demanded coordination among households over time, with food, digging, sentries and readiness depending upon more than battlefield bravery. Military loyalty required repeated obedience to assigned positions, cooperation over supplies and dependable performance under danger. Later spiritual authors examined vigilance and intention as moral disciplines; their arguments do not establish why particular archers abandoned their positions at Uḥud. The changing regional balance after the siege created conditions in which negotiation became possible. The next decisive encounter with Quraysh ended in negotiation and a treaty at al-Ḥudaybiyyah.

## 16. Al-Ḥudaybiyyah: an agreement that changed the terms of conflict

**Matn.** In the sixth year after the Hijrah, the Prophet ﷺ led companions toward Makkah intending pilgrimage. Quraysh barred their entry, and both parties negotiated. The agreement established a truce and postponed the pilgrimage until the following year. Its provisions concerning the return of certain people to Makkah deeply troubled Companions who had themselves suffered persecution.

Abū Jandal arrived during the negotiations in chains, making the cost of one clause immediate. ʿUmar asked the Prophet how a seemingly humiliating agreement accorded with the truth of the mission and the expectation of entering the sanctuary. He explained that the promise of entering the sanctuary did not mean they would enter on that journey.

When the agreement had been concluded, the Prophet instructed the pilgrims to complete the sacrifice and shaving that would end their state of consecration. They hesitated. He withdrew and told Umm Salamah what had occurred; she advised that he perform the acts without further speech. He followed her counsel. The companions then acted as they saw him act. His example helped the pilgrims carry out the instruction they had struggled to accept.

The truce made journeys and contact between Makkah and Madinah less dangerous for a time. Both sides also accepted obligations toward tribes that joined their respective alliances. The treaty did not remove immediate suffering or make every clause equally advantageous. Its later breach among allies supplied an occasion for the Prophet's return to Makkah. The Companions remembered both the difficult terms of the agreement and the Prophet's conduct as they struggled to follow his decision.

**Sharḥ.** **The complete event before the abstraction.** The most extensive connected source is the narration of al-Miswar ibn Makhramah and Marwān in [Bukhārī 2731–2732](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2731), which includes blocked travel, negotiation, different emissaries, treaty wording, Abū Jandal's appearance, ʿUmar's questions and Umm Salamah's counsel. The report is substantial but is not a neutral stenographic recording of every word exchanged; its transmission and relation to Ibn Isḥāq/Ibn Hishām and shorter hadith versions need comparison. Qurʾān 48:1–29 describes the event within a divine promise of opening and commends the pledge under the tree (48:18); *fatḥ* is not exhausted by an immediately visible military conquest.

**Pilgrimage and public conditions.** The intention of ʿumrah had real religious force; the pilgrims' inability to enter the sanctuary was neither imaginary nor automatically cancelled by a promise of later access. At the same time Quraysh possessed its own military and alliance arrangements, and a settlement had to be made by parties with enforceable capacities. The treaty's terms were not simply private expressions of patience. They made a public arrangement that could change the future conduct of many who were not present in the original tent.

**Abū Jandal's appearance.** A person arrives in chains while a return clause is being settled. Abū Jandal's arrival made the return clause immediately painful, because it would affect a man who had suffered persecution. Legal obligations of agreement, an individual's urgent vulnerability and the collective pursuit of reduced war cannot be treated as if they supplied one immediately self-evident outward answer. The transmitted sequence must remain intact even if later historical developments make the agreement appear successful. It would be unethical to infer that suffering was unreal or merely a convenient instrument of a higher purpose.

**The wording problem.** The return clause's scope differs among connected reports and later interpretations, with important questions about men, women, affiliation and timing. A fully decisive historical account requires the precise Arabic wording and collation with related hadith and Sīrah narratives. This sharḥ can describe the dilemma, but a juristic conclusion about the later treaty clauses cannot be determined from a generic summary such as “all fugitives must be returned.” The actual contractual content is a separate object of knowledge.

**ʿUmar's question.** His challenge is part of the report. It exposes a gap between the certainty that the Prophet's mission is true and a follower's expectation that the mission must produce immediate access to the sanctuary. The Prophet distinguishes between a promise that a pilgrimage will occur and the assumption that it must occur during this attempt. The moral formation is not a simple ban on asking questions. The correction concerns how confidence in divine promise is related to limits upon one's knowledge of the means and time of fulfilment.

**The reported conversation with Abū Bakr.** The same connected account records ʿUmar asking Abū Bakr related questions. This is an instructive distribution of formative responsibility within the community: a companion can reinforce a correction by drawing upon the Prophet's words rather than claiming independent authority to disclose the future. The sequence should be preserved without making every companion's reaction identical.

**Umm Salamah and the form of effective instruction.** The final part of the report is indispensable. Repeated commands to sacrifice and shave are not followed. Umm Salamah identifies a practical way to make the command recognizable and practicable: the Prophet goes out and performs the required rites. The companions follow. The episode is not simply an encomium of female wisdom; it shows the Prophet receiving advice, judging its appropriateness, and acting in a manner that enables a hesitant group to translate understood words into deeds.

At al-Ḥudaybiyyah, the treaty created real obligations even while Abū Jandal's arrival made one clause painful to witness. The Companions understood the command to complete the sacrificial rites but hesitated after the disappointment of being prevented from entering Makkah. In [al-Bukhārī 2731–2732](https://sunnah.com/bukhari/54/19), Umm Salamah advises the Prophet to act visibly without further explanation; he judges the advice suitable and the Companions follow his example. The distinction is not between law and kindness but among a binding instruction, the distress of those addressed, practical counsel and the mode by which persons are enabled to act. Fiqh may examine the treaty and ritual duties; education examines demonstration; political history studies the truce. Their truths remain partial apprehensions of the integrated event.

**Station of no station: a controlled comparison.** Chittick explains Ibn ʿArabī's *maqām lā maqām* as comprehensive realization without restriction to one conditioned prophetic mode; its inheritors see with the universal eye and the eye that recognizes the claims of the present moment. The treaty provides an historically attested situation in which military restraint, fulfilment of worship, fidelity to public agreement, compassion, consultation and instruction interact. This is not evidence that Ibn ʿArabī's technical theory was already verbally formulated in the treaty report, or that we have observed the Prophet's unexpressed inner state. It is a disciplined metaphysical *interpretation* of the integrated pattern, not a replacement for the political narrative.

**The limits of private virtue.** A person may develop patience, trust and courage while being unable to determine the proper wording of a treaty. Competence in negotiation, legal agreement and the recognition of an ally's injury is not identical to the spiritual disposition needed to endure a disappointment. The Prophet's comprehensive action places these different goods in ordered relation; later people, lacking his unique commission, need specialized expertise and mutual counsel.

**Historical mechanism of transformation.** A truce reduced the immediate cost of travelling and speaking across two centres; alliance clauses altered obligations toward third parties; the deferred pilgrimage set a public test of trust in the agreement; later conflict among allied groups brought the treaty's responsibilities back into view. These are material and political effects. They cannot be inferred from a metaphysical principle alone and should not be made into a triumphalist teleology in which every participant benefited equally.

**The source-critical horizon.** In addition to Bukhārī, the accounts of Ibn Isḥāq as preserved by Ibn Hishām, shorter reports about the wording, Qurʾān 48 and its early tafsīr require line-by-line comparison. The treaty's exact provisions, the arrangement of different negotiations and reports of subsequent conversions cannot be treated as fully verified here. The critical apparatus should include the differing witnesses and their shared lines of transmission.

**Relation to later civilizational forms.** Later jurists examined the treaty's legal obligations, while teachers drew on the companions' response to discuss patience, consultation and obedience. Historians trace how the truce and the alliances changed relations between Makkah and Madinah. None of those analyses by itself reproduces the integrated original. A later jurist or ruler can study how the claim of one suffering person and the conditions of collective peace must be weighed; the reporter's words do not automatically prescribe every future treaty. The enduring inheritance is therefore an authoritative source joined to the need for actual competence and formation.

## 17. Khaybar, the deferred pilgrimage and changing relations beyond Madinah

**Matn.** The truce with Quraysh allowed the Prophet ﷺ to direct attention to other parts of Arabia. At Khaybar, Muslim forces obtained control over fortified settlements and cultivated land. Under an agreement with the Muslim authorities, Jewish cultivators continued working Khaybar's fields in return for an agreed share of the produce. The settlement required cooperation over successive harvests.

The following year the Muslims fulfilled the deferred pilgrimage visit under the agreement with Quraysh. Entry into the sanctuary had religious significance, while observing its limited duration and terms required public restraint between parties with a history of hostility. The Khaybar settlement concerned the continuing use of farmland, while the pilgrimage demonstrated that the terms agreed with Quraysh could be observed.

As the Prophet ﷺ received envoys and negotiated new associations beyond Madinah, more people came under arrangements whose conduct could not be directed through his immediate physical presence in every place. Relations with cultivators, merchants and religiously distinct groups required promises that would remain effective when a particular encounter had ended. The growing community had to honour agreements and administer land and revenue in places where many inhabitants did not share its religious allegiance.

**Sharḥ.** The Khaybar report through Ibn ʿUmar in Bukhārī 2331 and 4248 identifies a half-share of the cultivated produce, though the reports' shared chain should not be represented as two independent attestations. Later questions of tenancy, crop-sharing and tenure require additional evidence. Agricultural time is not simply the instant of a treaty: a tree requires care before fruit, water must be distributed, harvest quantities are variable and parties need trustworthy methods for estimating and dividing what is produced. The institutional relation is made durable by practices distributed across persons and seasons.

Khaybar required sustained work after the fighting ceased. The reported agreement allowed Jewish cultivators to work fields for a share of the harvest ([al-Bukhārī 2331](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2331)); both sides depended on the real competence of those who knew the land. Legal analysis asks what contract was made and under which conditions; administration must determine shares, continuity of tenure and the treatment of disputes. Worship and the ethical requirement of honouring agreements are not thereby reduced to crop accounting, but cannot be lived by ignoring it. A comprehensive orientation to justice gives these activities a proper end; it does not substitute for agricultural knowledge or the actual historical terms of the settlement.

The deferred ʿumrah operates through another set of relations. A ritual orientation toward a sacred centre remains irreducible to diplomacy, yet its performance depended historically on negotiated safety and the other party's compliance with a limited agreement. This is a meeting of spiritual purpose and external obligation, not their identity. The same Prophet directed both relationships, and their later technical legal treatment would proceed through different domains of fiqh.

As communities grew beyond Madinah, the difference between personal trust and durable institutional credibility increased. People needed to know that an agent would honour an arrangement even when the Prophet was not physically standing beside him. The historical work of ʿumrān is therefore also the work of reproducing truthful action through many separated people and repeated encounters.

The Khaybar traditions are distributed across hadith, maghāzī and legal sources; Bukhārī's chapters on cultivation and the reports about the Prophet's dealings with Khaybar's inhabitants are important. Reports differ about the settlement's subsequent administration, and later jurists derived rulings on land and crop-sharing from the Khaybar precedent. Khaybar's agricultural settlement preserved cultivation while transferring control over land revenues. Agricultural land cannot be treated as a bag of captured goods: cultivation requires labour, access to water, seasonal effort and some expectation about shares. Continuing arrangements therefore put the new authorities in relation to people who did not all become part of the same religious household.

The agricultural dimension also gives the later Islamic law of landholding a historical problem to solve. Crop-sharing is a continuing relationship, not a one-time transaction: access to fields, division of produce and the labour of cultivators must be sustained season after season. New authority could enforce an arrangement while relying upon the knowledge of existing cultivators. The deferred pilgrimage instead tested whether Quraysh and the Muslims would honour the agreed truce and access arrangements. Placing the episodes together is useful only if their distinct effects are explained: one enlarged the community's fiscal responsibilities, the other demonstrated the public credibility of negotiated commitments.

A tangible contrast helps the two episodes belong together. The deferred ʿumrah tested whether public promises about access to the sanctuary would be honoured; the Khaybar agreement concerned agricultural labour over repeated harvests. Bukhārī 2329 transmits Ibn ʿUmar's report of an arrangement for half the produce of fruit or crops, with later provisions for the Prophet's wives also narrated. The reported initial agricultural settlement and later adjustments belonged to distinct stages of Khaybar's administration. Khaybar involved continued cultivation under an agricultural arrangement; the deferred pilgrimage depended on a negotiated agreement with Quraysh.

The deferred pilgrimage under the treaty also had a distinct function. It was an embodied return to a sanctuary that had shaped Meccan worship and identity, now undertaken by a community whose collective commitments had changed. Religiously it confirmed the continuity of the Kaʿbah with Abrahamic worship; politically it showed that an agreement could restrain old enemies while allowing public rites. Later narratives sometimes run treaty, Khaybar and pilgrimage together, but their institutional consequences should remain distinguishable. The linked expansion of alliance and obligation made the eventual breach between Quraysh-associated and Muslim-associated tribes especially consequential.

The reported agreement allowed local cultivators to work the land in return for a stipulated share of its produce. Ibn ʿUmar's report in al-Bukhārī 2331 and 4248 says that Khaybar's Jewish cultivators were to work the land for half of its produce. The two versions pass through the same Ibn ʿUmar–Nāfiʿ line, so their agreement is not independent documentary corroboration. This nevertheless grounds an explanation of why victory led immediately to questions of labour, possession, revenue and ongoing obligations. The record does not define the land-tenure status of every household or settle later juristic disputes about agricultural contracts. [Al-Bukhārī 2331](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2331); [4248](https://sunnah.com/bukhari/64/283).

## 18. The return to Makkah and the incorporation of Arabia

**Matn.** When fighting among allied groups breached the arrangements of al-Ḥudaybiyyah, the Prophet ﷺ advanced toward Makkah in 630 with a force capable of limiting organized resistance. The city that had resisted his proclamation came under his authority. Idols were removed from the Kaʿbah, and its worship was restored to the one God. The Prophet issued assurances of security and dealt with people who had previously opposed him. The transmitted accounts describe extensive reconciliation while differing about particular exceptions and incidents.

Even after Makkah's surrender, the Muslims faced resistance elsewhere in Arabia. At Ḥunayn a coalition confronted the Muslims, and initial reversals showed that a large force was not a sufficient ground for confidence. Captives, wealth and their distribution brought questions about justice, recent allegiance and the circumstances of particular persons. More Arabian groups approached the Prophet and entered relations of instruction, tribute, alliance or religious commitment under differing conditions.

The Prophet sent teachers, envoys and collectors to communities beyond Madinah. They taught prayer and religious duties and helped administer the obligations of newly allied groups. As Islam spread, people increasingly received instruction through his appointed representatives rather than through personal meetings with him.

**Sharḥ.** Makkah's return is a reorientation of a sacred trust rather than simply the transfer of a site. The Qurʾān's account of Ḥunayn (9:25–27) explicitly counters confidence in sheer numbers. Yet the historical treatment of Meccan amnesty must be discriminating: different transmitted accounts include specific exceptions, casualties and legal decisions. A general praise of mercy cannot justify suppressing those details or imputing one simple intention to every former opponent.

The Prophet's treatment of former opponents after the conquest of Makkah involved people who had reason to fear retaliation from a newly victorious authority. Offers of security and acts of reconciliation changed their practical possibilities without erasing every reported exception or dispute over individual cases. Qurʾān 9:25–27 places the later campaign at Ḥunayn under a warning against confidence in numbers alone. Fiqh can discuss protection and public rights; ethical inquiry considers forbearance under power; political analysis follows alliances and the incorporation of new groups. These are differentiations of one historically enacted orientation, not evidence that political triumph by itself establishes spiritual perfection. Teachers and collectors then carried authoritative words into places the Prophet could not visit continuously.

The Akbarian description of comprehensive humanity also clarifies why political predominance does not by itself prove spiritual perfection. To exercise mercy, justice and governance rightly, the agent must distinguish different claims according to their object and circumstance. A community's formal acknowledgement of the Prophet's position can expand under military pressure without thereby proving that every participant has attained *taḥqīq* or the spiritual state of an earlier close Companion.

The practical continuation lies in teachers and deputies. Instructions conveyed by an envoy are texts and habits in motion; a collector's honesty concerns money and personal trust; a local teacher has to recognize a student's questions; a judge must know the facts and applicable rule. These partial offices are necessary to extend a common orientation beyond the radius of one person's presence. Yet no one office reproduces the whole prophetic act, and the warrant for its authority remains limited.

The continuity of the Kaʿbah with Ibrāhīm's sanctuary, rather than its political possession alone, gives its restored cultic purpose. The extended civilizational history must likewise ask what each institution is **for**, how it works, and whether its operators still form persons able to realize that purpose.

The conquest is discussed in Qurʾān 110 and in hadith and Sīrah accounts; the Ḥunayn reversals in 9:25–27. The exact numbers, speeches and scope of amnesty are not all equally secure. The principal Muslim accounts describe Muḥammad's entry into Makkah and removal of idols; reports about individual exceptions differ and require separate evaluation. The Meccan sanctuary was not discarded: it was redefined within the worship taught by the Prophet. That mattered because pilgrimage, kin memory and the practical gathering of tribes were already rooted in it.

Integration also depended on the limits of central presence. A new Muslim household in a distant region could not consult the Prophet about every prayer, marriage or commercial dispute. Envoys and local teachers therefore had to communicate a core of practice while learning what questions the community actually faced. Tax collection brought another kind of encounter: people had to know the amount, purpose and authority of payment, and collectors had to be trusted not to abuse their power. Religious identity could expand rapidly after military victory, but the competence to live that identity required sustained instruction. After the Farewell Pilgrimage, the Companions carried the Prophet's instructions into communities that had not witnessed his conduct directly.

Incorporation is not an event completed by the removal of idols. After entry into Makkah, teachers, delegates and collectors had to make ritual competence and recurrent payments possible in regions distant from the Prophet. Newly allied groups could acknowledge the Prophet's authority before teachers and revenue agents established regular religious practice; formal allegiance alone did not establish inward conviction. The exact accounts of amnesty, individual exceptions and numbers of troops require report-level comparison.

Once many groups entered new relations, instructions required carriers. An envoy could convey belief and prayer, but regular contributions and disputes called for local collectors, teachers and judgments. The Prophet's dealings with delegations thus show the beginnings of a transmission problem: guidance could not depend indefinitely upon every person travelling to Madinah. Religious language, social custom and economic arrangements varied across Arabia. The Farewell Pilgrimage would give a public and widely witnessed example of worship while drawing attention to common obligations; the Prophet's subsequent death would make questions of transmission and authority unavoidable.

## 19. The Farewell Pilgrimage and the responsibilities that survived the Prophet

**Matn.** Believers from different regions gathered to accompany the Prophet ﷺ on his final pilgrimage. At the sanctuary and through ʿArafāt, Muzdalifah and Minā, they observed rites he performed and heard instructions joining worship to the inviolability of human life and property. Jābir and other Companions later described the rites and the Prophet's instructions from what they had personally witnessed. Members of his household also transmitted aspects of his conduct that were not visible to the wider gathering.

The pilgrimage joined public worship with reminders of people's obligations toward one another. The words of consecration, movement and prayer joined people to Abrahamic worship, while the Prophet condemned inherited forms of exploitation and recalled responsibilities among families and communities. He directed persons with distinct needs through rites performed in a common order.

During his final illness, the Companions faced the prospect of losing the person from whom they had received revelation and direct guidance. When he died in 632, revelation would not be reopened through another law-giving prophet. Families, merchants, judges and worshippers continued to need instruction and decisions after his death. The community had to preserve his teachings and find ways to exercise responsibilities that could no longer be brought to him personally.

Different modes of inheritance were already visible among those who had known him: reciters remembered revelation, witnesses preserved words and acts, members of his household supplied private knowledge, teachers formed pupils, and persons entrusted with judgments acted within their authority. Those responsibilities would later develop into distinct scholarly and political offices. The people who held them could transmit and apply aspects of the Prophet's guidance, but none could succeed to his unique office of receiving revelation.

**Sharḥ.** **Public exemplary action.** Jābir ibn ʿAbd Allāh's long report on the Farewell Pilgrimage in [Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1218a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:1218a), together with 1218b and other reports, supplies a connected account of the rites. It is a major witness because it relates bodily movements, places, legal answers, public speech and the narrator's recollections. Its later reception is itself an act of transmission: Jaʿfar ibn Muḥammad reports from his father Muḥammad al-Bāqir asking Jābir about the Prophet's pilgrimage. This relationship is too historically and intellectually significant to be compressed into “a hadith says.” It joins a Companion's memory to later generations of an important family of religious learning.

**Different observers, a shared rite.** The participants did not occupy one all-seeing position. A witness to a public sermon and a wife familiar with private conduct might preserve different aspects of the same Prophet's guidance. Students subsequently needed to distinguish how a report was heard or observed, what an act implied and whether it belonged to the Prophet's unique particularities. A correct juridical answer concerning the rites can be fully valid while remaining far from a complete biography of the person who enacted them.

**Pilgrimage as worship and common practice.** The rites brought together intention, bodily movement, appointed places and a large gathering of believers. Consecration and repeated movement do not simply generate a society for their own sake: they direct believers toward God through inherited, embodied rites. Yet the need for water, transport, care of the sick, shared space and instruction makes communal organization materially real. A complete explanation must give both accounts without mistaking the religious end for the engineering of a pilgrimage road.

**The public rights proclaimed.** In Jābir's report, the address emphasizes the sacred inviolability of blood and property and opposes inherited practices of retaliatory vengeance and usury, with specific examples named in the tradition. The legal consequences of each statement require comparison with related reports and later fiqh; the transmitted sermon should not be fabricated into one universally agreed verbatim English reconstruction stitched from distinct versions. The central point is nevertheless plain: worship at the sacred centre is linked to the proper exercise of human rights and obligations when people return to daily life.

**The possibility of enactment outside a small household.** The rites could be observed and repeated by people who had not experienced the intimacy of the first Meccan circle. This was genuine generalization of guidance through actual bodies and known practices, not mechanical duplication of the Prophet's incomparable inward spiritual state. The rite is valid insofar as its obligations are properly performed; spiritual fullness may depend upon states of repentance, concentration and trust that cannot be inferred automatically from attendance.

**The role of women and family transmitters.** The Prophet's wives, particularly ʿĀʾishah and Umm Salamah, preserved aspects of domestic life and the reasons or circumstances behind individual decisions. The credibility of their witness was not superseded by the larger gathering. A public sermon cannot disclose how the Prophet served his family or responded to the child's crying; no one of these events makes the other irrelevant. The first generation already contained a plurality of access-points to one life.

**The unique prophetic office.** In the Prophet's lifetime, receiving revelation, teaching, correcting dispositions, ordering worship and exercising public judgment were present within his uniquely commissioned person, although he delegated many tasks. The Qurʾān associates his mission with recitation, purification, teaching the Book and wisdom (62:2). This is not a blueprint allocating one office called “fiqh” to a jurist, another called “taṣawwuf” to a spiritual master and another called “politics” to a caliph. These later disciplinary differentiations had their own histories.

**What follows from finality.** The Prophet's death does not end divine guidance, the power of the Qurʾān, the practice of Sunnah or the possibility of inward realization through worship and obedience. It ends the life of the unique recipient of the final law-giving prophetic mission. Later persons may preserve and act upon his instructions, possess specialized skills, and—in the Sufi account—inherit works, states and knowledge at different degrees. The recipient's genuine excellence does not make him a second Prophet or an independent owner of revelation.

Ibn ʿArabī's account of prophetic inheritance, as discussed by [William Chittick](https://www.persee.fr/doc/horma_0984-2616_1996_num_30_1_1371), distinguishes works, states and knowledge. These are not separate professions into which the Prophet can be disassembled. At the Farewell Pilgrimage, people learned embodied rites, heard obligations concerning the security of life and property, and received guidance from someone whose comprehensive prophetic commission they could not reproduce. Jābir's report in [Muslim 1218a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:1218a) travels through later named transmitters, including Jaʿfar ibn Muḥammad's report from his father. A transmitted legal rule may be genuinely authoritative, while the actual spiritual fullness of the Prophet's worship is not transferred intact with a written description. Finality makes faithful differentiated inheritance necessary without making it identical to renewed revelation.

**The danger of a reverse reduction.** It would be mistaken to call fiqh a mere false shadow of the spiritually realized whole. A correct legal judgment can protect rights in ways not replaceable by a saint's privately felt intuition. Equally, legal validity does not guarantee that the judge or worshipper is free of vanity. Distinct types of proof and formation are necessary, and their proper relationship is part of the unfinished task of civilization.

**Succession without conflation.** The first disputed political decisions after the Prophet's death are treated in Chapter 20. Sunni and Imāmī accounts disagree fundamentally about the form of legitimate authority and the role of designation, the Prophet's family and the Imams. A metaphysical doctrine of the Perfect Human cannot be used to decide a historical dispute without evidence and argument. The participants in Saqīfah cannot simply be assigned technical terms from centuries later.

**Transmission after the Prophet's death.** His teaching continued through recitation, reports of his conduct, witnessed rites, household instruction, juristic judgment and the guidance of later scholars and spiritual teachers. These are neither independent religions nor automatically harmonious functions. Each can preserve something of a shared prophetic orientation and each can become detached from it. The later history must trace actual chains, manuscripts, offices and practices rather than assert that a universal original simply flows into every institution.

The Farewell Pilgrimage thus stands at the centre of the complete work. The Prophet's ﷺ public and intimate presence has taught people how to live; the challenge after his death is not to construct a substitute for that entire presence, but to preserve truthful, graded and mutually accountable forms of inheritance through which later persons can continue to become capable of worship, judgment and service.

# Book IV — Succession, conquest and imperial government

## 20. After the Prophet: allegiance and the survival of obligations

**Matn.** After the Prophet ﷺ died, the community could no longer bring new disputes to him or receive further revelation. It still needed leaders to direct public affairs, teachers to preserve his guidance and judges to settle disagreements. At the Saqīfah of Banū Sāʿidah, Anṣār and leading emigrants debated leadership and Abū Bakr received allegiance; the Prophet's household had not participated in the first meeting, and disputes over succession, property and interpretation did not disappear. Later Sunni and Imāmī traditions preserved different judgments about legitimate authority.

Many Arabian groups also reconsidered the ties that had joined them to Madinah. Some disputed payments of zakāt, others followed competing prophetic claimants, and some sought greater political autonomy. Abū Bakr insisted that public religious obligations were not extinguished by the death of the Messenger. Campaigns associated with the Ridda, including al-Yamāmah, brought heavy losses as authority was reasserted. Concern for preserving Qurʾānic recitation acquired new urgency, while teachers, collectors and governors continued working across distinct settlements.

The community now faced questions that had been answered during the Prophet's life through his own authority. Who would command the armies and appoint officials? Who would interpret difficult teachings or decide disputes? Who would transmit what he had said and done? These tasks would be carried out by different people and institutions in subsequent generations. The question of legitimate succession remained contested, and no successor could receive new revelation as a law-giving prophet.

**Sharḥ.** After the Prophet's death, the community had to continue government, teaching and adjudication without receiving new revelation. These responsibilities did not all pass to one office in the same way. A ruler may maintain defence, authorization of officials and collection of public revenues without receiving revelation. A transmitter may preserve words faithfully without becoming a caliph; a jurist may decide cases without thereby possessing a saint's degree of inward witnessing. This is not a retroactive claim that the first participants used the later division of disciplines. It is a necessary analysis of the different kinds of work whose continuation was at issue.

Saqīfah was a real meeting among named groups with political commitments and contested expectations, not the visible execution of a pre-existing metaphysical hierarchy. Sunni accounts of allegiance and Imāmī accounts of designation differ materially over the sources and nature of authority. The Prophet's family, the events of Ghadīr Khumm and Fāṭimah's property claims require careful independent discussion; one cannot use “comprehensiveness” to settle the debate by fiat.

After the Prophet's death, military command, collection of zakāt, adjudication, instruction and the preservation of revelation still required responsible persons. The dispute at Saqīfah concerns actual competing understandings of legitimate succession: Sunni and Imāmī historical and theological sources must be allowed their own claims rather than converted into interchangeable metaphors. In the Akbarian tradition, the persistence of *wilāya* and graded spiritual inheritance does not confer a second law-giving prophethood, nor make political allegiance sufficient evidence of inward realization. The inherited functions had to be exercised by fallible people in specific institutions. One cannot deduce who held a historical office or how a contested pledge occurred from a universal metaphysical hierarchy.

Abū Bakr's insistence on payments is a distinct historical argument: after the Prophet's death, what publicly binding obligations survived? The answer had effects on collectors, households, allegiance and the distribution of resources. Different tribes' actions during the Ridda cannot be assimilated into one identical motive. Reports associating Qurʾānic collection with losses at al-Yamāmah stand alongside evidence for written passages that circulated earlier. Public stability was a condition for continuing many forms of instruction, not the substance of that instruction itself.

The surviving accounts identify disputes over allegiance, consultation, the Prophet's family and the authority of the first caliph. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, reports concerning the allegiance and ʿUmar's retrospective description of Saqīfah, and the traditions concerning Fāṭimah's claim and ʿAlī's later allegiance are essential Sunni witnesses. They require direct reading, not paraphrases that erase conflict. Sunni theology generally distinguishes the non-prophetic office of caliph from the Prophetic gift of revelation; Twelver Shiʿi theology argues that ʿAlī and the Imams possess a divinely designated leadership and authoritative religious guidance. Ghadīr Khumm and the relation of *mawlā* to *wilāyah* feature in the dispute, but a full evaluation of competing reports would displace the history's principal purpose. Sunni discussions generally ground Abū Bakr's caliphate in allegiance and communal obligation; Twelver Shiʿi discussions understand ʿAlī's authority through the doctrine of designation and the Imam's religious office.

The difficulty of succession was especially acute because the Prophet left several kinds of obligation in operation. A governor needed orders, a tax collector needed authorization, an army needed command, and a family needed to know whom to consult over a dispute. The early pledge to Abū Bakr addressed the public coordination of those tasks. It did not remove affection and distinct religious claims attached to the Prophet's household. Keeping these forms of concern separate clarifies both the practical necessity Sunni sources emphasize and the continuing Imāmī argument about divinely designated guidance. Conquests subsequently multiplied the need for commanders, governors, judges and officials able to administer distant provinces.

Abū Bakr's insistence on continued zakāt payments was a decision about whether public obligations survived the Prophet's death. Enforcing zakāt after the Prophet's death concerned public obligation; the grounds of authoritative succession remained a separate theological dispute. The Sunni account treats his caliphate as the first political continuation; Twelver Shiʿi doctrine gives ʿAlī a divinely designated place in the succession to religious guidance. Only the indispensable evidence and principal interpretive difference belong here; the disputed speeches and details of Fadak can remain in a focused note.

The Ridda involved more than a uniform attempt to renounce religion. The actions of different tribes concerned tax, allegiance, local autonomy and prophetic claimants in varying combinations. Keeping military and fiscal relationships functioning was crucial to sustaining the new association: without it, local commanders, teachers and communities would operate under incompatible claims of authority. Al-Bukhārī and later collections associate losses at al-Yamāmah with a collection of Qurʾānic material; other reports also attest written Qurʾānic passages before 632. The expansion after Abū Bakr built on this reconsolidation but exposed the community to institutions much larger than those of Arabia.

## 21. Conquest and the problems inherited from older empires

**Matn.** The consolidation of authority in Arabia was followed by campaigns in Syria, Iraq and Egypt. Muslim commanders met established Roman and Sasanian military structures and won major battles, including al-Yarmūk and al-Qādisiyyah. Winning a battle did not settle how the conquered provinces would be governed. Farmers required water and access to fields; priests and local officials maintained institutions; soldiers needed provisions; scribes knew languages and forms of accounts that the conquerors had not created.

New governors negotiated surrender, adapted administrative practices, gathered revenue and dealt with populations whose religious commitments did not immediately change. Arab garrison towns, including al-Baṣrah, al-Kūfah and al-Fusṭāṭ, kept groups of soldiers and their families organized while surrounding rural communities continued much of their own cultivation. Conversion, linguistic change and legal transformation occurred at different rates and under different conditions.

During the Prophet's lifetime, many disputes could be brought directly to him. In the conquered provinces, governors and judges had to make decisions far from Madinah, often through local officials and written records. Questions of taxation, land ownership, religious difference and the rights of cultivators became unavoidable. Rulers needed administrators who understood the provinces and scholars who could apply religious guidance to unfamiliar disputes.

**Sharḥ.** The conquest made Muslim commanders responsible for lands and populations whose needs continued after the battles. Food supplies, taxation, security and property had to be administered under the new rulers. Each involves real claims of persons and material systems whose proper use could be governed by religious ends. But the historically specific instruments—tax registers, local intermediaries, garrison assignments and agreements—cannot be deduced from metaphysical principles. Administrative papyri, coins and regional chronicles constrain the broad narratives of later conquest writers.

The difference between a rightful end and a specific method of achieving it becomes sharper as scale increases. A provincial governor may sincerely proclaim justice while relying on local officials he does not understand; a soldier might receive wages drawn from a farmer whose harvest has failed; a Christian or Jewish community may possess its own institutions while living under new sovereign power. The concrete principle is *knowledge of differentiated rights*: obligations must be assessed in relation to actual persons and circumstances, not a generic declaration of equality or domination.

A commander in Syria or Egypt might have authority to maintain peace yet depend on local cultivators, administrators and scribes for water, tax and property records. Inherited provincial arrangements could be retained, revised or contested without a single uniform model. Normative guidance concerning justice reaches real people through these mediations: a trustworthy official must know whose fields are assessed and which promises bind him. The Akbarian account of comprehensive right action can criticize an office that equates its narrow task with all human good, but it cannot itself choose an irrigation schedule or calculate a tax. Provincial ʿumrān has independent material and political causes, and later fiqh developed categories for judgments that the Prophet had never had to administer in exactly these settings.

The changing geography also matters. Egypt, Syria and Iraq did not adopt one identical fiscal regime in one year, and the legal use of terms such as *jizya* and *kharāj* varied across regions. Later fiqh systematized questions sharpened by these provinces; its technical forms should not be projected backwards as the explicit wording of every first-century agreement. The relation of historical power and moral obligation is continuous, while their institutional vehicles change.

Conquest gave commanders territory, but administering that territory required decisions about cultivators, local officials, soldiers and revenue. Armies could acquire territorial authority in a campaigning season; provisioning troops and assessing land required continuing negotiation with cultivators, notables and former officials. Al-Balādhurī's *Futūḥ al-buldān* preserves accounts of treaties and settlements, but details vary across recensions and may reflect subsequent fiscal controversies. Administrative papyri and coins offer independent controls on gradual changes in government and language. Muslim governors adapted some existing fiscal practices and altered others; the conquered provinces did not immediately share one uniform tax code. The Syrian, Iraqi and Egyptian arrangements followed different trajectories.

The division between garrison and countryside helps explain why the conquerors' language and legal practices could spread without immediately making the subject population Muslim. Tax resources went to military stipends and government, while established cultivators and religious institutions continued many activities. The eventual change of language, conversion and local custom therefore took generations and differed by region. Governors in conquered provinces had to determine levies, recognize land claims, uphold agreements and restrain soldiers far from Madinah. The moral language of justice was inherited from the Prophetic teaching, but its application now involved a more complex set of agents and documents. Hugh Kennedy and the *New Cambridge History of Islam* document the variation among conquest settlements; surviving treaty reports do not preserve one uniform agreement.

Dated receipts and correspondence from Egypt preserve administrative practices that continued or changed under Muslim rule. A landholder and a soldier encountered power differently: the soldier needed regular provisions; the cultivator needed a predictable demand and protection from arbitrary seizure. Their interaction connected military policy to harvest cycles and irrigation. Nor is the word *jizyah* consistently used for one identical early fiscal arrangement in all sources; terminology and practice need local comparison. This matters to the later juristic tradition because arguments about the rights of cultivators and protected communities emerged in the aftermath of real negotiated settlements, some remembered through legalizing retrospective narratives. After victory, officials collected revenue, cultivators worked the land, and commanders supplied troops; these recurring duties sustained political control beyond the battlefield.

## 22. The garrison, the treasury and the provincial community

**Matn.** The new garrison towns grew into communities where soldiers and their families lived alongside traders, freed persons, clients, teachers and judges. Military quarters often followed inherited tribal affiliations, while common prayer and shared affairs brought people of different descent into repeated association. Stipend payments depended upon revenue from cultivated land and the decisions of officials in distant provinces.

The *dīwān* recorded soldiers and other recipients entitled to stipends. Its lists helped officials distribute revenue, but they also established distinctions in payment and standing that could become sources of dispute. Early participants in conquest could enjoy claims unavailable to later converts or clients. A soldier, an Arab settler, a Persian client and a Christian landholding family could inhabit the same political world while experiencing its authority differently.

The growing towns also needed teachers and judges. Their inhabitants disputed marriages, sales, inheritances, land and relations among religious communities, and could no longer bring such matters to the Prophet ﷺ. Companions and their pupils transmitted words and practices, while legal reasoning began to meet the complexities of a larger population. Disagreements over public revenue and legal status obliged officials and scholars to consider whether existing arrangements were consistent with the justice their religion required.

**Sharḥ.** The *dīwān* recorded claims to public stipends, enabling officials to identify recipients, calculate payments and settle some disputes about entitlement. A register can prevent a rightful recipient from being overlooked; it can also fix an unjust hierarchy or hide the claims of persons who lack representation. The formal accuracy of a record is therefore distinct from the substantive justice of the relation it records. The first relation concerns numbers and identities; the second concerns what the people are due.

The accuracy of a register and the fairness of the payments it records are different questions. Officials could keep careful accounts while still making unjust distinctions among recipients. Rather, it identifies where the spiritual obligation of truthful, proportionate judgment meets administration. Officials must be trained in facts and procedures and also restrained by norms concerning other people's rights.

Ibn Khaldūn's later *ʿaṣabiyya* explains how solidarity can mobilize groups for military and political purposes, while revenue and settlement change the foundations of that power. The growth of al-Kūfah and al-Baṣrah also illustrates a different social trajectory: scholars, traders and clients develop networks of learning that are not identical to a single government's command. The claims of *mawālī* varied across regions and times; their position must not be reconstructed from one later polemical description.

The stipend register, or *dīwān*, gave names and claims a durable written form; it could help allocate revenue while also preserving distinctions of rank that people disputed. Neither careful bookkeeping nor generous intention independently settles whether an entitlement is just. A treasury officer has a defined responsibility, a jurist examines the governing rule, and a claimant can contest the facts recorded. The Prophetic inheritance here is not an invisible mystical quality in the register but the enduring demand for truthful, competent and fair public action. Historians must explain the actual stipends, military organization and relations among Arab settlers, clients and cultivators before judging outcomes. As functions differentiated, their cooperation became a problem that could no longer be settled by personal proximity to the Prophet.

The *dīwān* was a register and payment system, not the equivalent of a modern civil service or a census of all residents. Accounts of garrison foundations and stipend rankings vary, while later settlement and fiscal needs altered administrative practice. Fred Donner traces the interaction of military recruitment, settlement and revenue administration during the early Islamic conquests. The precise policy on Iraqi land is known partly through retrospective juristic accounts in which allocation of conquered lands had become a legal problem.

Ibn Khaldūn later argued that group solidarity helps establish military authority, while durable rule also requires revenue and administration across wider territories. Yet he cannot substitute for the details of seventh-century stipends and provinces. A converted Persian client in al-Kūfah, a landed Christian family in Syria and an Arab soldier receiving payments did not enter the same arrangement. Soldiers, cultivators, clients, officials and scholars held different claims on revenues, property and public authority. It also explains why towns of conquest became centres of learning: they housed people who needed judgments on marriage, sale, slavery, inheritance and disputes involving communities with different practices. Students learned from Companions and successors amid these recurring problems rather than in a social vacuum.

Differences in military stipends shaped household income, prestige and competition for public resources. If an early participant in the campaigns held a recognised claim on the treasury, a later convert or client might not possess an equivalent claim even when sharing the same worship. That tension helps explain debates about the *mawālī* and the eventual pressures on Umayyad government. Clients and provincial populations encountered unequal arrangements, although access to stipends and public office varied across regions and periods. Some were wealthy and influential, and recruitment and fiscal practice changed. The closer historical explanation follows a particular garrison's register, its sources of revenue and its social composition. The relation between inherited solidarity and religious universalism becomes visible through conflicts over payment and status. It also clarifies why instruction mattered: a growing town needed means to identify valid testimony, settle competing property claims and teach new residents obligations that had developed first among a much smaller community.

## 23. Civil war and the limits of loyalty

**Matn.** As the Muslim polity expanded, the power of provincial commanders and the wealth controlled by governors grew. Opposition to ʿUthmān's government intensified; rebels killed him in Madinah in 656. ʿAlī received allegiance amid demands that the killers be punished, but there was no effective agreement on who held authority to enforce retaliation or how the armies involved would submit to judgment. Fighting near al-Baṣrah and later at Ṣiffīn brought Muslims against Muslims. Arbitration and disputes over it divided supporters, and subsequent violence involved the Khārijites. ʿAlī was killed in 661, and al-Ḥasan later reached a settlement with Muʿāwiyah.

The conflict divided people who had shared the Prophet's company or accepted his message. They disagreed over allegiance, punishment for ʿUthmān's killing, the authority of ʿAlī and the obligations of commanders and their followers. Many participants claimed to act rightly, while armies and provincial resources gave their choices lasting consequences.

The later Muslim world inherited both the grief and contested memory of these actions. Communities formulated different understandings of public obedience, rightful leadership and serious sin. The outcome of a battle did not settle the religious arguments raised by its participants. Their disagreements continued to shape Muslim discussions of lawful leadership, rebellion and the place of the Prophet's family.

**Sharḥ.** The first civil war arose amid disputes about the murder of ʿUthmān, the legitimacy of ʿAlī's rule, and who could order lawful retaliation. The duty to answer the murder of ʿUthmān, the need for a government capable of enforcing public orders, the protection of the Prophet's family and the fear of further civil war are not identical problems, even when the same actors invoked several together. To state that the participants faced complex and conflicting demands does not entail that all historical positions were equally justified or that all reports possess equal evidentiary value.

The events at the Battle of the Camel, Ṣiffīn and the arbitration survive through historiography shaped by different political and theological commitments. Al-Ṭabarī preserves narratives with multiple transmission lines and motives, not a transparent transcript of each participant's deliberations. Sunni and Shiʿi accounts require fair representation rather than treating one later doctrinal formulation as the undisputed consciousness of every actor in 656–661.

During the first civil war, parties made incompatible demands about allegiance, retaliation, political order and the authority of particular leaders. One group might invoke a genuine duty while others disputed its priority, the evidence for its fulfilment or the competence of the person enforcing it. The Akbarian concern with limited stations provides a disciplined question about partial judgment, not authority to pronounce hidden spiritual defects in the participants. Reports of the Battle of the Camel, Ṣiffīn and arbitration have contested chains and political histories; the historian must reconstruct those separately. The resulting legal and theological arguments about public obedience cannot be read straight off a single victory or an imagined one-sentence morality tale.

The event's continuing civilizational force came through teaching, family memory, political loyalties, polemics and legal-theological elaboration. A personal grievance could become a public demand; a declaration of allegiance could commit armed men; a judgment about authority could become the enduring norm of a school. These transformations explain how the micro-level becomes larger ʿumrān, and why source criticism must restrain symbolic reconstruction.

The broad order of events is well attested in early Muslim historiography; the words attributed to individual participants, precise motives and details of arbitration require more caution. Al-Ṭabarī brings together reports from transmitters with different affiliations, and these are not independent eyewitness accounts merely because they appear side by side. Sunni histories recognize the leading Companions' standing while preserving accounts of disputed decisions, armed conflict and deaths among their supporters. Shiʿi accounts give the rights and designation of ʿAlī a different constitutional and theological meaning. Sunni and Shiʿi arguments about legitimate authority developed amid disputes over retaliation, military allegiance and the duties of government.

The civil war also had geographical and material mechanisms. The garrison army of Iraq and the Syrian command were organized forces with their own leadership, pay and attachments. Muʿāwiyah could rely upon a developed provincial military apparatus; ʿAlī had to govern amid divided coalitions and disputes about the killing of ʿUthmān. Later kalām arguments about a grave sinner and political obedience grew in a world that remembered those choices. Rival factions invoked justice, but disputed who possessed authority to punish ʿUthmān's killers and whether political allegiance must precede retaliation. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Balādhurī preserve differing accounts of the first fitna, especially in reported speeches and motives; their transmissions do not independently establish each participant's private intentions.

Later theological classifications of the first fitna do not establish the exact beliefs or motives of every participant in the original conflicts. The battle at Ṣiffīn and the division over arbitration help explain how political disagreement prompted debates over the relation between judgment and divine law. The outcomes of the battles do not establish that every participant held the fully developed doctrines later associated with Khārijites, Sunnis or Shiʿa. The extant speeches are transmitted through literary and partisan filters. The strongest causal account connects the troops' alliances, demands about the killing of ʿUthmān and the procedural problem of retaliation. The later theological writings can then be read as responses to the remembered crisis, with their own dates and purposes. The disputes over allegiance, retaliation and ʿAlī's authority shaped later Sunni and Shiʿi understandings of legitimate government.

## 24. Damascus, Karbalāʾ and the making of an imperial administration

**Matn.** Muʿāwiyah governed from Damascus with Syrian military support and administrative practices drawn partly from earlier provincial worlds. The nomination of Yazīd sharpened disagreement about hereditary succession, consultation and the claims of the Prophet's ﷺ family. Al-Ḥusayn refused allegiance and set out toward Kūfah following appeals from supporters. In 680 he and members of his family and companions were killed at Karbalāʾ. Karbalāʾ became a defining event in Muslim remembrance of the Prophet's family and in continuing disputes about allegiance and legitimate leadership.

Further conflict preceded ʿAbd al-Malik's consolidation of Umayyad rule. State administration expanded its Arabic forms, issued distinctive coinage, sponsored great buildings and organized armies across far-reaching territories. The Dome of the Rock articulated religious claims in inscriptions; tax registers and coins gave the government recurrent means of representing and financing its power. These forms reached populations whose languages, ritual lives and local institutions remained diverse.

Coinage, public buildings and administrative records expressed the authority of the state, but religious learning also continued through jurists, preachers, Qurʾān teachers and ascetics outside the court. Conflicts about the authority of rulers and religious teachers continued throughout the Umayyad period. The memory of Karbalāʾ remained a powerful reason for Muslims to question claims that military control alone established rightful leadership.

**Sharḥ.** At Karbalāʾ, al-Ḥusayn and his companions faced armed opponents and were killed. The participants' choices, the deaths at Karbalāʾ and the event's later remembrance belong to its historical account, which cannot be replaced by spiritual symbolism. The victims, their obligations and choices, and the role of political coercion require primary attention. The sources differ about reported speeches, individuals and numbers, and shared reliance on transmitters such as Abū Mikhnaf must not be presented as independent convergence. The event possesses profound and differing Sunni and Shiʿi theological significance while its human reality remains irreducible to sectarian generalization.

Al-Ḥusayn's killing at Karbalāʾ cannot be converted into a metaphor for the death of spiritual rule: armed men killed real members of the Prophet's family and their companions, and later communities preserved painful, contested memories of the event. A dynasty could command an army and administer taxation without possessing the moral or spiritual legitimacy it claimed. Twelver Imāmī and Sunni accounts distinguish the rightful authority of the Prophet's family differently; those distinctions must be presented accurately rather than collapsed into generic *walāya*. The comprehensive Muhammadan standard judges conduct and obligations but does not turn the historian into a witness of every participant's inward state. Coins, inscriptions and administrative changes testify to a government's capacities, not to its perfected humanity.

Umayyad coinage, inscriptions and architecture are evidence for the public representation of rule and can be dated and compared materially. They do not prove the private assent of every subject or the spiritual quality of every administrator. The slow and uneven transition to Arabic in government also demonstrates how an institution's outward form changes across provincial languages, economic practices and existing staffs.

Religious teaching continued through different channels: reciters, households, jurists, local ascetics and later organized circles. Their autonomy was always conditioned by social relations and political pressures, not an ahistorical contrast between a purely sacred private realm and a purely profane state. The Prophet's total integration could not be recreated by concentrating the treasury and public symbols in Damascus. Its inheritance required more varied forms of fidelity.

Al-Ḥusayn and his companions were killed at Karbalāʾ; reports of speeches and exact casualty figures vary among later transmitters. The death of al-Ḥusayn and his companions is foundational to Shiʿi historical memory, while Sunni accounts also affirm the wrong and grief of the event, even as theological readings diverge. Al-Ṭabarī's use of Abū Mikhnaf and later treatments by al-Mufīd overlap in transmission; agreement between them cannot automatically be counted as independent attestation.

Umayyad coins, inscriptions and buildings record official policies and dynastic claims that later chronicles recount through their own traditions. Currency reform mattered because weights, taxes and payments connected distant provinces through common state symbols and procedures. Arabic administrative change was uneven, and the presence of Arabic in registers did not immediately displace Greek, Coptic and Persian from society. Nor should political consolidation be equated with universal religious acceptance. Schools of Qurʾānic recitation, ascetic teaching and early legal discussion grew within and sometimes against the horizons of state power. Al-Ḥajjāj's governorship and the experience of Iraqi opposition also reveal coercive dimensions of the imperial project. The distinction between the caliph's political authority and a teacher's credibility becomes more intelligible through these particular institutional pressures.

The Dome of the Rock supplies a particularly revealing contrast between official and social evidence. Its Arabic inscriptions present theological claims in a public imperial setting; its construction dates and physical form can be investigated independently of late court anecdotes. Coin inscriptions and administrative documents trace changes in how the state represented and financed itself. Yet none of these tells us what every market trader believed or how every household taught the Qurʾān. That difference matters: an empire may adopt common public signs while local traditions continue in distinct languages and social groups. In later decades the *mawālī*, tax burdens and provincial grievances entered contests over rule. The Abbasid movement arose within a consolidated Umayyad empire where political stability had not settled competing claims to leadership and status.

## 25. The Abbasid revolution and a change of ruling centre

**Matn.** By the middle of the eighth century, the Umayyad empire controlled extensive resources but faced political and social opposition across several provinces. In Khurāsān, military and religious networks mobilized under promises associated with the Prophet's household. Abū Muslim's forces advanced westward, and the Abbasids defeated the Umayyads in 750. The new dynasty drew upon supporters whose expectations of legitimate rule were not all identical, and it did not satisfy every ʿAlid claim.

The political centre shifted toward Iraq. Al-Manṣūr founded Baghdad in 762 on the Tigris, near agricultural revenues, trade routes and existing administrative skill. The court employed soldiers and administrators, while merchants, artisans, physicians, translators and teachers gathered around its markets and households. Patronage and trade helped make Baghdad a centre of scholarship, alongside teaching traditions carried there from other cities.

The revolution changed the dynasty in power without settling every disagreement over religious authority. Scholars taught and debated under the Abbasids, sometimes benefiting from court patronage and sometimes resisting the demands of rulers. Baghdad's prominence grew through court administration, commerce, book production and networks of teachers and students. The relationship between rulers and scholars would shape much of its intellectual history.

**Sharḥ.** The Abbasid revolution combined military organization, ideological promises, regional grievances, fiscal pressures and contested lineage claims. Its recruitment was not exclusively a movement of non-Arab converts, and the slogans circulating in Khurāsān did not specify one final shared theory of legitimacy. Different surviving accounts must be assessed against the political interests of their transmitters and the material organization of the movement.

Baghdad's foundation established an imperial capital, but its subsequent scholarly life grew through the work of translators, teachers, students, scribes and patrons. Baghdad's planned founding, location and palace administration helped supply conditions of settlement, but students learned in real encounters with teachers; scribes and booksellers prepared usable texts; artisans, transporters and farmers supplied the material basis of urban life. Those processes may reinforce court patronage without being reducible to it.

The Abbasid movement drew together Khurāsānian military forces, discontented groups and claims associated with the Prophet's household, but these elements were never a single intention shared by every supporter. After 750, the new rulers still had to finance soldiers, appoint officials and sustain peace; Baghdad's later scholarly life depended on teachers, translators, markets and patrons whose achievements were not gifts mechanically transferred by a caliphal title. The comprehensive Muhammadan exemplar gives a measure for how power ought to serve worship and justice, without suggesting that one caliph can inherit every mode of prophetic knowledge. Historical explanation therefore follows recruitment, administration, the movement of people and the production of books rather than equating the change of dynasty with the renewal of all religious life.

Scholarship in Baghdad depended on teachers able to form students as well as courts and patrons able to sustain them. The persistence of an institution did not guarantee that it continued to fulfil its educational purpose. That question is philosophical and normative; the historical development of Baghdad's schools, translation movements and religious sciences must still be traced through persons, books, patrons and particular controversies.

The Abbasid movement recruited support across several groups, including Khurāsānian military and political networks, and did not represent only non-Arab converts. The movement involved overlapping interests in Khurāsān, including established Arab settler communities, patron-client networks, religious expectations and military organization. Its slogans did not prescribe a single later theory of authority. Consult al-Ṭabarī alongside studies of the Abbasid revolution by M. A. Shaban and Elton Daniel, while recognizing disagreements in their reconstructions.

Khurāsānian support and the establishment of Baghdad in Iraq shifted the caliphate's military and administrative centre eastward. Iraq sat near productive agricultural districts and routes connecting Iran, the Gulf and Syria; Baghdad's foundation under al-Manṣūr in 762 deliberately concentrated administration. But courts do not create intellectual life by decree. The city inherited teachers, translators and merchants from older centres, while paper, libraries, private patrons and students increasingly made scholarship possible beyond the immediate court. Baghdad's scholarly activity depended on administrators, merchants, patrons, booksellers, translators and teachers, not on its foundation alone. Dynastic legitimacy, in turn, continued to be debated among jurists, Alids and different provincial populations; the Abbasid revolution settled who ruled, not every question of who could teach or interpret revelation.

# Book V — How knowledge and spiritual formation acquired lasting institutions

## 26. Baghdad and the work of a learned city

**Matn.** Baghdad developed around a court and an expanding urban economy whose households, workshops, markets and places of instruction drew people from many regions. Soldiers and officials received revenue from the state, but merchants, physicians, translators, jurists and teachers also worked through private patrons, families and their own circles. The Tigris and overland routes carried provisions and texts. Paper, scribes and booksellers increased the means through which knowledge could be copied, compared and circulated.

The scholars working in Baghdad pursued different kinds of inquiry. Physicians compared Greek and Syriac learning with problems of bodily treatment; mathematicians worked with numerical and geometrical proofs; translators made decisions about language and meaning; jurists investigated reports, custom and disputed rights. Scholars sometimes worked together and sometimes disagreed about the limits of one another's methods. Public support could enable difficult work, while public power could also enforce a doctrinal position, as in the *miḥnah*.

Learning depended upon experienced teachers, opportunities to practise, trustworthy copies and patrons or occupations that could support sustained study. A physician gained experience through treating patients; a translator improved texts by comparing manuscripts; students of law and theology learned through debate and correction. Students could acquire books without mastering them, and scholars often disagreed over the proper methods of interpreting revelation and other forms of knowledge.

**Sharḥ.** Knowledge spread in Baghdad through the work of teachers, translators, scribes, patrons and students who depended upon one another. A physician may possess a transmitted treatise without yet having clinical judgment; a translator may know two languages but lack the conceptual distinctions needed for a reliable version of Galen; a jurist may know several reports without knowing the particular facts of a contested contract. Each professional competence has an internal standard and a pedagogy. None becomes valid merely because a politically powerful patron admires it.

Dimitri Gutas's analysis of Graeco-Arabic translation and the history of Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq's scholarly labour distinguish actual people, patrons and texts from an inflated legend of one centralized “House of Wisdom” producing all sciences. Manuscript comparison, glossing, correction and teaching are acts through which a specific community improves its grasp of a subject. The adoption of paper expanded written resources but did not make every student learned or every text reliable.

Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq could compare Greek manuscripts and produce a careful Syriac or Arabic rendering; a physician still needed experience recognizing disease and treating actual patients. A jurist's competence and a translator's competence likewise had different objects. The Prophet's comprehensive guidance cannot be reproduced by aggregating the skills present in Baghdad, yet each skill can contribute genuinely to humane action when exercised rightly. In the Akbarian language of *maqām lā maqām*, universality of spiritual realization must not be mistaken for infallible mastery of technical crafts. This is why the translation movement is better reconstructed through copies, readers, patrons and revision than through one legendary institution possessing every science. The *miḥnah* adds a distinct warning: political force does not prove a disputed doctrine true.

The *miḥnah* sharpens the normative problem: a ruler can possess the power to demand statements from scholars without thereby possessing decisive authority to determine the truth of the proposition demanded. Conversely, a scholar's religious credibility does not by itself supply competence to rule all administrative matters. Baghdad's living achievement lay in a plurality of relations among teachers, scribes, patrons, reasoners, worshippers and rulers. Whether they served truth depended on what those people actually did, not the grandeur of the city.

Hārūn al-Rashīd's court and al-Maʾmūn's patronage are important, but the familiar story of a single 'House of Wisdom' producing all the sciences suppresses the multiplicity of patrons and workplaces. Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq's translation projects, al-Kindī's philosophical circle and the mathematical writing associated with al-Khwārizmī had distinct aims and constituencies. Dimitri Gutas, *Greek Thought, Arabic Culture*, and research on medicine and translation distinguish court translation from wider patterns of scholarly patronage. The adoption of paper expanded the possibilities for copying without making manuscripts cheap for every reader.

Baghdad gathered translators, booksellers, physicians, administrators and patrons who sustained scholarly work through different institutions. Secretarial administration required numeracy and records; medicine attracted patrons and clinical expertise; juridical differences demanded teachers able to transmit and reason; rich households could sustain independent specialists. State patrons, merchants and scholarly specialists jointly sustained Baghdad's translation and book culture, a relationship central to Hodgson's account of the period. But the learned world was not always harmoniously plural. In the *miḥnah*, caliphal demands about the createdness of the Qurʾān brought public theology and state coercion together. Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal's refusal and later memory of the episode exemplify a struggle over the boundary between the ruler's command and a scholar's religious authority. It would be misleading either to call this a conflict between 'religion' and 'science' or to identify all speculative inquiry with official coercion.

The history of translation is most persuasive when treated through a defined task. Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq's work on Greek medical texts involved comparing copies, choosing technical equivalents and revising earlier translations; a practitioner then had to interpret the resulting Arabic work in relation to patients and other medical knowledge. The procedure differed from al-Khwārizmī's mathematical writing or a jurist's evaluation of a report, even when they shared bookshops and patrons. For this reason 'Islamic science' is a historical umbrella, not the name of one single method. During the *miḥnah*, caliphal authorities sponsored intellectual inquiry while pressuring scholars to affirm a particular doctrine about the Qurʾān. Intellectual life became durable through a plurality of communities capable of criticising, transmitting and sponsoring knowledge, although their relative independence varied. Individual translations passed through named patrons, scribes, translators and readers, often with several stages of revision.

## 27. Travelers, households and the authentication of transmitted learning

**Matn.** People who wanted to learn the Prophet's ﷺ teaching could begin with a relative, a trusted teacher or a neighbour who had heard and witnessed him. Different Companions preserved different episodes. His wives remembered aspects of household life unavailable to large public gatherings; others had observed particular judgments, journeys, prayers or treaties. Their pupils met, compared and questioned them, and the search for reliable reports eventually connected Madinah, Kūfah, Baṣrah, Syria, Egypt, Khurāsān and more distant lands through journeys of learning.

Transmitters named the people from whom they had heard a report and described how it had reached them. Critics examined contacts between narrators, memory, discrepancies in wording, hidden defects and the possibility that different routes depended upon one earlier teacher. Students therefore learned not only reports but methods of testing them, while different schools could agree on a report and still dispute its legal meaning.

Written works multiplied the range of accessible accounts. The Muwaṭṭaʾ circulated in different recensions, carrying variants in transmitted reports and in Mālik's attached explanations. Later compilations, including the Ṣaḥīḥ collections of al-Bukhārī and Muslim, represented exacting critical undertakings without becoming one final legal encyclopedia. A trustworthy report could establish what the Prophet had said or done, while determining its meaning and application sometimes required further study.

Teachers corrected students, scholars compared copies, families preserved memories of the Prophet's domestic life and jurists debated the significance of reports. These activities helped the Sunnah reach generations who had never met the Prophet himself.

**Sharḥ.** Scholars transmitting ḥadīth examined several separate questions: what the Prophet had said or done, which narrators reported it, what wording they preserved and what religious obligations followed from it. The same text can be a reliable witness to an action while leaving open questions of whether the action was obligatory, context-specific or an instance of a more general principle. Early transmitters did not receive one fully completed school manual from the Prophet; the schools' later procedures arose within different social and intellectual conditions.

The household is vital. ʿĀʾishah's extensive transmission concerns ritual practice, intimate conduct and questions about the conditions behind religious words. Bukhārī 103 ([text](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:103)) describes her asking again when a statement appeared to conflict with a Qurʾānic passage about easy reckoning. The response distinguishes the presentation of accounts from a contested reckoning. The scene shows that an authoritative hearer need not be a passive memorizer; responsible inquiry involves noticing a real difficulty and obtaining a distinction from a teacher. The same principle can later be cultivated through commentary, but no commentary reproduces the immediate prophetic conversation.

Male and female learners had different opportunities according to household circumstances, travel and social structures. Bukhārī 101 ([text](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:101)) records women asking the Prophet for a dedicated time of instruction. Later learned women taught and transmitted through families and public scholarly circles. The existence of such witnesses does not erase historical inequalities or establish a uniform educational structure throughout all later lands.

The *isnād* is both a chain and an **accountable claim**. A teacher's name can be examined against chronology and the possibility of meeting; two reports with matching words can depend upon one earlier route; textual variants can result from abbreviation, memory or correction. Inclusion in a recognized compilation is not a substitute for understanding its editorial principles. Nor are a modern historical reconstruction of a report's earliest ascertainable use and a classical critic's judgment of narratorial trust necessarily identical questions.

Ahmed El Shamsy's 2021 study, [The Ur-Muwaṭṭaʾ and Its Recensions](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/a14wz-9hc46), is an unusually concrete test. He compares Mālik's reports as cited in al-Shāfiʿī's *Kitāb al-Umm* with versions associated with Abū Muṣʿab, Ibn Bukayr and Yaḥyā al-Laythī. The resulting variations can affect wording, accompanying juristic judgments and the relation between author and transmitting student. The recensions are not four completely unrelated books, but neither is the surviving text a perfect, unchanging artifact independently of its lessons and transmission.

The specific report of Ibn ʿUmar's bathing and splashing water into the eyes shows why these distinctions matter. The narrated bodily practice is one datum; Mālik's reported opinion on its necessity or acceptability is another. Different recensions and quotations can preserve the practice with different accompanying comments. Later jurists reasoned about the report's legal force. The question is not whether memory is spiritually inferior to the written word; both must be corrected by disciplined evidence and understanding.

A report is preserved by responsible people, not by a chain diagram existing independently of them. When ʿĀʾishah questioned the Prophet about a seemingly conflicting description of the reckoning ([al-Bukhārī 103](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:103)), accurate transmission depended on noticing a distinction and asking further. Later *isnād* criticism, comparison of recensions and legal interpretation develop genuine intellectual capacities, or *malakāt*, through repeated exercises with teachers. These are authentic modes of inheriting the Prophetic teaching, yet even a fully authenticated sentence leaves further questions about its scope, occasion and the spiritual life of the person who spoke it. No critic or jurist gains a monopoly over the entire Muhammadan reality merely by mastering one necessary discipline.

The Akbarian doctrine of inheritance supplies another distinction. A sound narration preserves something real of the Prophet's acts and teaching, but it does not transmit the entire actual state in which the act arose. The Sufi distinction between works, states and knowledge does not make the report a degraded false shadow: its truth within its proper domain may remain binding. It means that **textual transmission**, **correct practical judgment** and **realized inward formation** are related, nonidentical achievements.

The emergence of large-scale ḥadīth culture required transport, host households, stipends or private means, writing materials, teachers capable of hearing and correcting, and rules concerning how copies were authorized. These factors provide the material and relational mechanism of a civilization-wide learned network. An *ijāza* of hearing a text and the actual intellectual competence to interpret it must still be distinguished. That distinction is an enduring task of education rather than a denial of the value of documentary authority.

The subsequent legal schools inherited this increasingly differentiated evidence while facing new disputes that required more than a trustworthy citation. Their special work was the disciplined derivation and application of judgments.

## 28. Legal schools emerge from the need to decide recurrent cases

**Matn.** A contract could be sound in form but disputed in execution. A husband and wife might disagree about obligations, heirs about shares, traders about deferred payment and neighbours about a boundary. Such cases required knowledge of particular facts and a trained understanding of revealed sources. Across the growing Muslim world, jurists gathered reports, studied local practice, distinguished the force of precedents and formed methods for deriving judgments when no earlier case had exactly the same circumstances.

The circles associated with Abū Ḥanīfah, Mālik, al-Shāfiʿī and Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal developed through students as well as founding teachers. Their methods differed concerning the authority of reports, Medinan practice, analogy, consensus and the treatment of competing evidence. Other Islamic juristic traditions, including Imāmī and Zaydī ones, framed the relation of evidence to rightful religious authority differently. Manuals and commentaries eventually made much of this reasoning teachable beyond the city in which it had first been proposed.

The schools trained jurists to decide whether an act was valid, obligatory, recommended, permitted or forbidden under the relevant conditions. Students had to learn how a general rule applied to the facts of a particular case. Memorizing decisions was useful, but experienced teachers also trained them to weigh evidence and recognize important differences among cases.

Where legal expertise became an office or a professional identity, other dangers appeared: patronage, status rivalry and the temptation to regard technical victory as moral perfection. A jurist could know the law of a debt and still have to learn sincerity, patience and truthful speech toward the parties. Fiqh gave Muslim communities a disciplined way to apply inherited guidance to new disputes. Its practice also raised questions about the conduct and responsibilities of the jurists who exercised such authority.

**Sharḥ.** Fiqh arises because revealed norms address human acts that differ in their objects, evidence and circumstances. A report describing the Prophet ﷺ doing something can be authentic without proving that its performance is always obligatory. A ruling may be generally established without solving the actual classification of a transaction if the parties' agreement and conduct are unclear. The discipline of *uṣūl al-fiqh* arose as part of the effort to make these distinctions defensible.

A disagreement over goods entrusted to an agent shows why jurists must investigate the facts of a particular case. A merchant entrusts a commodity to an agent; the parties later disagree about the agreed price or liability for a loss. The judge must discover who was authorized, what terms were used, which witnesses or records are available, how the applicable rule concerns this kind of agency, and whether custom determines any unstated practice. A revealed prohibition of deception is relevant but does not by itself specify all these facts. Legal intelligence is therefore a **trained discrimination of real cases**, not a device for avoiding the material world.

The four later Sunni madhāhib did not arise as entirely finished systems during their founders' lives. The relation of Mālik to his students' recensions of the *Muwaṭṭaʾ*, as reconstructed by [El Shamsy (2021)](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/a14wz-9hc46), makes this clear. It is possible for a reported action of Ibn ʿUmar to circulate in more than one recension while Mālik's attached evaluation is preserved differently. The student and later jurist must distinguish the actor's conduct, the transmitted wording, the teacher's opinion and a school's eventual doctrinal classification.

Al-Shāfiʿī's *al-Risāla* gives a relatively early articulation of legal evidentiary method, but it is not the complete blueprint for every school's later practice. Abū Ḥanīfah's pupils, Mālikī transmitters, Ḥanbalī report culture and non-Sunni legal traditions require their own actual texts. The historical formation of a school involves seminars, judges, transmitters, patrons, commentaries and the practical authority its positions gain within particular places. A late manual can include substantial development not found verbatim in the founder's teaching.

The critical skill of *taḥqīq al-manāṭ*—in its developed technical usage—provides a useful analytic name for determining whether the conditions of a ruling are present in a particular case. Such competence requires knowledge of law and of relevant facts. The correct application may also depend upon the trustworthy treatment of witnesses or the restraint of a powerful litigant. This is a moral relation in which legal form and ethical character interact without being the same object.

The Sīrah supplies episodes that test the point. In Bukhārī 3475 ([text](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3475)), the Prophet challenges Usāmah's attempted intercession for a woman of standing in a prescribed theft case, rejecting preferential enforcement by rank. The report involves a severe legal penalty and cannot be converted into a feel-good slogan. A jurist must investigate the actual conditions of the penalty, its evidence and jurisdiction; an ethical teacher examines the unequal treatment of rich and poor; a political historian studies who can influence officials. Each perspective addresses real aspects of one event.

The distinction is even sharper with the narrations of Māʿiz and the Ghāmidī woman in Muslim 1695a. Their legal classification concerns confessions and public judgment; the descriptions of repentance, pregnancy, care of a child and correction of spectators disclose more than a tribunal's narrowly evidentiary question. But one cannot determine all the inward states from the legal record, nor can private spiritual intuition override formal standards of evidence.

The Messenger's ruling in a concrete case joins evidence, the meaning of words, knowledge of persons and responsibility before God. Later fiqh rightly isolates the conditions that make a transaction valid or a prescribed sanction applicable; this abstraction may be indispensable to protecting people from arbitrary judgment. Yet the woman of Ghāmid is not reducible to a confession entry: the source also recounts pregnancy, care of an infant and repentance while retaining the severity of the punishment. The Akbarian language of comprehensiveness identifies a limit upon isolated analysis, not a hidden repeal of legal rules. A jurist must still investigate witnesses and circumstances under established methods; a spiritual guide cannot substitute private intuition for public proof. The schools developed by making such differentiations accountable and teachable.

A school of law can therefore be an indispensable institution of the Prophetic inheritance while remaining incapable of reconstructing the Prophet's unique historical person. Its lasting excellence depends on how faithfully it preserves sources, trains the faculty of judgment, respects other forms of knowledge and protects actual persons under its authority. Books, exercises, teachers and courts can form juridical *malakāt*; no one of those alone ensures humility or sincerity.

Legal authority is also contested across Islamic traditions. Imāmī accounts of the Imams and Sunni accounts of juristic reasoning differ in more than professional genealogy; their claims about authoritative guidance must be represented accurately. The larger history of fiqh should not declare these differences dissolved by a later Akbarian synthesis.

## 29. Endowments, colleges and the social life of scholarship

**Matn.** A scholar's knowledge could attract pupils, but a teacher and students still needed shelter, books, food and time. As communities grew and scholars travelled, patrons established mosques, libraries, colleges and residences supported by endowed property. The yields of an estate or market could maintain teaching after the founder's death, while a legal instrument identified beneficiaries and responsibilities. The eleventh-century Niẓāmiyyah of Baghdad became a prominent institution, but it was not the model for all teaching in every later city.

Through *waqf*, donors set aside property whose income would support defined charitable or educational purposes. Its success depended upon sound management and upon teachers who were able to make good use of the support provided. A building might give shelter without transmitting knowledge; a scholar might be an excellent teacher without holding a prestigious office; an appointment could reward expertise or become a prize in conflicts between families.

In Damascus, endowed colleges became spaces not only for lectures but for elite social relationships and ceremonies. Households and private teaching continued alongside them. Medieval colleges developed under particular patterns of patronage and law, rather than reproducing the form of the Prophet's mosque unchanged.

Religious learning became more durable through such material support, but its proper end remained the formation of persons able to understand, transmit and act. A student needed exercises, correction and intellectual honesty; a patron needed fair stewardship; a teacher needed knowledge as well as trustworthiness. The building and endowment might survive even when the quality of teaching changed. The continuing education of students depended upon people as well as property.

**Sharḥ.** An endowed teaching institution can be explained through its property, the people who administer it, its legal rules and the purposes it is intended to serve. Property and its yield constitute a material support; the donor, administrator and workers act to establish and sustain it; a deed sets rules about beneficiaries, uses and responsibilities; the institution's declared purpose may be worship, teaching, charity or another legally recognized good. The fact that an endowment is legally valid does not alone establish that its managers act justly or that its teachers form competent students.

The source-critical history matters. Michael Chamberlain, [Knowledge and Social Practice in Medieval Damascus, 1190–1350](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/knowledge-and-social-practice-in-medieval-damascus-11901350/68A87013550B3C58B97BE438453BAFB9), shows that the proliferation of madrasas involved learned households, elite competition, property, appointments, ceremonies and personal reputations. The school was not necessarily a modern university transplanted into medieval Islam. The claim that such institutions were created only to indoctrinate a centralized state likewise suppresses local uses.

The teacher's relationship to students remained irreducible to a salary. An authorized instructor demonstrates how an argument works, corrects mistakes, recognizes who is ready for greater responsibility and shares a form of professional *adab*. The student who memorizes a manual and the one who can actually answer an unfamiliar case have achieved different kinds of competence. Ibn Khaldūn explicitly describes scientific instruction as a craft whose durable *malaka* exceeds the understanding of a single problem.

The dependence runs in the other direction too. Even an excellent scholar may be unable to provide sustained teaching without time, books or a secure place to meet. A waqf can convert an individual's generosity into a recurring material possibility for others' formation. But its ability to do so depends on revenue, legal enforcement, competent managers and local political conditions. A pious declaration alone does not ensure these conditions.

This social form can also create new temptations. A teaching chair might become a valuable object of competition; a patron might direct its use to secure household prestige; a student might prefer its visible qualification to actual mastery. Such failings do not make the institution inherently corrupt. They demonstrate why the normative end of learning cannot be read directly off the deed or the building.

A useful contrast is the private household in which a learned woman teaches a report or corrects another woman's misunderstanding, as in Bukhārī 314–315. Such instruction can possess genuine authority without having become a formal endowed college. Conversely, an endowed college may support excellent teaching without depending on the hereditary kinship of its founders. The relation between family, teacher, property and public benefit takes different institutional forms.

A madrasa endowed by *waqf* brings together a donor's property, a manager's responsibility, teachers' learning and pupils' work. The deed can establish a legally durable obligation to provide resources; it cannot itself give students *malaka* or ensure the instructor's sincerity. Ibn Khaldūn's account of learned skill as acquired through exercise clarifies one historical mediation, while the Akbarian distinction of knowledge, works and states points to a further limit: a formed spiritual life is not guaranteed by professional qualification. The higher goal of trustworthy understanding does not make rent collection or the selection of competent teachers dispensable. Institutional success must be judged through both reliable supports and what people actually learn and become.

The later colonial history of waqf illustrates how the same form can be reorganized under another political legal system. Chapter 44's case of Abul Fata Mahomed Ishak v. Russomoy Dhur Chowdhry (Privy Council, 1894) involves actual family records, legal classification and appellate authority; it supplies a named example of why the survival of the word *waqf* does not guarantee that all its historical relationships remain unchanged. That later story should not be projected backwards upon medieval Damascus.

The integrity of an educational institution is therefore best examined through two distinct outcomes: the material persistence of its endowment and the actual competencies and virtues formed among its people. Both can be investigated, but neither automatically proves the other. This is the difference between a nominally continuing discipline and a living learned civilization.

## 30. Translation, theological controversy and the uses of reason

**Matn.** Disagreements over divine attributes, human responsibility and the beginning of the world led Muslim theologians to develop distinctive forms of argument. Translation from Greek and Syriac brought philosophical texts into Arabic through the work of particular scribes, physicians, scholars and patrons. Translators and scholars pursued different aims. Physicians interpreted medical texts, theologians argued over the meaning of revelation, and philosophers investigated the principles of nature and existence using their own methods of reasoning.

Al-Kindī and al-Fārābī elaborated philosophical inquiries, while Muʿtazilī, Ashʿarī, Māturīdī and other theologians disputed the meaning and limits of the claims they drew from the Qurʾān. Ibn Sīnā later gave arguments about essence, existence and causation that shaped generations of learned disagreement. During the *miḥnah*, caliphal officials attempted to enforce a particular theological position concerning the Qurʾān. Scholars who resisted challenged the ruler's authority to settle such a dispute through coercion.

Students encountered these questions through translated books, commentary, argument, teacherly correction and repeated exercises. Students learned different standards of evidence in law, medicine, theology and philosophy, and their teachers disputed when the methods of one field could properly be applied to another. Their work contributed to a lively scholarly culture, sustained by translation, disputation, teaching and the criticism of arguments.

**Sharḥ.** The legal and philosophical distinction of the kinds of proof is fundamental to the history of Islamic learning. Al-Fārābī's *Enumeration of the Sciences* classifies logical, linguistic, mathematical, natural, metaphysical, political and legal inquiries under different methods. Ibn Sīnā's *Shifāʾ*, especially its metaphysical books, develops necessity and possibility, essence and existence, and relations of causation. Muslim theologians did not all receive these systems identically: the schools labelled Muʿtazilī, Ashʿarī and Māturīdī changed over time and argued among themselves about agency, justice and speech. Neither a generic “Greek philosophy” nor a single position called “occasionalism” explains all their disputes. The actual propositions, authors and disputed consequences determine what is at stake.

The translation movement offers a well-documented intermediate history rather than an abstract claim that one civilization “borrowed reason.” Dimitri Gutas's *Greek Thought, Arabic Culture* identifies the social and political setting of translations in Abbasid Baghdad. Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq describes comparing and correcting Greek medical manuscripts and producing Syriac or Arabic versions for patrons and practitioners. Translation is thus a series of judgments concerning copies, terms and concepts, not the automatic transfer of an entire intellectual world from one language to another. The ability to make an acceptable technical version must itself be taught.

The same holds for proof. A student can repeat a syllogism without appreciating which premise has not been established, and can memorize a medical definition without recognizing a patient's condition. The discipline of reasoning becomes a *malaka* when a learner exercises it with correction and tests it against relevant evidence. That is a genuine form of human knowledge, not an invalid shadow of spiritual unveiling. Its scope nevertheless remains relative to its object and method.

In the *miḥnah*, caliphal officials required affirmations of the Qurʾān's createdness. The controversy concerned profound theological claims, but the coercive method raised a separate question of who could legitimately bind scholars to a disputed doctrinal formulation. The authority to appoint judges or command a garrison is not simply identical with competence to prove a metaphysical proposition. This is the historical distinction between public coercion and intellectual authority, visible through actual institutions.

Al-Fārābī's classification of sciences and the later debates of al-Rāzī and Ibn Sīnā address claims that have to be judged by their appropriate arguments. A valid proof concerning a natural fact is not invalidated by saying that spiritual realization is higher; an inward experience cannot authenticate a Greek manuscript or settle a syllogism by itself. Qūnawī's distinction of particular tastings under divine Names from a comprehensive standpoint concerns modes of realization within an Akbarian account, not a license to combine contradictory propositions under one sense. This precision makes the wider Muhammadan frame philosophically fruitful: different cognitive powers can be truly ordered without surrendering their disciplines' own criteria. Baghdad's translators and students learned those criteria through work, correction and argument.

Baghdad's scholarly communities developed through people trained to distinguish the kinds of evidence proper to medicine, logic, jurisprudence and spiritual instruction. Their work brought different forms of knowledge into contact without making them interchangeable. A physician must know treatment, a jurist the case and rule, a theologian the force of an argument, and a spiritual guide the limits of what can responsibly be inferred from a person's conduct. Their knowledge can cooperate without any one department becoming a surrogate for the comprehensive Prophet ﷺ.

Sources for continued scrutiny include al-Fārābī's *Iḥṣāʾ al-ʿulūm*, Ibn Sīnā's *Ilāhiyyāt al-Shifāʾ*, al-Ashʿarī's *Maqālāt*, al-Māturīdī's *Kitāb al-Tawḥīd*, Gutas's history, and the existing Gnamma translation of Ibn Turka. Each requires a selected passage and technical context before definitive attribution.

## 31. Al-Ghazālī, al-Rāzī and the remaking of philosophical theology

**Matn.** As theology and philosophy developed, Muslim thinkers examined one another's arguments about the world, divine action and the limits of human knowledge. Al-Ghazālī criticised identifiable arguments associated with the philosophers, including claims about necessity and divine knowledge. He also wrote on jurisprudence and the spiritual life, distinguishing the valid performance of an action from purification of the intentions through which it is performed. Ibn Rushd replied to philosophical objections and defended the competence of demonstration within its field.

In the eastern lands, Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī examined Ibn Sīnā's analyses of essence, existence, definition and knowledge. Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī answered and reformulated many questions in subsequent commentary. Through such works, students read a lemma, objection, response and counter-objection. Students learned to follow an argument through its objections and replies, rather than repeat only a teacher's preferred conclusion.

Disputation could sharpen judgment, but it could also encourage rivalry and pride. Al-Ghazālī and other teachers also asked how knowledge ought to shape the conduct and intentions of the scholar who possessed it. These intellectual traditions continued to disagree while educating successive generations through study, argument and practical discipline.

**Sharḥ.** Al-Ghazālī's *Tahāfut al-falāsifa* attacks twenty identifiable philosophical propositions; some belong to the natural philosophy of causation and others to more fundamental claims about the eternity of the world and God's knowledge. In the seventeenth discussion he challenges the presumption that the customary relation of a cause and its effect must be necessary in the manner asserted by some philosophers. That does not settle all questions about his own view of causal powers. Ibn Rushd's *Tahāfut al-tahāfut* responds to these charges and defends the intelligibility of philosophical argument. Each author's proposition must be assessed within the actual text, not under a caricature of “faith versus reason.”

Al-Ghazālī's *Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn* examines another problem: the human subject whose acts may be valid yet inwardly compromised by vanity or greed. Its quarters concern worship, ordinary practices, destructive traits and saving traits. This is not a denial of law, but a separate inquiry into how a person acting in its presence becomes rightly formed. The jurist's evidence for validity and the spiritual teacher's understanding of intention need not be competitive forms of authority, although they may disagree in particular cases.

In *al-Munqidh min al-ḍalāl*, al-Ghazālī describes a difference between knowing about a spiritual state and tasting (*dhawq*) what it is like. One can give a description of generosity without yet being generous or explain the meaning of reliance without possessing the relevant state. The experiential claim is strong; it does not license a person's unverified spiritual assertion to serve as a mathematical proof or historical document.

Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī's critical work on Ibn Sīnā's *al-Ishārāt* is a concrete case of intellectual formation through disagreement. A question about whether a definition establishes knowledge of an essence outside the mind requires distinguishing conceptual clarity, evidence of actual existence and the structure of demonstration. A reader trained by objections learns to recognize the distance between an intelligible statement and what it proves. This skill is an acquired intellectual disposition; it is not automatically faith or sanctity.

A later teacher's commentary can preserve a debate more accurately than a summary which declares both parties harmonized. Al-Ṭūsī's responses to al-Rāzī also belong to this historically consequential dialogue. Where the commentator introduces an objection hypothetically, attributing it as his settled thesis can distort the entire history of ideas. The manuscript tradition, authorial text and context therefore must govern claims of doctrinal difference.

Al-Ghazālī's distinction between knowing about a spiritual state and *tasting* it asks what kind of person a body of knowledge forms. Al-Rāzī's objections to Ibn Sīnā, and al-Ṭūsī's subsequent replies, address a different task: whether a definition or argument proves what it claims. One may be intellectually competent yet vain, or disciplined in worship yet mistaken in a philosophical demonstration. Within the Akbarian comparison, a comprehensive spiritual standpoint cannot simply invalidate the logical distinctions on which true learning depends. Its relevance lies in the human subject capable of understanding, willing, practising and correcting different kinds of truth. The late scholarly tradition thereby exposes both the value of specialization and the danger of treating any specialized achievement as the entirety of wisdom.

The resulting learned civilization depends on forms of human character that institutions can support without guaranteeing. A lecturer may produce skillful disputants whose ambition corrupts their work; another may teach sound method and honest restraint. Scholarly vocation therefore has a material and social history—books, endowments, colleges, student journeys—and also a formative purpose. Preserving the difference between one discipline's genuine truths and another's legitimate methods is a condition of intellectual integrity.

The precise disagreements can be traced in the seventeenth discussion of al-Ghazālī's *Tahāfut*, Ibn Rushd's reply, the commentaries of al-Rāzī and al-Ṭūsī on the *Ishārāt*, and the account of *dhawq* in *al-Munqidh*. These texts show why the different arguments cannot be adequately represented by a single formula about reason and revelation.

## 32. The work of the heart becomes a teachable discipline

**Matn.** The Qurʾān called human beings to worship with sincerity and warned that outward action could be corrupted by showing off, self-admiration or withholding the rights of others. The Prophet ﷺ formed persons through concrete instruction, companionship, prayer, correction and acts of mercy and justice. Later spiritual teachers gave sustained attention to intention, sincerity and the habits that could undermine apparently correct acts.

Al-Muḥāsibī examined the hidden movements of self-regard and the discipline of self-accounting. Al-Junayd was remembered for sobriety, self-discipline and the measured relation of inward experience to religious obligation. Al-Qushayrī collected teachings about repentance, sincerity, fear, hope, intimacy, states and stations; al-Ghazālī connected the ordinary life of worship, household management and earning with destructive and saving human dispositions.

These teachers did not regard outward duties as dispensable. A person learns patience in encounters where anger arises, honesty in speech where lying would secure advantage, generosity by relinquishing real property, and humility through duties toward actual people. Repeated conduct may develop a stable *malaka*, but discipline alone cannot force every spiritual unveiling.

Over time, guidance on self-examination and spiritual discipline developed into systematic works studied by teachers and pupils. It examined the divine purpose of actions and the defects that externally valid compliance might conceal. Its teachers differed on the meaning of transient states, acquired stations and the reception of knowledge through divine opening; those differences became part of their learned traditions. They looked to the Qurʾān and the Prophet's example while developing methods of instruction suited to new generations.

**Sharḥ.** Qurʾān 91:7–10 joins the soul's creation and moral recognition to the responsibility of purification. Qurʾān 107:4–7 censures ostentatious prayer and withheld ordinary assistance. The first *ḥadīth* of al-Bukhārī identifies intentions as relevant to actions; its historical and juristic uses are more extensive than a formula suggesting only good feelings matter. A worshipper can perform a formally valid act while seeking recognition, and a donation can transfer real goods while revealing a heart attached to public praise. The act's outward conditions and its inward orientation are distinct, though related.

Al-Muḥāsibī's *al-Riʿāya li-ḥuqūq Allāh* is early evidence of a discipline of self-monitoring: a person must attend to the desires and justifications that occur while ostensibly performing good works. Such scrutiny can itself become narcissistic if it is undertaken solely to admire one's refinement. The actual act of correction requires another standard—the divine claim and the real demands of other people—rather than endlessly observing one's own emotions.

Al-Qushayrī's *Risāla* differentiates **aḥwāl** (states, often described as arrivals or gifts not manufactured by deliberate exertion) from **maqāmāt** (stations in which effort, discipline and divine help establish a more durable attainment). Compare his discussions of *ikhlāṣ*, *uns*, *hayba* and *ṣuḥba* (accessible Arabic starting point [Risāla](https://www.mashraba.org/read/qushayri?w=qs%3A13%3A6)). These terms do not mean that any intense transient emotion is a divine state, or that a station is attained solely through exertion independently of grace. Their textual meanings must be checked in context.

A *malaka* is also not interchangeable with a *ḥāl*. Ibn Khaldūn uses the former for durable professional, intellectual and ethical capacities formed through repeated work, while Sufi authors may describe a divine state received without the same causal mechanism. The distinction matters for historical explanation: an educational institution can train memory and practical skill without possessing the power to guarantee *maʿrifa*. Yet the possibility of lived realization does not make such skills spiritually worthless.

Al-Ghazālī's four quarters of the *Iḥyāʾ*—worship, customs of living, destructive qualities and saving qualities—connect this distinction directly to the household and city. A ruler may need to restrain his desire for recognition, a merchant his attachment to gain, a parent his anger, and a teacher his pride. Their spiritual work occurs precisely through the real people, offices and responsibilities with which they must act. The *Iḥyāʾ* is not merely a manual of private emotion; it studies the human subject of public and domestic action.

Al-Mahāʾimī (Gnamma MSH-055, pp. 67–68) contrasts cultivated *takhalluq* with a more profound *taḥaqquq* and distinguishes witnessing unity in multiplicity, multiplicity in unity, and a higher integration of both. This is a later Akbarian metaphysical reading, not one universal definition of virtue used by all Sufis. In particular the more realized person must not be presumed to receive infallible legal or worldly information merely because of an asserted state.

When the Prophet shortened prayer after hearing a child cry ([al-Bukhārī 709](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:709)), he remained engaged in worship while responding to the mother's actual distress. When he served his household and proceeded to prayer at its time ([al-Bukhārī 676](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:676)), neither responsibility abolished the other. The later disciplines of intention, self-examination and spiritual states distinguish features of these acts; al-Muḥāsibī, al-Qushayrī and al-Ghazālī offer different treatments. Their investigations matter because outward compliance can coexist with vanity, and repeated good action may form durable *malakāt*. Yet a claim of realized inward knowledge does not free one from the evidence or rights that govern a public case. Spiritual cultivation returns to conduct among real people.

In the Sufi account of *ṣuḥba*, a learner can meet a formed person whose actual response to circumstances teaches more than the recitation of a proposition. Living correction, affection, truthful rebuke and steady example may help reorient the student's perception and conduct. Claims of *baraka* or a higher spiritual influence add a specifically initiatic interpretation; they are not a measurable automatic transfer of a state, and the spiritual teacher does not acquire permission to violate the believer's rights or revealed obligations.

A civilization's ethical condition cannot be inferred by counting its devotional institutions. Recurrent legal, political and educational practices shape the opportunities for character formation. Their proper purpose depends upon people able to exercise judgment and correct themselves; yet a society also requires institutions that protect rights when some members lack virtue. The personal and structural questions are thus mutually implicated without being reducible to each other.

Al-Muḥāsibī's writings on intention, al-Qushayrī's treatment of states and stations, and al-Ghazālī's books on earning and blameworthy traits all attend to the formation of conduct. Their emphases differ, and the terminology of one cannot simply be attributed to the others.

## 33. From the teacher's company to enduring Sufi networks

**Matn.** Students seeking greater purity of heart accompanied teachers whose worship, speech and everyday conduct they trusted. Through repeated contact, counsel and correction, students learned ways of understanding and acting that were not fully conveyed by written rules alone. Some circles remained informal, while later generations developed recognizable spiritual lineages, lodges and forms of authorization extending across cities and rulers.

The Qādirī, Suhrawardī and Shādhilī traditions grew through different relationships of teaching, service, remembrance and patronage. In the Shādhilī school, Abū al-Ḥasan al-Shādhilī, Abū al-ʿAbbās al-Mursī and Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh articulated a life of trust in God that did not simply require abandoning ordinary work or family. Their teaching encouraged remembrance and reliance on God while people continued to work, raise families and fulfil ordinary duties.

Ibn ʿArabī developed another, partly overlapping question: how prophetic wisdom and the divine Names appear through differentiated spiritual stations. Al-Qūnawī and al-Farghānī developed detailed accounts of prophetic inheritance, spiritual knowledge and the relation between human understanding and the created order. This was not a single doctrine shared without argument by every Sufi community.

These paths transmitted remembered teachings and devotional practices through teachers, pupils and organized communities. Claims of spiritual inheritance and authorization were important, though affiliation alone did not establish a person's degree of realization. Yet an institution that cultivated trust, corrected false claims, sustained worship and enabled sustained formation could contribute to the actual life of religion across changes of political government. The continuing influence of the paths depended upon the actual teaching, worship and conduct of those who sustained them.

**Sharḥ.** The Sufi tradition cannot be reduced to ethical self-management, nor can every claim to a hidden spiritual state be accepted as an independent form of law. Its distinctive question is **how the human being becomes capable of receiving and acting through knowledge of God**—through correct practice, divine gift, self-knowledge and relationships that disclose habitual self-deception. The term *ṣuḥba* includes company, attention, counsel and correction. It can preserve genuine forms of practical knowledge not fully communicable by a detached list of propositions.

The Prophet's ﷺ company cannot be reproduced in totality by one later teacher. In the Akbarian interpretation, the Muhammadan Reality is comprehensive and the spiritually realized heir receives a degree of participation in the Prophet's knowledge, works and states, without receiving independent new law-giving revelation. Chittick's [“The Muhammadan Inheritance”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/horma_0984-2616_1996_num_30_1_1371) describes the triad **works, states and knowledge**. Neither “Sufi = states” nor “jurist = works” is adequate. A legally sound act may arise from sincere contemplation; a spiritual guide must obey law; different forms of competence can be genuinely present in one person.

Ibn ʿArabī's *maqām lā maqām* is sometimes interpreted loosely as moral flexibility. Its meaning is stronger. The realized Muhammadan standpoint is not confined by the demands of any one partial prophetic station; the whole concerns the divine Names and different modes of manifestation, not simply a leader's good temperament. Chittick's discussion of Qūnawī and the [SEP article on Ibn ʿArabī](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ibn-arabi/) offer modern ways of checking its doctrinal context. A particular saint's claim to this degree is not independently verified by a reputation or lineage.

**Transmission has several different forms.** The *isnād* of a narrated prophetic saying claims the passage of particular words through identified people. An *ijāza* can authorize a scholar or disciple in a defined practice or text. A Sufi *silsila* asserts continuity in a spiritual line, sometimes through initiation. Living companionship entails a concrete relationship of instruction and possible transformation. These may intersect but are not equivalent documentary proofs of one uninterrupted teaching event. A lineage chart is not a complete record of conversations, nor evidence that all members possessed the same spiritual degree.

In the Shādhilī environment, Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh's *al-Ḥikam* distinguishes improper attachment to one's own planning from the responsible action of persons whose livelihoods continue. His teachers and the memory of their teaching appear in *Laṭāʾif al-minan*. A disciple may learn reliance while conducting trade or supporting dependants, not simply by withdrawing from work. This is no proof that every later lodge attained such integration; the actual institutions supported hospitality, teaching and remembrance under differing material conditions.

Al-Qushayrī's discussion of *uns* and *hayba* keeps intimacy and awe together. A teacher's close presence can encourage confidence, while the pursuit of spiritual intimacy can be distorted when admiration of the shaykh removes critical judgment or ordinary rights. The claim of an independent infallible human intermediary would contradict the limited character of inherited office. Divine gift is not identical with a transfer of psychic substance from teacher to student; *baraka* belongs to a spiritual interpretive horizon and requires its own theological account.

In the Akbarian line, Qūnawī's *al-Nuṣūṣ* distinguishes *dhawq* under a particular divine Name from comprehensive witnessing. Farghānī's *Muntahā al-madārik*, especially the fourth foundation pp. 103, 110–116, relates the revealed Qurʾān to the Prophet's enacted comprehensive character. Al-Mahāʾimī's *Mashraʿ al-khuṣūṣ* (pp. 67–70) differentiates bodily, legal, luminous and inward prophetic inheritance. These are working Gnamma translations whose Arabic need further witness collation; their claims are a particular metaphysical interpretation and do not establish the biography of every Sufi shaykh.

The history of enduring Sufi networks is material as well as spiritual. A lodge needs a building, food, sources of support and people who maintain it. A traveler needs hospitality; a disciple may move between distant cities through known affiliations; a teacher's practices are sustained by actual gatherings. Work by Alexander Knysh and Devin DeWeese shows the diversity and gradual formation of the order-based structures. Their specialization is not automatically degeneration, but institutional perpetuation can occur without inward knowledge.

The Shādhilī insistence on reliance upon God amid work and the Akbarian distinction between *takhalluq* and *taḥaqquq* address different stages of becoming a trustworthy person. A disciple may learn a correct practice through instruction, develop stable restraint by repetition, and receive a spiritual opening that neither syllabus nor lodge can manufacture. Ibn ʿArabī's three inheritances of works, states and knowledge, summarized by [Chittick](https://www.persee.fr/doc/horma_0984-2616_1996_num_30_1_1371), are not three mutually exclusive guilds. *Ṣuḥba* may help integrate them, while the actual verification of a legal report still belongs to hadith criticism and a patient's condition to appropriate medicine. The Prophet's comprehensive station remains an orienting original, not an office possessed whole by one later shaykh.

As Sufi teaching networks spread, their practices were carried into communities with different languages, livelihoods and forms of government. The higher orientation supplies its final direction; the social forms are contingent and require independent evidence. That balance avoids making the living tradition merely an invisible elite society projected upon an irrelevant material world.

# Book VI — An international civilization with many centres

## 34. Cairo, Damascus and Jerusalem: government, learning and the Crusades

**Matn.** Muslim communities lived under several competing rulers, while scholars, merchants and pilgrims continued to travel between their territories. The Fāṭimids founded Cairo in 969 and established Ismāʿīlī institutions of rule and teaching; Sunni rulers and scholars developed others in Syria, Iraq and neighbouring lands. Seljuk military power entered Baghdad while maintaining a relationship with Abbasid caliphal prestige. Teachers, pilgrims, merchants and Sufi travellers crossed these political boundaries, carrying books and practical disciplines without requiring every city to belong to the same state.

The crusading capture of Jerusalem in 1099 brought violence, displacement and new Latin principalities among diverse Muslim and Eastern Christian populations. Nūr al-Dīn's consolidation of Syrian power and Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn's government of Egypt enabled the forces that recovered Jerusalem in 1187. The recovery depended upon soldiers, revenues, alliances and diplomacy, while Jerusalem's religious significance was central to the commitments of those involved.

In Cairo, Damascus and Jerusalem, mosques, courts, endowments, hospitals and teaching circles formed overlapping orders of authority. A jurist might teach across rulers, a physician serve different patrons, a traveller receive hospitality, and a spiritual guide form students whose political loyalties differed. These cities sustained scholarship and worship despite political division, although their rulers and religious communities sometimes competed over the authority to define and support religious teaching.

**Sharḥ.** In Cairo and Damascus, shared scholarly traditions continued under rulers with different political and religious allegiances. A person seeking Qurʾānic recitation, legal teaching or lodging could find recognized forms of instruction under quite different rulers, while tax regimes and military loyalties changed. This was possible because teachers, texts, recognized methods, households and foundations supplied channels of continuity that did not depend entirely upon one sovereign. None of these channels alone represented the Prophet's comprehensive guidance.

The Fāṭimid Ismāʿīlī teaching institutions, Sunni colleges and different Sufi circles should be investigated according to their actual doctrines and practices, not silently treated as equivalent manifestations of one undivided religious perspective. Their shared urban forms do not eliminate disagreements about the Imam, the caliphate, valid interpretation or authority. Historians can trace buildings and endowments and should state where spiritual claims exceed documentary proof.

Warfare gives the case an additional test. The capture and recovery of Jerusalem were enacted through military force, political coordination and human suffering. No symbolism of sacred space can replace the history of killed or displaced people. The right relationship of a sacred end to the means of protecting or regaining a city is a normative question requiring particular judgments. Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn's success did not confer universal mastery of every science or guarantee the justice of every official under his rule.

The transformation of al-Azhar under different governments and Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn's campaigns around Jerusalem concerned unlike kinds of continuity. A school survives through teachers, pupils, appointments and resources; a campaign proceeds through provisions, alliances and armed force; the sacred worth of Jerusalem is neither an army nor a budget. The comprehensive Prophetic example offers a standard by which these activities may be rightly related without treating Egyptian, Syrian and Crusader institutions as historically interchangeable. Ismāʿīlī and Sunni doctrines of religious authority also differed in substance; sharing urban buildings does not make them versions of the same theology. A civilizational relation exists through actually interacting people, not merely because a map groups several cities under one colour.

Muslim and Christian rulers fought, negotiated truces, exchanged envoys and traded with one another; neither population acted as one undifferentiated political bloc. The Latin principalities interacted with diverse eastern Christian communities and with Muslim dynasties that themselves fought rivals. Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn's military success depended on prior Syrian consolidation, control of Egypt's resources and treaties with local powers. Ibn al-Athīr, Ibn Shaddād and Latin chroniclers preserve accounts of the campaigns from differing political and religious positions. No single court chronicle is a transparent record of all participants.

This case also clarifies what an 'international Islamic society' meant. A traveler passing from Cairo to Damascus encountered different rulers but could find Arabic books, legal teachers, markets, hospitals and spiritual guides across the frontier. Political fragmentation sometimes increased patronage competition. Fāṭimid Ismāʿīlī institutions and Sunni endowments promoted different understandings of religious authority while sharing some urban and administrative forms. The coexistence of disagreement and common social practices needs explanation, not rhetorical celebration. Crusading warfare shaped taxation, fortification, migration and religious preaching; it did not create the scholarly networks from nothing. For orientation consult the *New Cambridge History of Islam*, volumes 2 and 3, and specialist biographies of Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn.

Al-Azhar's institutional history began in Fatimid Cairo and changed under subsequent governments and scholarly communities. Founded in Fatimid Cairo, the mosque became associated with a particular ruling dynasty's religious and educational project; its subsequent life passed through different political and doctrinal settings. It would be inaccurate to describe every later Egyptian scholarly institution as simply a continuation of Fatimid propaganda, yet a building's history can register changes in authority that a list of dynasties conceals. Similarly, military and charitable endowments under Nūr al-Dīn and Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn tied urban property to public aims, while actual teachers and students used those places in diverse ways. The wars shaped what cities could finance and whom they could protect. Endowed institutions financed teaching under differing patrons, just as individual campaigns depended on particular alliances, revenues and military aims.

**Case: al-Azhar across successive confessional governments.** The official al-Azhar history dates its construction to 970–972 under the Fatimids, in a programme explicitly supporting their Ismāʿīlī religious order ([al-Azhar institutional history](https://azhar.eg/en/Uzbekistan-International-Conference/ArtMID/10764/ArticleID/46011/About-Al-Azhar-Mosque)). The same building later supported Sunni scholarly life under different rulers and teaching relationships. A sacred architectural site can survive while the authorities governing its preaching, the doctrines taught in its circles, the persons supported by its endowments and the households sending students all change. A stone building does not preserve a school automatically: it must be inhabited by qualified teachers, financed, and used through repeatable instruction. Nor does a new ruling doctrine erase every older inhabitant's learning overnight. The Akbarian question of integrality here concerns the right relation of authority, valid knowledge, living pedagogy and worship; its answer cannot be inferred from the building's name alone. The case demonstrates **institutional continuity under changing doctrinal and political forms**, not a proof that the various parties believed the same theology.

## 35. Al-Andalus and the Maghrib: cities, scholarship and the loss of a political world

**Matn.** ʿAbd al-Raḥmān I, an Umayyad prince who had escaped the Abbasid overthrow, established his rule in Córdoba in 756. Over subsequent centuries al-Andalus became an important centre of government, agriculture, craft and learning, closely connected with North Africa. Its towns brought Muslims, Christians and Jews into commerce, intellectual contact and relations of unequal political and legal standing. The Cordoban caliphate eventually fractured, and successor kingdoms competed for resources and alliances. Almoravid and Almohad powers connected sections of Iberia and the Maghrib through different combinations of military, religious and scholarly authority.

Judges and teachers carried Mālikī legal learning across the straits; physicians, poets, grammarians, philosophers and Sufis wrote and taught in different urban settings. The works of Ibn Rushd and Ibn ʿArabī arose within this wider western world, though their intellectual and spiritual questions were not identical. The Nasrid kingdom survived at Granada until 1492. After its conquest, restrictions on remaining Muslims deepened toward forced conversion and the expulsion of Moriscos in the early seventeenth century, with grave consequences for families and inherited practices.

As Muslim political power contracted, scholars and families carried books and teaching traditions into North Africa, while others remained under increasing restrictions. These movements could preserve learning but did not undo the losses suffered by displaced communities. Scholarship and devotional life continued in the Maghrib through its own teachers, households and institutions, building on an inheritance that had developed on both sides of the straits.

**Sharḥ.** The political histories of the Umayyad emirate, caliphate, *ṭawāʾif*, Almoravids, Almohads and Nasrid Granada must be kept distinct. Their changes involved revenue, rural production, warfare and diplomacy rather than a single spiritual cycle imposed retrospectively. The historiographical term *convivencia* cannot alone characterize centuries of exchange and coercion. A Jew, Christian or Muslim under different rulers could face importantly different legal and social conditions.

Ibn Rushd's jurisprudence and philosophical demonstrations and Ibn ʿArabī's accounts of spiritual realization developed within related western Islamic worlds, yet their specific arguments cannot be equated by geographical proximity. In Córdoba and Granada, actual judges, scribes, artisans and teachers sustained different forms of competence under changing governments. After 1492, families attempted to preserve worship and learning under the loss of political and legal protection; the costs of forced conversion were real, not steps assumed to be spiritually beneficial. The comprehensive Muhammadan ideal remains intelligible across political change, but no metaphysical analogy establishes the legal rights promised in Granada's capitulations or proves that they were honoured. Archives, lives and doctrines must retain their differences.

When conquest changes the government of a city, the survival of religious life depends upon actual relationships: where families can gather, who has the right to teach, whether a manuscript is carried to another shore, and whether the next generation can practice openly. Persecution and forced conversion can destroy the material conditions of public worship even when some inward conviction persists. It would be morally and historically false to call such losses necessary purifications of the tradition.

For displaced families and scholars, the survival of teaching depended on whether they could settle, find pupils, preserve books and continue practising their religion. A teacher displaced to North Africa may preserve a text and form students there, but this does not restore the lost livelihoods or guarantee that every carried practice survives. The higher meaning of faithful endurance must be related to such real consequences, not substituted for them.

The institutional trajectory is more informative than a rise-and-fall label. Umayyad rule in Córdoba was initially an emirate and only later a caliphate; the caliphal order ended in 1031, after which different *ṭawāʾif* competed for revenues, military support and legitimacy. Almoravid and Almohad movements emerged within North African conditions and intervened in Iberian politics, bringing their own doctrines and ruling practices. The Nasrid regime at Granada persisted for more than two centuries while balancing warfare, diplomacy, taxation and economic relations with its neighbours. María Viguera-Molins describes the collapse of the Cordoban caliphate in 1031 and the subsequent competing kingdoms, while archaeological studies document the continuing urban and agricultural life of al-Andalus.

Learning followed related but distinct pathways. The *Muwaṭṭaʾ* circulated in versions associated with western transmitters; Mālikī jurists acquired durable authority in the Maghrib and al-Andalus. Scholars could write Arabic commentaries and teach across the straits even when the regions were politically divided. Ibn Rushd's philosophical and legal writings belong to this western history, while Ibn ʿArabī's early life in al-Andalus and later movement eastward illustrates a transregional scholarly-spiritual trajectory. Ibn Rushd and Ibn ʿArabī pursued distinct philosophical and spiritual projects within the changing political and scholarly conditions of al-Andalus.

Christian, Jewish and Muslim relations varied across cities, institutions and centuries. The concept *convivencia* is a subject of historiographical debate, not a license to erase coercion or shared daily life. The forced conversions imposed after Granada and the expulsion of Moriscos during 1609–14 are historically distinct from the conquest of 1492 itself. Communities and books displaced toward the Maghrib could survive political collapse, yet the material cost to families was severe. Archaeology, local documents, Muslim and Christian chronicles and scholarly biographies preserve different evidence for political change, religious practice and learning in the western Mediterranean.

**Case: the 1491 Granada capitulations and their later breach.** The surrender terms agreed in November 1491 included provisions for Muslims' property, religious life and adjudication according to their laws. After Granada's surrender in January 1492, coercive policies intensified by the end of the decade, and conversion measures in 1499–1502 transformed the previously promised conditions ([archival description of the capitulations](https://historylab.es/rolled-privilege-of-the-capitulations-for-the-surrender-of-the-city-of-granada-to-the-catholic-monarchs-1491/)). A legal permission recorded in a treaty is not the same as the capacity of a family actually to teach, pray, own property and transmit faith when the enforcing regime changes. The moral character of the affected people cannot be deduced from the state's success or their outward compliance under compulsion. Here the material powers of courts, parish authorities and arms changed the conditions of inward and communal transmission. The episode demonstrates how **the language of a promised right may survive in a document after the institutional relationships necessary to exercise that right have been damaged**. It does not establish that every local experience followed precisely one chronology.

## 36. Persianate language, commerce and the circulation of books

**Matn.** Arabic remained the language of the Qurʾān and a major medium of religious scholarship, while Persian became increasingly important for administration, narrative, ethical instruction, poetry and learned exchange across Iran, Central Asia, Anatolia and India. Students often moved between Arabic technical texts, Persian explanation and the languages used within their own homes. Courts cultivated poetry and histories; merchants travelled roads and ports; spiritual teachers, secretaries, scribes and jurists carried books across political frontiers.

Books were translated, copied and explained by teachers before readers in other regions could make full use of them. The translation of a technical term could clarify an argument or lead readers to misunderstand it. Teachers often supplied explanations for students learning through a language different from that of the original work. Persianate literature could cultivate moral attention and devotional imagination, though not every poem or court practice had the same religious end.

Arabic religious texts reached new audiences through Persian explanation and translation. Students could recite the Qurʾān in Arabic, study scholarly explanations in Persian and discuss religious matters in the languages of their households. Translators and teachers had to preserve technical distinctions while making texts accessible to people trained in other languages.

**Sharḥ.** Arabic, Persian and local vernaculars carried different kinds of learning across Muslim societies. Arabic remained central to Qurʾānic recitation and many formal disciplines. Persian became an important language of administration, historical narrative, ethical literature and theological or Sufi explanation. A student might learn an Arabic lemma, discuss its meaning in Persian and apply it in a household that spoke another language. Such mediation depended upon teachers competent in more than one textual tradition.

Marshall Hodgson distinguished *Islam* as a religion from the broader historical field he called *Islamicate*. Shahab Ahmed later challenged accounts that effectively exclude poetry, philosophy or forms of cultural life from the ways Muslims themselves understood and practiced Islam. The categories can illuminate different scholarly questions, but they should not determine in advance whether a particular poem or philosophical claim agreed with another author's religious commitments. Authors' words, practices and disagreements remain the evidence.

A book's movement between regions must be demonstrated rather than imagined. Colophons, dated copies, ownership marks, reading certificates, quotations and library inventories can sometimes identify people who possessed or taught a text. Shared vocabulary alone does not establish that one writer read another. The same discipline could travel through a copied Arabic work, a Persian paraphrase, oral explanation or a later vernacular adaptation; each path leaves different historical traces.

A reader of an Arabic juristic text through Persian explanation can correctly grasp a ruling without sharing the spiritual experience of its author; another might learn a Persian ethical poem by heart while misunderstanding its contested metaphysical claims. Qūnawī's attention to restricted standpoints and realization permits a later interpretation of these differences without downgrading competent translation. Dārā Shukoh's 1657 *Sirr-i akbar* offers a sharper test: Sanskrit specialists, Persian translators and a Mughal patron brought texts together under one contested interpretive programme. The spiritual affinity Dārā asserted cannot replace philology or establish that every participant agreed with him. Books travel when particular people copy, translate, teach or carry them, and those routes remain subject to documentary proof.

**Dārā Shukoh and the Upaniṣads.** The Mughal prince sponsored a Persian translation of Sanskrit Upaniṣadic texts completed in 1657 under the title *Sirr-i akbar*. The undertaking involved Sanskrit-knowing scholars, Persian literary workers, patrons and decisions about technical ideas. Dārā read the texts through a universalizing spiritual framework and associated them with a hidden scriptural wisdom. That identification was his contested interpretation; it is not a conclusion authorized by the Qurʾān or accepted by every Akbarian commentator. See the [Oxford study of Dārā's Vedāntic interests](https://academic.oup.com/book/25385/chapter-abstract/192483329) and [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/dara-sokoh/). The case joins intellectual comparison to a documented translation enterprise whose claims require separate evaluation.

## 37. Mongol conquest, conversion and the survival of scholarly worlds

**Matn.** Mongol armies devastated cities and displaced communities across Central and Western Asia in the thirteenth century. Baghdad fell in 1258 and its ruling Abbasid court ended. Yet scholars, families, traders and institutions elsewhere continued to carry texts and teach. Cairo and Damascus remained major centres, while new governments in Iran and Central Asia needed local fiscal and administrative expertise to maintain control over populations whose practices did not vanish with earlier dynasties.

Mongol rulers supported different religions and made differing choices concerning Islam. Aḥmad Tegüder had ruled as a Muslim Ilkhan before Ghāzān's conversion and accession in 1295 brought a more enduring public Islamic orientation to Ilkhanid rule. Scribes, scholars, officials and patrons contributed to changing courtly and communal institutions. Rebuilding could renew foundations and learning, while the destructive losses of war remained real.

Religious learning survived where teachers, students, books and the resources needed to support them survived. Communities rebuilt institutions and continued worship under rulers whose policies differed from those of earlier dynasties. The ruler's conversion influenced patronage and public religious policy, but it did not mean that all the people under his authority had converted at once. The new administrations drew upon scholars, scribes and institutions inherited from the communities they had conquered.

**Sharḥ.** Political conversion and the formation of a religious community are not one event. Ghāzān's public Islamic identity can be dated in relation to political acts and patronage; the spiritual convictions of particular households cannot be inferred simply from royal ritual. Earlier Muslim members of Mongol dynasties and different local populations complicate any story of a single moment at which “the Mongols became Muslim.” The archives of the Ilkhanids, Mamluks and later historians require comparison across political interests.

Aḥmad Tegüder's earlier Islam and Ghāzān's adoption of Islam in 1295 warn against treating a dynasty as a single spiritual subject. Court policy could change through the ruler and his advisers; schools, books, *waqfs* and local households could also survive, fail or transform at different rates. The Akbarian idea of comprehensive order does not erase the losses caused by conquest or turn destruction into a necessary purification. A scholar who kept teaching after losing a patron preserved one part of a wider inheritance through actual human effort. Administrative decrees, manuscript transmission and inward conviction belong to different kinds of evidence, and the continuity of religion must be tested through each.

Mongol conquests caused immense material loss and human suffering. Later metaphysical accounts of spiritual return do not explain the military causes of these events or make the destruction a necessary condition of religious renewal. Perpetrators and victims retain their moral claims. The principle of continuity under change concerns the possibility that truth can be transmitted by persons and practices despite a destroyed political centre, not a guarantee that loss is ultimately beneficial to those who suffered it.

Ibn Khaldūn's model of conqueror solidarity and subsequent settlement provides a useful general comparison but cannot establish the local mechanisms without independent documents. The precise relationships of a Persian administrator, a Mongol military household, a Sufi guide and a scholar in Cairo explain more than a general contrast between nomadic force and urban refinement.

The difference between a dynasty's conversion and a population's religious transformation is essential. Peter Jackson's *The Mongols and the Islamic World* and the *Encyclopaedia Iranica* account of the Mongols trace the varied trajectories of the Ilkhanids, Jochids and Central Asian khanates. Aḥmad Tegüder had already ruled as a Muslim Ilkhan; Ghāzān's accession later established a more durable public Islamic orientation at the court. Rashīd al-Dīn wrote under Ilkhanid patronage, while Mamluk and other chroniclers recorded the same conflicts through different political allegiances. Motives given to a conqueror by an official chronicler are evidence for political representation as much as for private conviction.

The mechanism of institutional persistence is visible in Cairo and Iran. A *waqf* could support a school across changes of dynasty, but endowments might be ruined or reallocated; a jurist's students could copy his work even when his patron was defeated. Mongol administrators also adopted Persian fiscal and literary practices for practical reasons. An Ibn Khaldūnian model can illuminate the transition from conqueror solidarity to the difficulties of administering settled societies, while local documents decide how far the analogy holds. After the Mongol wars, scholars continued to produce works in theology, logic, astronomy and Sufi thought, although warfare altered their centres, resources and patrons.

A later chronicler who says that Ghāzān's accession brought Islam to the Ilkhanate is reporting a major change in public royal identity, not the conversion of everyone under his rule. Aḥmad Tegüder and Muslim Mongol soldiers preceded Ghāzān's accession and therefore demonstrate earlier Islamic affiliations within Ilkhanid society. Later Mongol rulers' patronage of scholars, foundations and monuments also served political purposes, but religious conviction cannot be disproved merely by identifying those interests. The contrasting Mamluk and Ilkhanid reports should therefore be compared as partisan accounts of a contested transformation. Courts, colleges, manuscripts and commercial routes carried learned and devotional practices through changing dynastic conditions. Mongol campaigns devastated cities and scholarly households, while surviving people and institutions later rebuilt teaching and textual circulation under new rulers.

**Case: Ghāzān's conversion in 1295.** The Ilkhan Ghāzān's adoption of Islam occurred during conflict with Bāydū; the Muslim commander Nawrūz, related by marriage to Ghāzān, was a significant actor in the episode. Official narratives, including Rashīd al-Dīn, associate conversion with changing political allegiance; later Mamluk reports and modern scholars give differing emphases ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gazan-khan-mahmud/); [Peter Jackson on royal conversion](https://doi.org/10.12987/yale/9780300125337.003.0014)). The small-scale encounter between a ruler and a politically capable adherent acquired consequences for patronage, courtly symbols and the religious privileges of communities in a wide empire. Yet the royal announcement does not independently prove the inward state of Ghāzān, the sincerity of every supporter or the conversion of each subject. The case illustrates how an individual decision may acquire large-scale consequences through military networks, jurisdiction and institutions **without reducing the decision to those consequences**. The reported conversion should be narrated without retrospective claims that all Mongol practices vanished instantaneously.

## 38. The Middle Niger: markets, kings and independent scholars

**Matn.** Routes crossing the Sahara linked the Middle Niger with North African markets. Merchants moved salt, metals, textiles and other goods through settlements whose rulers and households did not share one form of religious life. Muslim traders, literate officials and teachers could live at courts where many subjects continued different practices. Mansa Mūsā's pilgrimage in 1324 brought Mali's wealth and connections to the attention of people in other parts of the Muslim world. Religious practice within the kingdom continued to vary among its towns and rural communities.

Under later Songhay rule, Timbuktu and Jenne supported merchants, jurists, teachers and families who collected and copied books. Scholarship required more than a ruler's patronage. Students learned from named authorities, debated cases arising from local marriages, property and exchange, and preserved memories of teachers whose credibility did not depend wholly upon office. Writers such as Aḥmad Bābā participated in wider Mālikī discussions while responding to the histories of their own towns.

Scholars in the Middle Niger studied works also taught in North Africa while developing their own learned networks and responses to local questions. Local languages, commerce, learned households and relations with rulers gave inherited disciplines distinctive forms. Spiritual guidance could be received through actual teaching and companionship while political authority remained contested. Merchants, scholars, rulers and families each contributed to the religious life of the region through commerce, teaching, public patronage and household practice.

**Sharḥ.** Muslim scholars in the Middle Niger taught Mālikī law and other inherited disciplines in local settings that differed from the colleges and courts of Cairo. A student in Timbuktu could study Mālikī law and Arabic grammar within familial teaching relations and local patterns of commerce very different from those of a Cairo college. Historical evidence must identify the actual teacher, book, ownership record or surviving biography. A library catalogue documents material survival and circulation; it does not reveal the spiritual station of its owner.

The merchant's place is equally important. Trust in credit, shelter along a journey and repeated exchange can make a town able to sustain scribes and teachers, but commercial contact does not automatically produce conversion or a spiritual discipline. Such changes require people who teach, listen, imitate and acquire recognizable habits. Royal patronage, meanwhile, can support a mosque or court and can also threaten scholarly independence when public judgment opposes powerful interests.

Aḥmad Bābā's forced displacement following Moroccan conquest demonstrates why an Islamic civilization cannot be defined by whichever government holds Timbuktu. His learning could still be exercised through people and books, while the loss of a library or teaching circle harmed families and pupils. A merchant's trustworthiness, a jurist's judgment and a teacher's companionship can support the same religious life without each containing its totality. The Akbarian account of comprehensiveness allows them to be ordered toward an ultimate good, not to claim that all the city's residents shared one achieved spiritual station. The surviving manuscripts and biographies tell different parts of that history and cannot be combined into an imagined continuous eyewitness chronicle.

The evidence remains uneven. The *Tārīkh al-Sūdān*, travellers' narratives, scholarly biographical writings and archaeological materials must not be flattened into one chronology. Manuscript collections vary widely in age and provenance, so the historical claim is local and document-dependent: real African communities produced and transmitted Islamic learning in their own distinct social settings.

The *Tārīkh al-Sūdān*, learned biographies, manuscripts, archaeological evidence and reports by travellers such as Ibn Baṭṭūṭah preserve different aspects of the Middle Niger's history. Zachary Wright traces West African scholarly authority through teaching communities and inherited learned relationships, which did not depend wholly on appointment by royal courts. Northwestern University's West African Arabic manuscript collections and catalogues also make local textual production accessible.

Timbuktu supported learned families, libraries and teaching circles whose organization differed from modern universities; surviving manuscript inventories vary greatly in age and provenance. The age, provenance and content of particular libraries need separate examination. A study of 31 private libraries found variation in the age and provenance of collections, limiting claims of uninterrupted scholarly transmission across every household. The surviving libraries preserve evidence of local scholarship but differ in the periods and networks they document. Trade supported towns and literacy; rulers patronized institutions; scholars organized teaching and defended authority within those changing conditions. West African scholars used Arabic and Mālikī learning within local commercial, political and educational arrangements, adapting inherited disciplines to their own institutions.

Aḥmad Bābā (d. 1627) wrote on Mālikī learning and scholarly biographies, and his career was disrupted by Moroccan intervention in Timbuktu. His works on Mālikī scholarship and learned biographies belong to a world that maintained ties with North Africa; his career was also affected by Moroccan intervention in Songhay and the removal of scholars from Timbuktu. Aḥmad Bābā's scholarly career and forced removal after Moroccan intervention connected the independence of learned households with struggles over political power. The surviving works document learned circles and libraries in particular towns; they do not establish Cairo-style colleges everywhere or a uniform date for the manuscript collections. Their ownership and contents vary. Dated manuscripts and biographical records document particular West African scholars and libraries without establishing identical educational institutions in every town.

**Case: Aḥmad Bābā and the Moroccan seizure of Timbuktu.** The scholarly networks of the Middle Niger were disrupted by the Moroccan invasion beginning in 1591. Aḥmad Bābā, a jurist and prolific author, was removed with members of his family to Morocco, where his intellectual life continued under constrained political circumstances; his personal manuscript collection suffered losses ([University of Cape Town Tombouctou Manuscripts Project](https://tombouctoumanuscripts.uct.ac.za/overview/timbuktus-history)). The episode puts concrete people between a widely used learned tradition and political force. A teacher's competence can travel and be exercised under new conditions; the material books and relationships that sustained it can nevertheless be destroyed or separated. Neither his knowledge nor the suffering of exiled families can be adequately measured by the preservation of one library's surviving titles. This is a historically grounded instance of **intellectual continuity that is neither institutionally invulnerable nor identical with dynastic sovereignty**. Numbers and exact dates in later biographies require source-by-source qualification.

## 39. Across the monsoon seas: ports, households and the Malay world

**Matn.** Merchants and sailors travelled the Indian Ocean by the seasonal winds, crossing between East Africa, the Arabian shores, India and Southeast Asia. Traders of different religions shared ports where lodging, credit, weights, protection and trusted interpreters could affect the success of a voyage. Some travellers remained in settlements, formed households through marriage and established places of worship and instruction. Children born into these communities learned local languages and participated in maritime economies without ceasing to belong to wider religious networks.

By the fifteenth century Melaka had become a major port whose rulers governed resident and temporary merchants through harbour arrangements, customs and protection. Its commercial life involved Chinese, Indian and island Southeast Asian networks as well as Muslim ones. Malay religious instruction and literature developed through locally teachable forms, including Arabic-derived writing, learned travel and the work of translators and jurists. The date of particular rulers' conversions and the sequence of local changes remain disputed.

Muslim communities grew in different ports through trade, settlement, marriage and the work of religious teachers. Some trading relationships developed into enduring settlements where families supported mosques and the teaching of children. The adoption of Islam by a court did not mean every resident shared the same beliefs or practices. Local languages and institutions influenced how people studied and lived their religion.

**Sharḥ.** In Melaka, the *syahbandar* and harbour customs help identify the practical causes that brought different people into sustained contact. A visiting merchant needed protection and predictable dealings; a resident family needed continuity through seasons when ships departed; a teacher needed a language in which students could understand instruction. These functions could support one another without any one being reducible to the others. Historical research by Kenneth Hall and A. C. S. Peacock distinguishes harbour administration, trade, Arabic and Persian knowledge and local Southeast Asian sources.

Some port merchants settled with families, supported places of prayer and arranged instruction for their children. Teachers and scribes helped preserve these practices beyond the duration of an individual trading voyage. Every arrow requires real history. It is a hypothesis, not a claim that every documented Malay community developed by an identical sequence.

The *syahbandar* officials of Melaka attended to traders whose languages, business relations and places of origin differed. Their work made passage and dispute settlement more predictable; it did not itself convert visiting merchants. Muslim families and teachers later sustained prayer, instruction and textual learning among particular port communities. These practices could belong to a common religious orientation without requiring one uniform cultural form. A juridical ruling concerning exchange, a merchant's practical integrity and a teacher's cultivation of students are legitimate partial inheritances, not competing claims to be the whole Prophet. The chronology of royal conversion remains disputed and must be reconstructed independently from dated sources rather than inferred from port prosperity.

The precise conversion chronology of early Malay rulers remains disputed. Written Malay, Arabic, Persian and Portuguese accounts are not one transparent collective witness. Source evaluation must preserve distinct time periods and the difference between a court's public Islamic identity and the acquisition of religious practice by persons throughout the port.

Kenneth Hall's study of the eastern Indian Ocean documents Melaka's warehouses, resident merchants, harbour offices and rules for customs and commercial protection. A. C. S. Peacock's 2024 study adds Arabic, Persian and Turkish accounts, inscriptions and navigational sources that connect the port to wider Muslim geographical knowledge. These witnesses do not settle the exact conversion dates of early rulers; the evidence for that sequence is famously inconsistent, as Christopher Wake noted. Muslim, Hindu, Buddhist and other merchant communities participated in Indian Ocean commerce; Muslim merchants did not monopolize its ports.

Indian Ocean port communities linked mercantile trust, resident families, worship and instruction through sustained settlement. Trust among commercial partners is not identical to faith, but people who lived together could build prayer spaces, commission teachers and sustain scholarly ties. A port administration that protected foreigners did not by that fact produce Muslim conviction; it furnished conditions in which a community could reproduce itself. Malay literary and legal manuscripts record Islamic teaching and court practice in forms that differed among ports and ruling houses. The transition from an Arabic book to Jawi instruction was likewise mediated by language, translators, scribes and patrons. Local rulers, merchants, scribes and teachers shaped how Islamic practice entered Malay institutions and vernacular texts.

Melaka's *syahbandar* officers, customs regulations and arrangements for visiting merchants make an excellent causal case. They helped reduce uncertainty about lodging, weighing, protection and market transactions for temporary residents. Some of these residents formed more durable households and communities in which religious learning could continue between trading seasons. Harbour privileges supported trade and settlement, but dated inscriptions, legal texts and local chronicles give differing evidence for the conversion of particular rulers and communities. Peacock's Arabic and Persian material locates Melaka within broader Muslim geographical knowledge, while Malay and Portuguese accounts supply different perspectives. Holding these kinds of evidence apart prevents commerce from being treated either as proof of spiritual transmission or as an explanation that makes spiritual teaching unnecessary.

**Case: Melaka's merchant-specific harbour masters.** Accounts associated with Tomé Pires describe four *syahbandars* administering relations with merchants arriving from differing commercial regions: Gujarati traders, visitors around the Bay of Bengal, island Southeast Asian groups and Chinese/Ryukyuan networks ([scholarly reconstruction](https://www.macaudata.mo/showbook?bno=b000614); [Brill chapter](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004407671/BP000014.pdf)). The official did not simply proclaim one universal rule to an undifferentiated population. Practical security, translation, lodging and dispute management required knowledge of the particular commercial communities whose affairs he coordinated. A merchant's trust and repeated contract developed within an institutional arrangement not identical to any one person's piety. This supplies an excellent real-world analogy for **unity through correctly understood differentiation**: common port order could coexist with varying languages and customs. The spiritual legitimacy of any trader or ruler cannot be inferred from their place in that administration, and the original harbour records require closer study.

## 40. Bengal: fields, settlements and changing religious communities

**Matn.** In the eastern Bengal delta, waterways shifted while cultivators cleared fields and established new settlements. The spread of Muslim communities did not simply reproduce the path of earlier military conquest. Land grants, new village institutions, locally remembered religious figures and expanding agrarian work often moved together in particular districts. Religious teachers and their households sometimes became associated with new settlements, where mosques, shrines and inherited practices developed over time.

In the fifteenth century, the settlements and religious foundations associated with Khān Jahān at Bagerhat stood amid the work of cultivating the Bengal delta. His tomb inscription provides a dated witness; later traditions credit him with additional work on roads, water and settlement, but their details cannot all be treated as contemporary records. Elsewhere, grants associated with religious teachers could support worship or instruction while also affecting landholding, cultivation and local authority.

Over generations, prayer, family custom, religious education and local memory acquired recognizable continuities among communities living far from the earliest centres of Muslim political power. Households carried the practices learned from parents and teachers into succeeding generations, while changes in waterways, cultivation and landholding shaped their settlements. Later communities remembered some founders as saints. Inscriptions document parts of their historical activity, while local traditions sometimes preserve details first recorded much later.

**Sharḥ.** In eastern Bengal, political expansion, cultivation and the spread of religious communities took place through different but sometimes intersecting processes. Richard Eaton's [*The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier, 1204–1760*](https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/the-rise-of-islam-and-the-bengal-frontier-1204-1760.html) draws on inscriptions, dated foundations, land records and literary traditions to distinguish these processes. A grant can identify rights over land without demonstrating the beliefs of every person who later cultivated it. A later story about a saint may record an enduring local memory without establishing that every detail occurred in the century attributed to him.

**Khān Jahān and Bagerhat.** The inscription on Khān Jahān's tomb provides a dated anchor for a figure who died in 1459. Local accounts collected centuries later credit him with forest clearance, waterworks, roads, settlement and mosques. These reports cannot all be treated as contemporary eyewitness testimony. The surviving structures, settlement history and remembered patronage nevertheless make Bagerhat an important case for investigating how cultivation, resources, religious foundations and local authority could develop together.

The establishment of a village required practical knowledge of soils, drainage and water as well as labour, food, protection and agreements about land. A mosque and its supporting property could contribute to a settlement by providing a recurring place for worship and teaching. A religious teacher might gain followers through instruction and conduct, but association with a grant or shrine cannot itself prove the inward conversion of a whole district. Similar institutions could function differently in neighbouring places.

At Bagerhat, the dated tomb inscription of Khān Jahān and the later memories of waterworks, roads and settlements furnish different kinds of historical testimony. Drainage and cultivation made habitation possible; grants, patrons and teachers could sustain worship and learning; families could transmit practices to new generations. The relation of all these actions to the worship of God is a genuine question of their purposes and conduct, but the Akbarian account of the divine Names cannot supply a missing land deed or a farmer's private conviction. The comprehensive human vocation is realized, if at all, through knowledge of created conditions and just dealings with others, not by ignoring mud, rain, labour and the rights of cultivators.

## 41. The Ottoman settlement: courts, colleges and a growing imperial household

**Matn.** Ottoman rulers built an empire from an Anatolian principality, with Constantinople becoming the imperial capital after its conquest in 1453. Soldiers, tax officials, palace households and local intermediaries supported authority across the Balkans, Anatolia, the Arab provinces and the eastern Mediterranean. Mosques, courts, colleges and charitable endowments provided recurrent settings for worship, teaching and service among communities of different languages and religious affiliations.

Judges learned Ḥanafī legal doctrine while working alongside sultanic regulations concerning revenue and public administration. In a town, the court heard litigants whose disputes involved actual kin, debts, witnesses, trade and customary practices; a centrally issued order could address a different set of administrative concerns. Scholars gained office through increasingly organized educational appointments, but learned authority also persisted through households, private circles and Sufi networks that no court could fully control.

The empire supported courts, mosques and schools across a vast territory, but these institutions did different kinds of work and held distinct forms of authority. Judges settled cases, officials collected revenues, teachers trained students and Sufi shaykhs guided their followers. Their responsibilities sometimes overlapped, leading to cooperation as well as disputes over authority. Local worship and instruction continued through families and teachers as well as imperial offices. The relationship among courts, scholars and religious communities differed from province to province.

**Sharḥ.** Ottoman towns contained courts, fiscal offices, colleges, mosques and Sufi lodges, each with its own responsibilities and sources of authority. A qāḍī's court, the *ʿilmiyye*, fiscal officers, Sufi lodges and family schools possessed different kinds of authority. Their relative integration was a historical achievement dependent upon appointments, training, documents, witnesses and particular rulers; it was not automatically secured by pronouncing the sultan the spiritual axis of society.

A court record preserves persons presenting specific claims and a judge's procedure; a sultanic *qānūn* shows a ruler's intended regulation. The two do not necessarily coincide in daily practice. Similarly, a madrasa credential attests a particular mode of scholarly achievement and authorization, not the possession of universal spiritual realization. Conversely, a shaykh's spiritual authority should not be treated as competence to set every tax or decide every title dispute.

In Ottoman Aintab, the case of Haciye Sabah's religious teaching and punishment by a court forces a distinction between preserving legal procedure and judging the justice of its operation. A court could record allegations and enforce a ruling; the woman and her pupils had a different understanding of the learning taking place in a household. The historical record cannot disclose every person's inner motives, yet it can record the judge's use of public power. The prophetic inheritance involves both the authority of valid rules and the obligations of knowledge, fairness and restraint toward persons; no single office guarantees their complete integration. The Akbarian comparison offers a norm of comprehensive right judgment, not retrospective endorsement of Ottoman punishment.

Changes over centuries matter: the full sixteenth-century hierarchy should not be retrojected upon early fourteenth-century Anatolia. Annexation of older Arab scholarly regions brought further forms of local authority into the empire. The very possibility of common religious practice amid differing provincial arrangements is evidence against equating Muhammadan civilizational unity with one unchanging administrative form.

The Ottoman *ʿilmiyye* offered a relatively structured hierarchy of appointments but cannot be equated with a single centralized clergy. Gilles Veinstein's chapter in *The Cambridge History of Turkey* documents how judges, mosques, Friday sermons, colleges and pious foundations became part of Ottoman governance from its early period, while scholars were originally recruited from other regions as well. Later institutions changed over time; do not project the sixteenth-century hierarchy back onto the fourteenth century.

A judge in an Ottoman town faced questions raised by actual families and markets, while sultanic *qānūn* regulated matters such as tax and administration. The relationship between juristic tradition and imperial rule varied by subject and province; a law code is not evidence that everyone obeyed it. Endowments supported services but also elite households. Sufi lodges and the pilgrimage connected people across frontiers, including Arab provinces with established scholarly traditions. Rulers patronized colleges and courts, but jurists, household teachers and spiritual lineages retained forms of learning that governments did not wholly control. Ordinary subjects encountered the empire through courts, markets, taxes, village administration and worship, not solely through the decrees of Constantinople.

An Ottoman court register provides a different view of the state than an imperial legal code. In a register, litigants present claims about inheritance, debts or property, and the qāḍī records settlements within a social world in which witnesses, custom and political order matter. The imperial law code reveals how the ruler intended to regulate some activities. These documents may agree or differ without either being irrelevant. A madrasa certificate, meanwhile, records a different kind of authority, that of learned transmission. The empire's durability required all three—imperial command, local adjudication and teaching—but their relations varied. Ottoman court records, the *ʿilmiyye* hierarchy, endowments and regional Sufi institutions document distinct and sometimes competing forms of authority.

**Case: Haciye Sabah's teaching in Aintab, 1541.** Leslie Peirce's study of an Ottoman provincial court identifies Haciye Sabah, a woman who led religious instruction in her home. In July 1541 the judge ordered public humiliation and banishment after the court found that she had admitted male instructors to classes for women ([Peirce, *Morality Tales*, chapter 6](https://doi.org/10.1525/california/9780520228900.003.0012)). The account is not a simple example of just judicial procedure: it concerns contested gender expectations, real educational activity and punitive use of public authority. The woman and her students occupied a small teaching relationship whose status became a larger legal and political question. Preserving the official record does not imply endorsing its punishment. This case makes visible the way that **household learning, gendered authority and imperial court procedure** actually constituted one another, rather than assuming that each domain exists separately. The complete court register remains necessary to establish Sabah's exact words and intentions; neither is independently attested here.

## 42. Safavid Iran and Mughal India: rival confessional and imperial settlements

**Matn.** In early modern Iran the Safavid rulers made Twelver Shiʿism a defining public feature of their government, supported learned specialists and sought to alter the religious organization of communities whose practices had previously varied. The process took generations and occurred differently across provinces. Scholars arriving from established Imāmī centres taught law and doctrine, while political power, local institutions and religious attachments shaped the reception of their teaching.

Mughal rulers governed across India through military power, revenue arrangements, courtly Persian and negotiation with regional elites of diverse religions and languages. Akbar cultivated forms of discussion and imperial inclusion; later policies, including those under Aurangzeb, differed, with the compilation of major Ḥanafī legal materials among their learned projects. Jurists, philosophers, Sufi teachers, poets and learned households circulated works whose authority did not always coincide with a ruler's preference.

The Safavid and Mughal empires developed different policies toward religious authority and the communities they governed. Safavid confessional policy, Mughal courtly discussion and Ottoman judicial institutions involved different relations between public power and religious scholarship. Religious teachers could benefit from imperial patronage while disagreeing with the policies of their rulers. Courts and officials governed public affairs, while families, scholars and spiritual guides continued to transmit religion in varied local settings.

**Sharḥ.** The Safavid adoption of Shiʿi identity must be differentiated from the gradual formation of local institutions and personal practice. A ruler can order public symbols and sponsor teachers, yet the inward allegiance of families changes through schooling, marriage, liturgy, instruction and sometimes coercion over time. Twelver Shiʿi claims about the Imamate are not merely political preferences and must be represented in their own theological terms, while differing from Sunni and Akbarian accounts of *wilāya*.

The Mughal case demonstrates further plurality. Akbar's discussions and court policies were not identical to the apparatus collecting revenue, just as the *Fatāwā-yi ʿĀlamgīrī* was a scholarly compilation whose practical reception varied in place and period. The historian needs dated decrees, local records, texts and the work of actual jurists rather than a uniform characterization of “Mughal Islam.”

The spiritual and philosophical traditions of Safavid Iran and Mughal India were likewise not one discipline. Mullā Ṣadrā's metaphysics, Imāmī legal reasoning, Persian poetry, the work of Sufi lineages and South Asian hadith transmission interacted through specific people who sometimes disputed one another's claims. One cannot infer from their coexistence that they all endorsed the same theory of the Muhammadan Reality.

The Safavid promotion of Twelver Shiʿism and the Mughal compilation of the *Fatāwā-yi ʿĀlamgīrī* represent different projects. The first included public confessional policy and the work of learned communities; the second required jurists, scribes, texts and imperial patronage. Neither can be equated with the inward religious life of all inhabitants, and the Imāmī doctrine of the Imamate cannot be silently converted into an Akbarian account of saintly inheritance. The Muhammadan centre remains a theological interpretation of ordered guidance, not the administrative identity of any one dynasty. The comparison becomes concrete when it asks who taught, what the jurists argued, how courts operated and what people actually practised.

The Safavid adoption of Twelver Shiʿism was a state project with long and uneven social consequences, not an instant change in the convictions of all Iran's inhabitants in 1501. The arrival and support of scholars from regions with established Imāmī learning are part of that history. The relation of rulers to jurists also changed between Ismāʿīl I and later Safavid reigns. Mughal policies toward religious communities, revenue and scholarship differed among rulers and provinces, and changed across time. Akbar's courtly debates, revenue administration and imperial household were not interchangeable institutions; the *Fatāwā-yi ʿĀlamgīrī* is a major compilation whose practical reception demands separate research.

Safavid Isfahan supported philosophers and theologians including Mullā Ṣadrā, while Indian scholarly networks developed curricula in law, ḥadīth, logic and spiritual discipline. Philosophers, jurists and spiritual authors within each empire disputed one another's positions rather than expressing a uniform imperial doctrine. Shāh Walī Allāh's eighteenth-century work later connected South Asian hadith, law and spiritual concerns in a setting shaped by Mughal fragmentation. Comparative studies of the three empires should account for revenue systems, military organization, confessional policy and local communities before making large claims about decline or renewal. Safavid patronage of Twelver Shiʿi scholarship gave Iran institutions different from those sustained by Ottoman Sunni and Mughal scholarly networks.

**Case: producing the Fatāwā-yi ʿĀlamgīrī.** Under Aurangzeb a group of Ḥanafī jurists coordinated the *Fatāwā-yi ʿĀlamgīrī*, also called the *Fatāwā al-Hindīyya*, as a wide-ranging legal compendium prepared under the leadership of Niẓām al-Dīn of Burhānpur ([Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/fatawi-e-alamgiri/); [Oxford research project](https://fa.ames.ox.ac.uk/about/)). This was a collective scholarly undertaking made possible by earlier texts, editors, scribes and the material support of an imperial patron. A book concerning prayer, trade, property and public law did not automatically become the empire's enforced law in every village; actual courts and persons still mediated decisions. The compendium was subsequently copied beyond Mughal India and interpreted under colonial legal arrangements. Its biography demonstrates how a valid specialized science can acquire a large-scale written form **without turning either its emperor or its editors into a new prophetic source**. The source still needs manuscript-level comparison to track exact decisions and later uses.

# Book VII — Modern change in the conditions of religious life

## 43. Oceans, finance and the changing balance of power

**Matn.** European ships entered Indian Ocean systems already sustained by Arab, Indian, Persian, African and other merchants. Portuguese voyagers reached India by the Cape route in 1498, and Portuguese forces seized Goa in 1510. Asian merchants nevertheless continued extensive commercial networks. Dutch and English companies subsequently obtained trading privileges, employed armed shipping and sought territorial influence. In 1765 the East India Company acquired revenue-collection authority in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa under an imperial grant, while local officers and institutions remained essential to collection. Controlling a commodity, a port and the revenues of a province were different forms of power. Industrial production, public borrowing, steam transport and changing military organization later altered the resources available to governments and companies.

These developments affected Muslim societies differently. A merchant family in a Gulf port, a landholder in South Asia, an Ottoman official and a teacher supported by an endowment experienced different pressures. Governments altered military expenditure and revenue demands, while companies sometimes acquired the authority to govern territories rather than merely conduct trade. The material conditions of law, teaching, pilgrimage and households changed through specific institutions.

These changes brought new questions for merchants, rulers and religious teachers. In more distant and complex transactions, people still needed trustworthy dealing, sound judgment and institutions able to protect their rights. Long-distance trade and improved communication widened access to goods and information, but the expanding power of states and companies also created new forms of dependence and vulnerability.

**Sharḥ.** European trading companies entered an Indian Ocean system in which merchants, ports, credit networks and regional rulers had long been active. The Indian Ocean contained routes, port offices, credit practices and resident communities whose history predated the companies. Their subsequent coercive capacities changed unevenly. Political control of one harbour is not the same as monopoly of all regional trade; an inland territorial revenue system differs again from shipping insurance or commodity credit.

The material consequences reach the inner life through actual mediation. A household's ability to maintain education may depend upon employment or migration; a legal institution may be reorganized as a company acquires jurisdiction; a merchant's transactions may involve new distances, documents and impersonal mechanisms of enforcement. The virtues of honesty and fairness do not become irrelevant, but their exercise requires knowledge of the new kinds of dependence and harm that a transaction can produce.

Hodgson's “Great Western Transmutation” examines changes of production, technology and communication; Guénon's critique of the reign of quantity judges the spiritual implications of a different set of ideas and experiences. Neither can be treated as a substitute for the other's evidence. A significant modern question is when technical abstraction and numerical administration serve legitimate ends and when they obscure personal relationships, qualities of work or claims of the vulnerable.

At Surat and other Indian Ocean ports, a merchant needed reliable measures, safe shipping, credit and a means of settling disputes. Company fleets later acquired coercive capabilities and territorial revenue powers through particular acts, but did not thereby obtain authority over every commercial relationship. The normative demand for truthful exchange remains recognizable even as contract distance, insurance and state finance change its practical tests. Hodgson's historical account of technological and economic transformation and Guénon's spiritual critique of quantity ask distinct questions. An Akbarian explanation of comprehensive human judgment may bring them into conversation but cannot establish either maritime chronology or the moral status of a transaction without evidence. The human subject remains answerable to rights obscured by an abstract price or statistic.

Hodgson's 'Great Western Transmutation' describes changes in technology, communication, production and social organization that accompanied and exceeded Europe's overseas conquests. Atlantic credit, maritime trade and continuing Asian commercial networks developed together, challenging claims of a sudden and universal European monopoly. The familiar claim that Europeans simply monopolized the Indian Ocean from 1500 is false; studies of Gujarat and the Gulf document cosmopolitan communities and continuing Asian participation into the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Nor should a single Ottoman defeat be made the beginning of a uniform Muslim 'decline.'

Material change mattered to intellectual and religious life through identifiable intermediaries. A state unable to finance armies might reform taxes, borrow money or reorganize offices. A trading company that acquired territorial power could alter courts and property claims. Printed books and eventually telegraphy changed the speed and scale at which arguments moved. These were uneven transformations: an endowment-supported teacher in Cairo, a village jurist in India and a merchant family on the Swahili coast experienced them differently. Guénon criticized modern conceptions of knowledge and quantity, a metaphysical interpretation distinct from documentary evidence about finance, commerce and administration.

Surat's maritime trade demonstrates why European arrival did not produce instant European control. Merchants of different religions and regions financed vessels, exchanged credit and relied on commercial partnerships; European companies entered these existing networks while later acquiring territorial power in different ports. Political control over a harbour, a monopoly over a commodity and control of an inland agrarian region are three different achievements. In Europe itself, changing relations among taxation, debt and military expenditure affected how states competed. Hodgson's 'transmutation' and traditionalist critiques of modern civilization address different questions: the former seeks historical mechanisms of change; the latter evaluates the intellectual and spiritual meaning of modern modes of life. Their conclusions can be compared only after the subject of each has been specified.

**Case: Surat, Swally and Mughal port authority.** In the early seventeenth century, Surat's governor permitted the English Company to use Swally as an anchorage while Mughal authorities sought to regulate European access to the Tapti River. Warehouses, transport routes and control over customs subsequently made Swally part of Surat's maritime system ([Shinsaku Kato, *Formation of Surat Port City*](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jjasas/2017/29/2017_33/_article/-char/en)). Indian merchant and banker networks also supplied brokerage and credit without which European companies could not operate effectively ([Aasim Khwaja, *Mughal port officials*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/08438714211061670)). The history is not a one-directional story of foreigners immediately dominating every local market. Individual negotiations, port officers and repeated commercial relations changed the distribution of power. The higher ethical question concerns whether commerce and public force serve rightful claims; the operational history concerns ships, warehouses, loans and officials. Conflating the two would make neither intelligible.

## 44. Colonial courts and the transformation of Islamic law

**Matn.** In parts of South Asia, British Company rule assumed powers of revenue collection and adjudication formerly exercised through Mughal and other local institutions. Officials retained selected formulations of Muslim and Hindu law while placing their application under courts organized by new procedures, translations, officials and appellate authorities. The Hastings Judicial Plan of 1772 marked an important change in Bengal, though later regulations altered the system again. Many legal terms and books remained in use, but colonial courts changed who interpreted them and how decisions were made and enforced.

The transformation can be followed in a particular family dispute. The dispute began with a deed executed by two brothers in the Sylhet district in 1868. They set aside property to support descendants, with provision for the poor once the family line had ended. The local court accepted the deed as a waqf, but the Calcutta High Court disagreed. On appeal in 1894, the Privy Council in London held that the promised benefit to the poor was too remote to establish a valid charitable endowment. Its decision affected many similar family arrangements and helped prompt legislation permitting qualifying family waqfs in 1913, later extended to earlier deeds by an Act of 1930.

The case shows how the application of a familiar legal doctrine could change when disputes were heard before new authorities. The family's documents, the jurists' interpretations, the advocates' arguments and the powers of an imperial appellate court all affected the outcome.

Muslim scholars continued teaching inherited jurisprudence, spiritual disciplines and household obligations under these changing conditions. New madrasas and colleges responded differently to the altered distribution of expertise. Muslim teachers and jurists therefore faced the difficult task of preserving learned methods while working within courts and educational systems that had changed their authority.

**Sharḥ.** **A documented institutional transition rather than a general thesis of decline.** Hastings's 1772 Judicial Plan in Bengal redistributed public jurisdiction while claiming to preserve certain forms of law appropriate to religious communities. Elizabeth Lhost, *Everyday Islamic Law and the Making of Modern South Asia* (2022), and Robert Travers, *Empires of Complaints* (2022), show why this was not a simple replacement of “religion” by one impersonal colonial statute. Company officials drew upon earlier Mughal petition and revenue practices while reclassifying claims and relocating decisive authority in new courts ([Travers, chapter 2](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/empires-of-complaints/recasting-mughal-law/B4722B505337E0E7D234FE6368E2D2C6)).

**The real object of a legal ruling.** A classical jurist distinguishes the normative category of an act, its legal conditions and the evidence of the actual case. Yet the application of that knowledge depends upon people: the claimant, respondents, witnesses, documentary evidence, judges, appointed experts and officers enforcing the outcome. Moving a rule into another institutional order changes who may decide, what evidence can be heard, how arguments are translated and which judgments create binding precedent. Written verbal continuity does not entail the identity of these relations.

**A named case: Abul Fata Mahomed Ishak and others v. Russomoy Dhur Chowdhry and others (Privy Council, 1894).** Gregory C. Kozlowski, *Muslim Endowments and Society in British India*, chapter “Creating a Law of Muslim Endowments” (Cambridge, 1985; [chapter overview](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/muslim-endowments-and-society-in-british-india/creating-a-law-of-muslim-endowments/427E1B538EC75D0080552CDB4DE4F7DB)), reconstructs this Sylhet-connected case involving a familial waqf. Nandini Chatterjee's “Archives as windows. A Zamīndār's records in a colonial court” (*Quaderni storici*, 2021, [DOI](https://www.rivisteweb.it/doi/10.1408/102878)) studies how members of a landholding Muslim family assembled documents from household stores for colonial litigation. The case is exceptionally valuable because it joins the microhistory of family records and conflict to the macrohistory of an empire's appellate law.

**The deed and its legal treatment.** In the [published judgment of 15 December 1894](https://courtkutchehry.com/judgments/abul-fata-mahomed-ishak-and-others-appellant-hash-russomoy-dhur-chowdhry-and-others-respondent), Lord Hobhouse recounts a waqf deed executed by Abdur Rahman and Abdool Kadir on 21 December 1868. The brothers and their descendants were its immediate beneficiaries; the poor, widows and orphans of Sylhet would benefit only when the family line became extinct. The settlors remained managers, retained discretion over allowances, and restricted beneficiaries' powers to demand accounts. A subordinate judge in Sylhet had accepted the waqf, but the Calcutta High Court reversed that decision. The Privy Council heard no new witnesses; it judged the agreement using earlier records and counsel's legal arguments. The Board rejected the appeal because it considered the charitable dedication too remote and uncertain to be substantial.

**A contested legal interpretation and a legislative response.** The Privy Council discussed contrary views argued by Amīr ʿAlī concerning family support and the purposes of waqf, but did not adopt them. Its judgment reflected one tribunal's interpretation of Muslim law and did not end juristic disagreement. Sections 3–4 of the [Mussalman Wakf Validating Act, 1913](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1852964/) subsequently recognized qualifying family waqfs even where benefit to the poor would come only after the extinction of descendants. The Act initially applied prospectively. Section 2 of the [1930 Validating Act](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/192467222/) gave it qualified retrospective effect while preserving rights already accrued. The sequence reveals how a judgment concerning one family's deed entered a wider controversy over the institutions through which Muslim property law was administered.

**The historical difference between law's norm and law's social form.** A rule of Islamic jurisprudence can remain recognizable while its site of authority shifts. A mufti's fatwa, a qāḍī's judgment, a deed drawn by local specialists and a Privy Council appeal are different operations under different claims of jurisdiction. Even if two reach the same substantive result, their social relations and the capacities they train are not identical. Conversely, difference of procedure alone does not prove each colonial judgment substantively false. The analysis must inspect each case and its evidence.

**Character and professional competence.** A trained jurist may understand classical authorities but lack knowledge of a particular family's documentary history; a judge may know appellate procedure but misunderstand an inherited technical term; a litigant may possess family documents while selectively presenting them. Justice needs relationships among these forms of knowledge. Scholarly *malaka* includes the capacity to discriminate facts, rules, proof and limits of competence. It does not follow automatically from holding office, reading a translation, or possessing claimed spiritual insight.

The 1868 Sylhet deed and the 1894 Privy Council decision make a precise distinction visible. A jurist may recognize the category of a family *waqf*, while a colonial appellate court controls which arguments, translations and standards of proof determine legal effect. In 1913 and 1930 legislation altered the rules again. This is a shift in jurisdiction and interpretation, not merely loss of a single sacred word. The Prophet's comprehensive guidance is an orienting whole for justice, yet no modern tribunal or inherited juristic formula recreates the personal discernment of every concrete relationship. That observation cannot decide this dispute by mystical intuition: the legal record, the parties' intentions as evidenced, valid doctrine and actual statutory provisions still have to be studied. The case follows family actions into imperial institutions through identifiable documents.

**Impact upon learning.** Changing courts and legal professions affected which forms of expertise could find public use, what languages advocates and officials learned, and why Islamic schools sought different models of preservation, renewal or engagement. Deoband, Aligarh and many local institutions made distinct decisions under these pressures. Their founding aims, curricula and historical effects require their own evidence; one Privy Council case cannot be made the sole cause of all nineteenth-century Muslim educational reform.

**From household documents to imperial law.** The two brothers' attempt to protect family property passed through local litigation, an appellate ruling in Calcutta and a final decision in London. The same case then became an argument in public discussion and legislation over family endowments. The means of transmission are identifiable: a deed, litigants, family records, lawyers, courts, a published judgment and statutes. The history demonstrates the effect of changing institutions upon a specific legal practice without assuming that one judgment represents every view within the Ḥanafī tradition.

The colonial transformation of Islamic law thus illustrates neither the futility of legal forms nor the self-sufficiency of textual preservation. It demonstrates the need for an integrated order of persons, proofs, judgments, and institutions, whose proper end is justice and whose actual shape is contingent upon power, language and history.

## 45. Renewal through teaching, reform and print

**Matn.** Scholars confronted new conditions of political power, schooling, transport and the circulation of books. Printing made commentaries, collections of prophetic reports, devotional manuals and polemics available to people far beyond the circles in which some works had been taught. Railways and steamships altered pilgrimage and communication; changing schools prepared students for occupations that did not necessarily share the older disciplines' curricula. Schools and readers acquired new ways of accessing religious texts, while teachers adapted their methods to these changing circumstances.

Teachers and donors established a seminary at Deoband in the 1860s to sustain inherited religious learning under colonial conditions. Sir Sayyid Aḥmad Khān established a school at Aligarh in 1875, followed by the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College in 1877. Its curriculum pursued modern subjects and English-language education alongside its religious commitments. Scholars in Cairo, Ottoman lands and elsewhere also debated legal reasoning, religious schooling, science and reform. Sufi teachers, households, independent scholars and regional networks continued forms of company and instruction outside these formal schools. These schools pursued different aims and often disagreed about how inherited learning should relate to new forms of education.

A printed page could preserve a wording accurately while circulating without its earlier teacher's qualifications or explanation. Conversely, wider availability might enable a student to locate texts and questions once inaccessible. Teachers and educational institutions faced new questions about how readers could understand texts without the immediate help of an experienced scholar. They responded with curricula, examinations, study circles and public debates, with differing results.

**Sharḥ.** The printing press can extend both genuine knowledge and mistaken extraction from context. A reader may accurately repeat a phrase and still lack the language, source comparison or practical distinctions needed to understand it; another can use widespread access to scrutinize claims previously insulated by local authority. These possibilities cannot be decided by declaring print intrinsically spiritually inferior or automatically emancipatory.

Teaching therefore involves **different achievements**: accurate recognition of a text, contextual understanding, the capacity to reason from it, action conforming to its requirements, and formation of the relevant dispositions. These are related but cannot be replaced by one standardized examination. Written media preserve the first two in one way; teachers, practice and correction can contribute to the latter three. Some specialist skills also require experiments, calculations and evidence not supplied by religious literary sources.

The differences among Deoband, Aligarh and other schools should be reconstructed through real founders, curricula, student bodies, finances and institutional aims. Religious educators were not one uniform party, and the history of Sufi communities likewise includes multiple approaches to personal guidance and scholarly training. The revived concern with hadith, law and spiritual practices belongs to distinct, sometimes contentious traditions of renewal.

Deoband and Aligarh answered different educational problems in colonial South Asia through donors, teachers, languages, curricula and changing expectations of employment. A printed book could carry a correct text to new pupils, yet the student still needed the ability to understand, argue, act and, where appropriate, cultivate inward sincerity. An examination may certify one learned skill without proving every dimension of character. Conversely, personal spiritual reputation cannot replace mastery of law, medicine or language. The Akbarian account of the Prophet's comprehensive example is most useful here as a measure of how these distinct achievements may cooperate, not as a slogan declaring all modern schools spiritually empty. The reported 1866/1867 Deoband chronology requires separate source resolution.

Reformers disagreed over inherited curricula, state schooling, legal authority and new sciences; their programmes crossed any simple traditional–modern division. Deoband, Aligarh, reformist writers in Cairo, Ottoman educational institutions and West African learned networks pursued different objectives. Some expanded hadith study; some sought a reorganization of legal authority; some defended established Sufi instruction; some integrated administrative or scientific subjects. Printed editions also altered textual practice: a student's stable printed page differed from a manuscript exemplar annotated through personal reading, but neither medium guaranteed correct understanding.

Al-Attas connects education to *adab* and the ordering of knowledge; Guénon criticizes the modern priority given to quantity. Their arguments interpret modernity at a different level from the institutional histories of particular schools. Al-Attas and traditionalist authors later offered distinct critiques of knowledge and modernity; neither expressed the intentions of every nineteenth-century Muslim teacher. The historical evidence involves school registers, syllabi, printed editions, biographies, correspondence and institutional records. The better question is which students each new arrangement served, what it enabled them to do, and what forms of knowledge became easier or harder to transmit. The answer will vary across classes, languages, genders and regions. Print, mass schooling and institutional examinations changed how teachers explained texts, corrected mistakes and assessed students' understanding.

Deoband and Aligarh represented distinct institutional responses among many forms of South Asian Muslim education. Deoband emerged through a specific scholarly and financial arrangement, while the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College developed its own residential and institutional aspirations. Women, merchant communities, village teachers, Sufi networks and other regional schools also shaped religious learning. Print made a legal opinion or devotional text more widely available, yet public circulation could intensify disputes over wording, authority and context. Examinations, lessons and student writings reveal differences between memorizing formulas, explaining a text and applying its reasoning to unfamiliar cases. This links the formation of *malakāt* to actual instructional practice without pretending that one curriculum was uniformly successful.

**Case: Deoband's formative decades.** Local educational records and retrospective institutional histories date the seminary's establishment to 1866, whereas scholarly reference works commonly give 1867, including [Barbara Metcalf's *Encyclopaedia Iranica* article](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/deoband/) and [SherAli Tareen's Oxford bibliography](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62361/reference-article-abstract/571507741). Until the Islamic and Gregorian dates and different definitions of opening and founding have been compared directly, the narrative uses “the 1860s” rather than declaring one year certain. Deoband developed through contributions from teachers and donors, classes in inherited disciplines and journeys by students who later taught elsewhere. Urdu-medium teaching also shaped the relation between Arabic textual training and changing South Asian languages.

**Aligarh's different institutional sequence.** Sir Sayyid Aḥmad Khān's educational initiative began with a school in 1875; the [Aligarh Muslim University history](https://www.amu.ac.in/about-us/history) dates the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College's foundation to 7 January 1877. The college developed under different expectations about English learning, government employment and the sciences. Comparing the institutions requires curricula, resources, student careers and stated aims, not a caricature of one as immutably traditional and the other as simply secular.

## 46. Nation-states, migration and the new scale of Muslim life

**Matn.** The Ottoman sultanate ended in 1922, and the Turkish Republic abolished the caliphate in 1924 while reorganizing public religious administration. Elsewhere the end of colonial rule produced different political settlements: British India was partitioned in 1947, while Indonesian independence was proclaimed in 1945 and formally recognized by the Netherlands in 1949 after further conflict and negotiation. New borders, citizenship rules, schools and ministries changed the conditions under which Muslim communities governed and taught. Migration also carried families, mosques and scholarly networks across state boundaries. Mass schooling, broadcasting, air travel and later digital networks connected people who did not share one language or jurisdiction.

A child might learn prayer from a parent, Qurʾānic Arabic in school, legal reasoning from a university teacher and spiritual counsel from a person whose character the family trusts. An online archive can make an enormous body of transmitted writing searchable; a short video can bring a question to someone who lacks a local scholar. Yet accessibility of information does not itself create understanding, accurate attribution, practical judgment or a formed disposition. The very same means that extend teaching can fragment attention or detach claims from their original circumstances.

Muslims continued to turn to the Qurʾān and the Sunnah for guidance while learning to address circumstances unfamiliar to earlier generations. Scholars and teachers drew upon inherited principles, interpretation and experience in dealing with new questions. Families, schools, workplaces and religious communities still needed people capable of trustworthy judgment and care for others. The means of communication had changed, but the work of teaching and forming human character continued through actual relationships.

**Sharḥ.** The political changes of the twentieth century did not create one uniform replacement for earlier Muslim government. The Ottoman sultanate was abolished in 1922 and the caliphate on 3 March 1924. The Turkish state simultaneously reorganized public religious offices and schooling. These changes affected legal and educational authority through new institutions, rather than merely through the disappearance of a title. See Martin van Bruinessen, [“The Abolition of the Caliphate, Secularization and Kurdish Nationalism”](https://research-portal.uu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/235621636/10.4324_9781032613468-6_chapterpdf.pdf).

British India's partition in 1947, Indonesia's struggle for independence during 1945–49 and different African decolonizations changed borders, courts, languages and Muslims' legal positions in different ways. A household within a new majority state faced different public questions from a migrant family establishing a mosque under minority-community laws. Ministries, local associations, jurists, Sufi circles and universities did not inherit identical forms of religious authority.

**Transmission and formation.** A parent teaches a child to pray; a teacher corrects Qurʾānic recitation; a jurist works through evidence about a dispute; a physician attends to bodily needs; a spiritual guide offers counsel within that role's competence. These activities may support one human life without being interchangeable. Earlier Islamic schools similarly distinguished authoritative texts, disciplined interpretation, practice and formation of character.

Digital archives extend access to manuscripts, editions and commentaries, yet each new technical possibility introduces further requirements: accurate metadata, manuscript comparison, linguistic competence, source attribution and error correction. Machine-assisted matching may help identify a parallel passage without proving that an early witness is authentic or that its argument is understood. Competent researchers can combine searchable texts with instruction, collaborative review and disciplined verification.

**A contemporary documentary case.** The British Academy describes an [ongoing Oxford project to create an annotated bilingual digital edition of the *Fatāwā-yi ʿĀlamgīrī*](https://www.thebritishacademy.ac.uk/projects/islamic-law-in-south-asia-the-fatawa-alamgiriyya-project/). Its plan connects legal issues, passages, places and earlier scholarship. The work is not described as a completed scholarly interface. It illustrates how text comparison and structured retrieval can assist future jurists and historians without themselves conferring legal authority or settled understanding.

A searchable digital edition makes a text available under conditions unavailable to a medieval student. It does not automatically produce a reliable variant reading, a correct juristic judgment or the *malaka* needed to apply one. The ongoing [Oxford *Fatāwā-yi ʿĀlamgīrī* project](https://www.thebritishacademy.ac.uk/projects/islamic-law-in-south-asia-the-fatawa-alamgiriyya-project/) illustrates the genuine promise of connecting passages, places and prior authorities while leaving their verification to trained inquiry. Al-Qūnawī and al-Farghānī offer an Akbarian interpretation of the Prophet's comprehensive spiritual inheritance; they do not make one digital scholar or one spiritual teacher a replacement for the final Messenger. A living Muhammadan-oriented civilization will depend upon persons able to relate trustworthy sources, specific competencies, justice, worship and character under altered historical conditions.

# Critical apparatus, worked cases and independent audit

## Editorial purpose and limits

This is a **narrative-critical study edition** of the Sīrah and later Islamic history. It is not a diplomatic edition of an early Sīrah manuscript, and it does not claim to establish a new critical Arabic text of Ibn Isḥāq, al-Ṭabarī or another primary author. The matn presents a continuous narrative without numbered textual citations. The sharḥ tests its consequential claims through primary witnesses, historical context, later argument and appropriately identified contemporary scholarship. Its principal Sunni narrative frame makes room for the distinct Shiʿi theories of religious authority and historical memory where they materially shape the sequence; it does not make their controversy the organizing subject.

Four different questions must not be confused: what a revealed text says; what a transmitted historical account reports; what can be corroborated or reconstructed from multiple witnesses; and what a philosopher or spiritual teacher argues that the event means. They often illuminate one another but possess different standards of justification. The matn's chronological use of earlier prophets follows sacred history; biblical numerical chronologies and modern reconstructions, where relevant, belong in the sharḥ. Guénon's interpretation of the sixth-century BCE religious transformations is compared as a philosophy of sacred history, not presented as a verified causal event.

The 46 entries are not supposed to contain equal amounts of material. The Prophetic chapters take the reader through decisions and changes of circumstance. The later chapters are organized around regional or institutional processes with sufficient narrative room to avoid a list of dynasties. A chapter is successful when a reader can answer *what created this difficulty, what people did about it, why that response had consequences, and how we know*. If it cannot answer those questions, another paragraph of names will not remedy the defect.

## Dossier 1 — Meccan protection, public recitation, and the Hijrah: one report read closely (chapters 8–11)

The early Muslim community cannot be understood by simply saying that its members were persecuted and then migrated. They belonged to households whose members enjoyed unequal degrees of protection. Some could withdraw into a powerful clan, others were dependent clients, and some had no effective protector. Religious instruction therefore travelled through existing households even as it called into question the inherited basis of standing within them. The clearest way of seeing this interaction is through the long report transmitted from ʿĀʾishah by ʿUrwah b. al-Zubayr, from Ibn Shihāb al-Zuhrī, in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī*, no. 3905, chapter on migration to Madinah. The chain matters: it is a report from an early family and scholarly network, not a series of contemporaneously dated depositions for every episode it narrates.

The report describes Abū Bakr setting out toward Aksum when opposition made life in Makkah difficult. At Bark al-Ghimād he met Ibn al-Daghinah, a tribal leader who offered him *jiwār*—public protection. Ibn al-Daghinah justified the offer by referring to Abū Bakr's care for relatives, dependants, guests and people in distress. Protection was thus negotiated through a reputation for social obligation recognizable even to people who did not share his belief. The Meccan notables accepted the arrangement on the condition that Abū Bakr worship quietly within his house. When he began reciting in a prayer-space in his courtyard, women and children gathered to listen; his public recitation unsettled the notables, who pressed the guarantor to restrict him. Abū Bakr refused to confine worship in the demanded manner and returned the guarantee. The passage joins religious emotion, domestic space, public attention, kinship prestige and the enforceable conditions of protection in one narrated sequence. It gives a much better explanation of the social stakes of preaching than a generic statement that the old elite feared Islam.

The crucial difference between private and public recitation was not a change of doctrine. A man reading aloud in view of other households created an audience not fully controlled by patriarchs or clan elders. The report presents women and children attending to the recitation; it does **not** give demographic figures for conversion or license a claim that all women or children adopted the message. It shows how a practice that was legally tolerable within the home became politically contentious at its threshold. In this setting, sincerity, steadfastness and courage were not abstract virtues added by later writers: they were manifested in a decision whether to surrender protection in order to continue a practice. Yet the subsequent precise interior state of Abū Bakr is known only insofar as the report describes it; later accounts of spiritual stations should not be made to stand in for historical testimony.

The same report proceeds to the prospect of a new destination, describes the Prophet's permission to migrate, Abū Bakr's preparation of mounts, the flight and cave, and arrival in the oasis. Its story of preparation is not evidence that every migrant undertook identical measures. Its value for the narrative lies in the sequence: protection at Makkah had limits; Aksum offered refuge under a Christian ruler but not a Muslim public order; Yathrib, through instruction and pledges, promised a place in which believers could defend and administer collective obligations. The Hijrah was therefore at once flight from danger, obedience to guidance, and the beginning of new burdens upon hosts and newcomers.

**Textual control.** The report is [al-Bukhārī 3905](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3905); the Arabic chain and extended narrative can also be checked in [the numbered Arabic transcript](https://equranlibrary.com/hadith/bukhari/1317/3905). The *matn* of chapters 10–11 should tell the episode. The *sharḥ* must distinguish the narrated facts, the inferred social mechanism (control over audiences and protection), and the spiritual reading (the testing of constancy). Open tasks: compare parallel reports of *jiwār* and the ʿAqabah pledges in Ibn Isḥāq/Ibn Hishām and al-Ṭabarī before treating speeches and precise sequences as independently confirmed.

## Dossier 2 — The Medinan *kitāb*: obligations before constitutional theory (chapter 12)

When the emigrants arrived in Yathrib, they entered an already inhabited and politically divided oasis. Households owned date gardens and had to manage labour, water, blood-compensation, captivity and military alliances. The survival of the new fellowship demanded a working answer to practical questions. Who pays compensation when a member of one group causes a death? Who helps ransom a captive? Who contributes to defence if a neighbouring coalition attacks? The transmitted *kitāb* attributed to the Prophet addresses precisely such problems. Its clauses are preserved in Ibn Isḥāq's material through Ibn Hishām and in another transmitted recension associated with Abū ʿUbayd's *Kitāb al-Amwāl*. The existence of recensions calls for comparison; it does not justify either dismissing the whole document or treating one modern clause-numbering system as an original feature of the text.

The opening describes participants as **«أُمَّةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ مِنْ دُونِ النَّاسِ»**—a distinct community or association. But subsequent clauses do not erase inherited group membership. They refer to the emigrants and named Yathrib clans retaining their arrangements for collective compensation and ransoming captives. The recurrent expression **«يَتَعَاقَلُونَ مَعَاقِلَهُمُ الأُولَى»** makes precisely that continuity visible. An injured family would not suddenly receive compensation from an anonymous modern treasury; actual groups still bore responsibilities while entering a wider system of obligation. For the newly arrived emigrants, arrangements of mutual support had to operate alongside these obligations. Religious allegiance did not automatically furnish food, land or enforceable claims. It created grounds on which people could undertake and judge those claims together.

The document also contains clauses about Jewish participants, their religion, expenditures and mutual aid. In one recension the brief wording **«لِلْيَهُودِ دِينُهُمْ وَلِلْمُسْلِمِينَ دِينُهُمْ»** appears. This is important evidence of specified religious differentiation, but it must not be taken to imply that every Jewish group in the oasis had signed the same instrument, or that its provisions amounted to a modern charter of equal individual citizenship. Michael Lecker's detailed study argues that participation was more restricted than popular presentations suppose and that some major groups had separate arrangements. The 2024 publication of his revised study contains separate chapters on Muslim and Jewish participants, the treaties, dating, unity, recensions and preservation. These are disagreements about **which persons and obligations** were included, not merely arguments over terminology.

This illuminates a transition that religious or political slogans conceal. The religious *umma* emerging around the Prophet included believers bound by worship and allegiance. But its public security could require arrangements with groups of different religion. Religious fellowship and political protection were therefore partly overlapping, partly distinct associations. The Prophet's adjudicating role operated at that intersection. The *kitāb* presents conditions for making cooperation predictable; it does not show that every clan ceased to function or that disputes ended. Nor does it establish by itself when each later Medinan command concerning marriage, property, fasting or inheritance was revealed. Those commands should be established separately in the relevant textual histories.

Modern comparison: Michael Lecker, *The “Constitution of Medina”: Muhammad's First Legal Document* (2004), [author's institutional summary](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-constitution-of-medina-muhammads-first-legal-document-2/), and the [2024 revised edition's chapter architecture](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/constitution-of-medina/index/649E9B0DFF89432F11FF4FC90A79C48F). The present edition has checked the passages and scholarship but has **not** transcribed and line-by-line collated both complete recensions; clause numbering should therefore be treated as an editorial convenience, not definitive textual segmentation.

## Dossier 3 — Al-Ḥudaybiyyah: a difficult treaty as a test of communal judgment (chapter 16)

The Prophet's party approached Makkah with the purpose of pilgrimage rather than a declared attempt to take the city. Quraysh's obstruction forced a choice: withdraw without entering, fight, or seek an enforceable arrangement. The narrative in al-Bukhārī 2731–2732 gives political deliberation and spiritual trial as one event, not two unrelated lessons. The party manoeuvred to avoid cavalry, halted near al-Ḥudaybiyyah, and exchanged envoys. Negotiations culminated in Suhayl b. ʿAmr's arrival and a text whose disputed wording dramatized the difficulty of recognizing parties while each asserted different religious claims.

The terms deferred the intended pilgrimage, regulated a subsequent return visit, established a truce and addressed the treatment of fugitives from Makkah. The clause concerning a person arriving from Quraysh without a guardian's permission was especially painful when Abū Jandal, Suhayl's son, appeared in chains. It is insufficient to say that the treaty was a strategic success; that description leaves out what the participants thought they were surrendering. The report includes ʿUmar's distressed questions and the Prophet's insistence that his trust in the outcome did not exempt him from carrying out the agreed decision. The episode's immediate object was not trade expansion or later conversion statistics, but an encounter between fidelity to revelation and the difficulty of obeying a command whose effects were not yet visible.

A second scene explains the transition from agreement on paper to actual public conduct. After the terms had been made, the Prophet instructed the pilgrims to sacrifice and shave. The report describes their hesitation. Umm Salamah advised him to perform the rites himself without further exhortation; the Companions then followed. This is a rare sequence in which a political agreement, domestic counsel, ritual action and collective discipline are causally linked. The prior blockage of a religious goal generated grief and confusion; a visible act resolved the immediate practical impasse more effectively than repeating an order. The source allows us to say this. It does not license the stronger claim that we can identify every individual's motive for hesitation.

The familiar judgment that al-Ḥudaybiyyah proved an opening (*fatḥ*) belongs to the transmitted religious interpretation and to the subsequent sequence of events. The truce helped alter the terms in which groups could approach the Muslim community, and later alliance violations preceded the march on Makkah. Yet the exact growth of adherents between 628 and 630 cannot be calculated simply from this one account. A treaty opens opportunities; the inference that it mechanically caused every subsequent conversion would outrun the evidence. Nor should the restrictions on returning women be treated as permanently identical to the initial terms: the report relates subsequent revelation and a distinction in their treatment.

**Limited comparison of two published narrative recensions.** The version of Ibn Isḥāq preserved by Ibn Hishām (2:319, passages headed *What troubled the people at the treaty and the arrival of Abū Jandal*, and *The Prophet sacrificed and shaved, and the people followed*) describes Abū Jandal arriving in irons during the writing, the public distress at returning without entering Makkah, and the Prophet's sacrifice and shaving after the treaty. On seeing him, the Companions proceeded to sacrifice and shave. This part of Ibn Hishām's published presentation does not recount Umm Salamah's intervening counsel. Al-Bukhārī 2731–2732 describes the hesitation after three instructions and attributes the solution to Umm Salamah's advice that the Prophet act first without further speech. These accounts share the crucial sequence—disappointing treaty, sacrifice and shaving by the Prophet, communal imitation—but they give differently detailed explanations of the intervening action. The narrower comparison confirms the *reported* role of visible Prophetic example; it does not demonstrate that the earlier redaction denied Umm Salamah's role. Other transmissions linked to Ibn Isḥāq preserve it. The published texts have been compared here, **not** their complete manuscript witnesses or all chains. Primary passages: [Ibn Hishām on Abū Jandal](https://www.islamweb.net/ar/library/content/58/1280/), [Ibn Hishām on sacrifice and shaving](https://islamweb.net/ar/library/content/58/1282/من-شهدوا-على-الصلح), and [al-Bukhārī 2731–2732](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2731). For an additional Ibn Isḥāq-associated line mentioning Umm Salamah, compare [al-Dhahabī's presentation](https://islamweb.net/ar/library/content/60/6317/قصة-غزوة-الحديبية).

**Source and limit.** [Al-Bukhārī 2731–2732, *Kitāb al-Shurūṭ*, chapter on treaties](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2731) is the detailed base witness; compare [the continuation at 2732](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2732). Qurʾān 48:1–29 and 60:10 belong in the *sharḥ*, where their theological and legal roles can be distinguished from what the narrator reports. A limited comparison with the published Ibn Hishām presentation has now been made; full manuscript comparison, systematic isnād-cum-matn analysis, and dating of subsequent tribal breaches remain outstanding. This dossier establishes a coherent, source-grounded explanation of a difficult event; it does not constitute a full isnād-cum-matn collation.

## Dossier 4 — Khaybar: cultivation, revenue and the limits of political simplification (chapter 17)

The military settlement at Khaybar created a problem familiar in agrarian conquests: an army could acquire claims to land without possessing the expertise or labour to cultivate its orchards immediately. Evicting all cultivators could remove the productive workforce and reduce the harvest; leaving production unchanged could sustain families whose relations to the new authority had altered. The transmitted arrangement answered that problem through terms of cultivation and a share of produce. This institutional detail matters because a victory in a chronicle does not explain how a garrison or community lives in the following season.

Ibn ʿUmar's report in al-Bukhārī 2331 states that the Prophet assigned the Khaybar lands to their Jewish cultivators so that they would work and cultivate them, taking half the yield. A related formulation appears at al-Bukhārī 4248. A short operative phrase in the Arabic is **«وَلَهُمْ شَطْرُ مَا خَرَجَ مِنْهَا»**. This is concrete evidence of a crop-sharing arrangement. It tells us something about the distribution of produce, and by implication the need to maintain agricultural work after conquest. It does not tell us the complete land register, the duration of every household's tenure, the legal status of every parcel, or the motives of each party. Nor does it prove a general policy toward all religious minorities in all later Islamic states.

The two Bukhārī passages should not be called two wholly independent contemporary documentary witnesses. Both transmit the report through the Ibn ʿUmar–Nāfiʿ line with different later narrators and slight wording variation. The distinction matters because *corroboration through related transmission lines* differs from independent material corroboration. Later jurists debated sharecropping, labour, fiscal rights and the conditions of such arrangements. The chapter on jurisprudence should show what problem later legal reasoning needed to resolve instead of presenting Khaybar as the unqualified source of a single doctrine of tenancy.

The case also corrects a one-dimensional account of public authority. Worship gave believers a moral understanding of their obligations, but it did not automatically determine the harvest, the proportions of produce, the appointment of collectors or the settlement of disputes. Those activities required terms, agents and procedures. A legal-historical *sharḥ* can explain these without portraying the Prophet's decisions as mere economic necessity: the reported choice is both a practical judgment under particular circumstances and an action that later jurists studied within a broader religious framework.

**Source and limit.** [Al-Bukhārī 2331](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2331) and [4248](https://sunnah.com/bukhari/64/283), both in the Ibn ʿUmar–Nāfiʿ transmission. The causal connection between cultivation and fiscal continuity is an inference from the agricultural and military setting, not a direct statement of the report. Further work: variants in al-Muwaṭṭaʾ and Muslim's collection, administrative reports and later juristic discussion of *musāqāt* and *muzāraʿa*. Those materials have not been comprehensively collated here.

## Dossier 5 — The *Muwaṭṭaʾ*: when teaching became a text without becoming motionless (chapters 27–29)

The transmitted works of early jurists were not published like modern single-edition books. A student heard a teacher, studied a written copy, sometimes read it back for approval, and passed on a certified recension. A teacher could continue to expand, omit or reorganize material in subsequent lessons. The resulting variants do not by themselves mean that the book had no author; nor do they mean that all surviving copies represent precisely one unchanged dictation. This is central to the emergence of Islamic scholarly authority: a ruling was preserved through human learning practices, and differences among those practices became material for further learned comparison.

Mālik b. Anas's *Muwaṭṭaʾ* is an unusually well-documented case. Ahmed El Shamsy's detailed study compares the surviving complete recensions of Yaḥyā b. Yaḥyā al-Laythī, Abū Muṣʿab al-Zuhrī and Ibn Bukayr with quotations of Mālik preserved in al-Shāfiʿī's *Kitāb al-Umm*. In one case about compensating an injury, the position remains almost identical while a verb describing earlier authorities' activity changes. In another, the vocabulary used in a reciprocal gift exchange varies by one word without apparently changing the legal reasoning. These are minor differences. But the examples concerning a rule's degree of scholarly acceptance matter more: phrases equivalent to “the rule in our view” and “the agreed rule among us” attribute different degrees of collective authority to a position. They are not interchangeable editorial ornament.

A particularly instructive example concerns Ibn ʿUmar's reported practice of sprinkling water in his eyes when performing the major ablution. Yaḥyā's recension preserves the practice without an explicit attached judgment by Mālik, while al-Shāfiʿī quotes Mālik as saying it was not accepted practice; Abū Muṣʿab and Ibn Bukayr preserve comparable statements that the action was not obligatory. El Shamsy's comparison supplies exact identifiers: *al-Umm* 8:696; the Yaḥyā recension no. 113; Abū Muṣʿab no. 124; Ibn Bukayr no. 128. This is an example where absence or presence of a teacher's comment can materially affect what a reader believes the teacher required. A lay reader selecting the most familiar recension might never see the interpretive problem.

A second example concerns chapter organization. El Shamsy shows that the chapter on testimony appears in markedly different positions within the Book of Judicial Rulings across the recensions. This is not evidence that one text's hadith statements were all invented; it demonstrates that the pedagogical arrangement of material also belonged to the history of transmission. A reader studying from chapter headings should therefore distinguish Mālik's teachings, later or concurrent editorial arrangement, and a school's retrospective classification of its authorities. The text's authority was produced and maintained by identifiable practices: reading, hearing, comparison, disputation, correction and regional teaching.

The historical consequence becomes clear through the expansion of legal schools. A school was not simply a student's uncritical reproduction of Mālik's or Abū Ḥanīfah's every opinion. Jurists compared precedent, authenticated reports, distinguished locally received practice from transmitted statement, and trained successors who could apply or criticize inherited reasoning. The *Muwaṭṭaʾ* recensions reveal an early layer of these processes; they cannot by themselves explain every later Mālikī development, still less the origin of all madhhabs. Nor is Muḥammad al-Shaybānī's work best treated as a straightforward equivalent of the three complete recensions: El Shamsy describes it more precisely as a partial critical commentary.

The article gives exact modern-edition identifiers and compares specific witnesses, including at least one relevant manuscript. ## Dossier 6 — Learning to act: al-Ghazālī, al-Qushayrī and the Shādhilī explanation of inward formation (chapters 9, 13, 32–33)

The Sīrah contains people praying, reciting, sheltering emigrants, sharing resources, resisting persecution, disputing wealth and accepting judgments. Later disciplines of spirituality were not already present in their later technical form. Nevertheless, the actions provided enduring questions: what makes an act of worship sincere? What habits allow a person to give while facing scarcity? Why can outward conformity coexist with vanity, anger or cruelty? To answer them we must distinguish (a) narrated practices and Qurʾānic demands, (b) the historical development of explicit psychological and pedagogical methods, and (c) later metaphysical interpretations of spiritual realization.

Al-Ghazālī's introductory plan in the *Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn* is a model of genuine intellectual integration. He divides the work into worship, ordinary dealings, destructive qualities and saving qualities. These are not four unrelated collections. Worship establishes recurring acts that orient the person to God; customary dealings expose the person to property, family and social obligations; destructive qualities name dispositions that can corrupt either activity; saving qualities identify the habits of attention, intention and love through which action acquires a proper spiritual quality. Al-Ghazālī's own distinction between outward acts and the inward activity of the heart is therefore an account of **how one moral agent participates in several sorts of obligation**. It does not mean that an eleventh-century psychological framework was explicitly formulated by every Companion.

In *al-Risālah al-Qushayriyyah*, the chapter on sincerity transmits Abū ʿAlī al-Daqqāq's distinction between guarding one's act against consideration of other people's approval and guarding it against self-admiration. This is more precise than the sentence “Sufis taught sincerity.” It identifies two different obstacles: one can behave well in order to be praised by others; one can also inwardly admire one's own religious achievement even in solitude. A teacher must therefore ask more than whether the observable act took place. This produces a practical method of examination, not merely a vocabulary.

Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh makes the relationship particularly concise in *al-Ḥikam*: **«الأَعْمالُ صُوَرٌ قَائِمَةٌ»**—acts are standing forms—while their animating reality is the secret of sincerity. The contrast is not between useless outward law and valuable inward experience. It is between an act considered only by its visible configuration and the act considered as deliberately directed toward God. A debt can be repaid outwardly while the agent seeks admiration for generosity; a person's prayer can conform to required forms while attention and intention remain disordered. Jurisprudence and spiritual formation therefore concern distinguishable properties of the **same** action. The statement must not be used to excuse violations of the outward obligations that it seeks to animate.

The historical continuity lies in practices and questions, not in asserting uninterrupted identity of institutional form. Early prayer, fasting, generosity and repentance are directly attested in Prophetic teaching. The *Iḥyāʾ*, the *Risālah* and later Shādhilī circles made methods of assessing and cultivating dispositions more explicit. Organized Sufi lineages, teaching relationships, litanies and endowed residences are developments with their own documentary histories. What persists through these changes is the problem of integrating outward conduct and inward purpose; the shape of its solution must be studied in each author rather than assumed universally identical.

Al-Qushayrī, *al-Risālah*, [chapter on *ikhlāṣ*](https://islamweb.net/ar/library/content/1087/142/). The later explanations are identified as retrospective spiritual readings of early practice, not unreported words in the mouths of the Companions.

## Dossier 7 — Prophetic inheritance and *walāyah*: do not collapse practice, doctrine and ontology (chapters 19, 32–33)

The finality of Muḥammad's prophethood does not mean that instruction, judgment, sanctification or learned transmission ceased at his death. But those continuities are not one undifferentiated inheritance. A judge issues rulings by reference to authoritative evidence and established procedures; a ḥadīth transmitter preserves reports under a discipline of criticism; a spiritual teacher guides repentance and remembrance; a metaphysician asks what makes prophetic knowledge and human perfection possible. The historical institutions that sustained these activities often overlapped, while their claims to authority differed. The Sunni–Shiʿi discussion of succession can be acknowledged where necessary without making that controversy the foundation of the entire spiritual history.

Al-Ghazālī and al-Qushayrī treat *walāyah* in a field of spiritual practice and sanctification. Ibn ʿArabī makes *walāyah* central to a larger theory of divine knowledge, prophetic inheritance and realization. Souad Hakim's analysis of *al-Futūḥāt* emphasizes that the way of spiritual attainment can be considered through human conduct under divine commands and through witnessing; she explicitly cautions against imposing a single mandatory ladder of stations on Ibn ʿArabī. Thus obedience and spiritual realization are connected, but the relation is not that an act automatically produces a metaphysical degree as a measurable result. The act is a site of divine address and human response; realization cannot be reduced to a behavioral checklist.

Qūnawī's opening treatment of *wujūd* in *Miftāḥ al-ghayb*, also incorporated into *al-Nuṣūṣ*, introduces another order of explanation. In William Chittick's translation and commentary, Qūnawī distinguishes the one *wujūd* of the Real from the multiplicity of its relations and loci of manifestation. The passage concerns how plurality and knowledge are intelligible without multiplying the Real as such. It does **not** straightforwardly answer the historical question of how a particular family in Madinah learned to pray, or whether a treaty clause was accepted by a particular tribe. To import this metaphysics directly into those narratives would confuse ontological interpretation with documentary history.

The expression *al-ḥaqīqah al-muḥammadiyyah* likewise needs historical differentiation. The revised Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy article on Ibn ʿArabī observes that he used the term in passing, while Farghānī offered its first detailed systematic explanation. Farghānī's exposition is connected to Qūnawī's teaching on Ibn al-Fāriḍ's *Naẓm al-sulūk*, first interpreted in Persian and then extensively reworked in Arabic. We must therefore not treat Farghānī's mature metaphysical language as a verbatim formulation of the first Muslims, nor even as the detailed terminology of every passage in Ibn ʿArabī himself. The historically sound account traces a chain of intellectual formulation; the theological and metaphysical account then assesses its arguments on their own terms.

One fruitful integrative question remains: how can a religious act be at once legally valid, ethically virtuous, spiritually sincere and understood by later thinkers as a locus of divine disclosure? These are different formal considerations of a single act, not four separate events. A competent commentary should start with the event's evidence; identify the legal and moral questions it raises; then reconstruct the later spiritual argument with its own premises and textual witnesses. There is room for a traditionalist cyclical interpretation of sacred time, but Guénon's historical cycles cannot be used to supply missing isnāds or infer undocumented causes of seventh-century decisions.

## Dossier 8 — International Islam: two regional mechanisms that must not be blended (chapters 38–40)

The spread of Islamic teaching through Africa and Asia was neither one centrally directed conquest nor a single wave of Sufi preaching. The processes differed with agriculture, settlement, trade, court patronage, writing systems and the density of scholarly institutions. Bengal and Melaka can be compared precisely because they challenge the assumption of a universal sequence in which a ruler converts, his subjects follow and Islamic institutions automatically appear.

In eastern Bengal, agrarian settlement, the extension of rice-growing into forest and delta environments, and the establishment of local religious institutions developed over long periods. Richard Eaton's study *The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier, 1204–1760* argues that political control, agrarian expansion and processes of Islamization did not follow identical frontiers or identical dates. This is a causal thesis to be evaluated against settlement patterns, inscriptions, grants and regional history—not a claim that cultivation mechanically generated religious belief. It invites the sharḥ to ask who cleared land, how local lineages acquired legitimacy, which religious institutions endured and what language people used for instruction.

Melaka was a maritime entrepôt rather than a delta agricultural frontier. Seasonal trade connected diverse merchants and resident communities; courts negotiated passage, customs and disputes, while teachers and families circulated religious practices and texts. The political endorsement of Islam increased some institutional opportunities, but cannot be assumed to have instantly converted every visitor or resident. A careful chapter therefore moves from the practical need for trust and settlement to the possibility of sustained instruction, then tests actual court and literary evidence. Arabic, Persian and Malay-language contact should be documented rather than inferred from a generic picture of trade.

These examples illuminate Hodgson's distinction between Islamic religious life and the broader Islamicate social world. The same route could transmit commodities, words, legal practices and devotional instruction, each by different agents and at different speeds. It does not follow that all such transmission can be labelled the same kind of religious conversion. The chronology and supporting evidence remain regional.

**Controls.** Richard Eaton, [*The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier*](https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/view?docId=ft067n99v9); A. C. S. Peacock, *Islam and the “Land Below the Winds”* and specialist studies of Melaka; maritime-historical scholarship on the Malay trading world. Only selected secondary studies have been checked here, so the critical apparatus should not claim a complete collation of Bengali or Malay inscriptions.

## Dossier 9 — Colonial institutions: the transformation of authority must be specified (chapters 43–45)

The transformation of Islamic legal and educational institutions under colonial and modern states cannot be explained by the word “decline.” Courts, schools, state budgets and codified statutes changed who had the right to settle disputes, how judgments were recorded, how jurists were trained and which bodies could enforce their conclusions. These developments occurred differently across the Ottoman domains, British India, Egypt, French North Africa and later nation-states.

A strong commentary distinguishes at least three relationships. First, a jurist giving an opinion (*fatwā*) is not identical to a judge issuing an enforceable decision (*qaḍāʾ*). Second, a large juristic literature containing rival opinions is not the same kind of instrument as a statute that selects a particular rule for officials to administer. Third, the practical scope of a religious obligation for an individual need not be coextensive with the jurisdiction allowed to a state court. These differences explain why an alteration in the state's judicial organization can transform religious authority without ending private study or devotion.

The Ottoman *Mecelle* (1869–1876) is one case in which selected Ḥanafī civil-law material was arranged into articles for courts. Its emergence should be studied alongside Tanzimat-era administration and the legal constraints of its own time, not treated as a complete codification of all sharīʿah or as an instant replacement of every older court. In British India, colonial courts' selection, translation and enforcement of legal opinions produced different institutional consequences. Deoband's curriculum and Aligarh's educational programme responded to overlapping but different questions: how to sustain religious scholarship and how to train persons for a new state and professional order. Those responses should be explained through their respective institutions rather than placed along an unexamined line from tradition to modernity.

For spiritual life the issue was likewise institutional. An endowed teacher, a mosque preacher, a state employee, a privately appointed shaykh and an author of a printed manual could reach students through different networks. Printing and later broadcasting made texts more accessible to people outside established teacher-student chains, while also altering the ways readers evaluated credentials. Religious learning did not uniformly disappear or thrive: its forms of supervision, funding and audience changed. The same distinction persists in digital communication today.

**Limits and later work.** This dossier establishes the variables a case study must measure: jurisdiction, training, revenue, procedure, enforcement, linguistic medium and access to instruction. Detailed Ottoman archives, provincial colonial records and specific madrasa syllabi have not been collated as a cross-regional data set here. No single political explanation should be applied uniformly to all societies or periods.

## Three rounds of independent editorial self-assessment

### Round 1 — Narrative continuity

**Test.** Does every major turn grow out of a concrete historical problem? The Sīrah sequence (1–20) now links household instruction, unequal protection, asylum, pledges, migration, domestic worship, common defence and treaty relations. Chapters 21–26 explain how conquest created administration, settlement and revenue needs, and how those pressures led to new centres of government and learning. Chapters 27–33 connect transmission, jurisprudence, institutions, philosophical method and spiritual formation. The regional chapters (34–42) each begin from a distinctive configuration, rather than one unexplained wave of Islamic expansion.

**Result.** Improvement is substantial, but chapters 1–6 necessarily use different chronological registers and remain panoramic. Chapters 38–42 are partly simultaneous regional histories, so transitions must be made explicit in a timeline. The contemporary chapter is a starting framework rather than an account of every country.

### Round 2 — Explanatory and evidentiary sufficiency

**Test.** Can the commentary identify witnesses appropriate to its claims, and avoid using a later interpretation as direct evidence for a seventh-century event? The four inherited Sīrah dossiers contain precise textual anchors, and the five added dossiers give controlled comparisons for jurisprudence, learned institutions, philosophical theology, Bengal/Melaka and colonial law. The regional sharḥ chapters cite specific published scholarship, with source distinctions and known uncertainties.

**Result.** The apparatus is improved but not manuscript-critical. A named scholar's secondary reconstruction is not a substitute for reproducing and collating primary passages. Source density is uneven: the Indian Ocean and Bengal cases have better modern controls than the early West African and Safavid sections. Political and sectarian differences are described proportionately rather than allowed to dominate the narrative.

### Round 3 — Narrative density, spiritual coherence and teaching value

**Test.** Does the matn invite reading, or merely pack facts? Does the sharḥ reconstruct mechanisms or attach author lists? Are later spiritual doctrines identified as later, without severing them from the practical religious questions they address? Several weak transitions from edition 1.2 were replaced by episodes of money, movement, teaching, cultivation and institutional judgment. The metaphysical and spiritual sections distinguish the disciplines of inward reform from the later systematization of the Muhammadan Reality.

**Result.** The expanded chapters can now be studied as episodes and compared through the dossiers. However, some new sharḥ paragraphs remain too compressed for specialist verification; the edition still needs annotated maps, dated manuscript cases and classroom testing. Its most important test is not whether it has 46 titles but whether a reader can explain the difference between, for example, a harbour's commercial protections and a community's transmission of faith.

## Overall editorial judgment and next bounded steps

The 46-chapter architecture is **provisionally suitable for an advanced narrative study**, not a final proof of every historical or metaphysical claim. The opening Sīrah sequence is more concrete than in version 0.6; the later world is no longer squeezed into seven general surveys. Source controls and methodological limits are visible in the sharḥ and dossiers rather than interrupting the matn.

Remaining tasks are bounded: (1) establish parallel Arabic witnesses for al-Ḥudaybiyyah and the Medinan agreements; (2) collate one juristic transmission across recensions and downstream manuals; (3) trace a named Shādhilī or Akbarian teaching chain through dated books and attestations; (4) add dated material evidence for one West African and one Southeast Asian case; (5) obtain historical and spiritual-studies reader feedback. The manuscript should be retested after those changes; repeated self-audits by the same author cannot substitute for independent source expertise.

## Revision 1.3 — final structural correction

A final audit identified the missing history of al-Andalus and the Maghrib. The expanded outline consequently has **46**, not 45, matn–sharḥ pairs. New chapter 35 addresses Córdoba, the taifa periods, North African interventions, the Nasrid kingdom, the continuity of legal and intellectual learning, and the aftermath of Granada. The change is substantive: it restores the western Islamic lands as historical agents rather than treating them as a footnote to the eastern caliphates.



## Revision 1.4 — source-critical validation and remaining deficiencies

### What was directly checked

- The extended al-Bukhārī narrative of Abū Bakr's protection, courtyard recitation and migration (no. 3905): the causal claim is about the limits of kin protection and public recitation, not a quantified conversion movement.
- The transmitted Medinan *kitāb* in Ibn Hishām, compared against scholarly discussions of other recensions: its explicit compensation, ransom and alliance clauses are the evidentiary centre. No universal claim about the membership of all Jewish groups is made.
- Al-Bukhārī 2731–2732: the clause affecting Abū Jandal and Umm Salamah's counsel are integrated into the pilgrimage-treaty narrative. The long report is one transmitted complex rather than multiple independent documentary records.
- Al-Bukhārī 2331 and 4248: the Khaybar cultivation terms are quoted in short excerpt and their shared Ibn ʿUmar–Nāfiʿ transmission identified.
- Ahmed El Shamsy's published comparison of the *Muwaṭṭaʾ* recensions: the example about the washing of eyes in *ghusl* and differences in status-phrases were checked in his documented collation and given their printed witness identifiers. This is **not** an independent manuscript collation.
- Al-Ghazālī's introduction to the *Iḥyāʾ*, al-Qushayrī's chapter on *ikhlāṣ*, and an aphorism of Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh were inspected as separate works. The Akbarian development was verified through specialist published translations and studies, not direct comparison of Arabic witnesses.

### Four-pass critique

**Narrative test.** The matn still proceeds through 46 coherent episodes without citation interruptions. The strongest case-based chapters are 10–12, 16–17 and 27–28. The later accounts of philosophical argument, Persianate networks, East Africa and modernity remain too broad for all their mechanisms to be established at this granularity. The revised matns of chapters 16 and 17 now contain a visible action or economic arrangement rather than only a conclusion about institutions.

**Evidence test.** The nine dossiers separate explicit words of a source, the historical mechanism inferred from them, and claims that need independent corroboration. They do not claim that two passages from the same transmission line establish independent witnesses. Lecker's interpretation of Medinan participation and El Shamsy's recension comparisons are attributed to those authors. Manuscript facsimiles and every extant chain were not collated, so *critical edition* here denotes a critical **study edition**, not a definitive reconstructed archetype.

**Conceptual/spiritual test.** Practice, disposition, pedagogical institution and metaphysical formulation are distinguished. The *Iḥyāʾ*, *al-Risālah*, *al-Ḥikam*, *al-Futūḥāt*, *Miftāḥ al-ghayb* and Farghānī's commentary are neither assumed contemporaneous nor collapsed into one terminology. The philosophy of *walāyah* requires extended textual analysis beyond this source dossier and is therefore marked as provisional.

**Compression test.** The matns remain navigable and generally narrative; the commentaries have received primary-based amendments rather than an added sequence of unrelated author names. Some repetition between a chapter sharḥ and its corresponding dossier is intentional for standalone consultation, but the dossiers are more detailed and identify precisely which claims remain unproved. A full editorial review should use a second independent reader for flow and source checking.

### Priority unresolved tasks before a definitive critical edition

1. Obtain and compare the full Ibn Hishām and Abū ʿUbayd recensions of the Medinan document from specified printed editions and, where accessible, manuscript witnesses; make a diplomatic Arabic clause apparatus including omissions and word differences.
2. Collate the al-Ḥudaybiyyah narrative across al-Bukhārī, Ibn Hishām/Ibn Isḥāq and early maghāzī transmissions; distinguish independently transmitted details from common source strata.
3. Produce original-script parallel columns for the cited Mālik variants using the Yaḥyā, Abū Muṣʿab and Ibn Bukayr recensions and the *Umm*, with exact page and report identifiers and image verification.
4. Reconstruct one limited conceptual argument in Ibn ʿArabī–Qūnawī–Farghānī through the primary Arabic texts, separating doctrinal lineage from retrospective interpretations of the Sīrah.
5. Have specialists independently review the Arabic text extracts, chronological claims and theological descriptions, and record corrigenda. No internal self-audit is a substitute for that review.

**Result:** The v1.4 historical explanation is more thoroughly evidenced at six pivotal transitions, but the full 46-chapter scope remains an advanced teaching synthesis with selected critical dossiers, not a completed primary-text critical edition of Islamic history.
