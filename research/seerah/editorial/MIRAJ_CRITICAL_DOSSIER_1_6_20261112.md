# The Miʿrāj: Comprehensive Station, Differentiated Prophetic Wisdom, and Return
## Source-critical and Akbarian analysis for Sīrah Book II, Chapter 11

**Research dossier · 12 November 2026.** A connected analysis of the extant Qurʾānic and core ṣaḥīḥ narrations, Qūnawī's and Ibn ʿArabī's spiritual interpretations, Lings's reading, and the source-indexed early Sufi themes preserved by al-Sulamī. This dossier is **not** a complete collation of the 56 sayings in Colby's translation of al-Sulamī or a final reconciliation of every canonical hadith variant.

## I. The primary events must be separately established

### Qurʾānic foundation

Qurʾān 17:1 speaks explicitly of God's taking His servant at night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque, to show him some of God's signs. The grammar emphasizes the divine subject of the journey and the servanthood of its human recipient. It should not be turned directly into a detailed numbered map of the heavens. Qurʾān 53:1–18 includes visionary language, an approach, a second sighting and the Lote Tree at the utmost boundary; the referent of the sighting and its relation to vision of God or Gabriel were debated in early exegetical accounts.

**Limits:** The date of the journey, the relationship of individual versions of the night journey and ascent, and the nature of vision cannot be settled from 17:1 alone. Nor should later reports about Gabriel's wings, the limits of an angel's capacity or particular mystical garments be presented as part of one certain verbatim Qurʾānic narration.

### Principal connected hadith: Muslim 162a (Anas)

The report includes al-Burāq, prayer at Bayt al-Maqdis, the choice of milk rather than wine and Gabriel's interpretation of that choice, the requests for admission through successive heavenly gates, recognitions from earlier prophets, the Lote Tree, and the original burden of fifty daily prayers subsequently reduced, through dialogue with Moses, to five while the reward remains equivalent to fifty.

[Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 162a](https://sunnah.com/muslim/1/316) is the primary narrative base. [Bukhārī 349](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:349) differs in sequencing and compression; one should not combine their detailed sequences into a synthetic “literal transcript.” Versions of the Prophet's journey vary in their placement of the prescription and ascent details. The five prayers are settled in the received Sunni narrative; what different narrators omit or foreground remains subject to analysis.

### Seven prophetic encounters

| Heaven in Muslim 162a | Prophet(s) encountered | What the report explicitly supplies | Interpretive research question, **not** automatic scriptural attribution |
|---:|---|---|---|
| First | Ādam | Greeting, reception; in other reports differentiation of souls | Adamic gathering of human possibility and descendants; how does bodily descent relate to universal return? |
| Second | ʿĪsā and Yaḥyā | Greeting and welcome | Breath, spiritual life, purity and special prophetic relations; do not read two prophetic stations as a fixed rank |
| Third | Yūsuf | His beauty is mentioned and he welcomes the visitor | Sensible beauty, imaginal form and recognition of perfection |
| Fourth | Idrīs | Welcome; Qurʾān 19:57 on elevation is recalled | Science, elevation and transformations of knowledge |
| Fifth | Hārūn | Welcome | Kindness, priestly responsibility, and interpersonal care; precise assignments vary by author |
| Sixth | Mūsā | Welcome and the further encounter during descent | Legislating for a community, counsel based on lived experience and rights of worshippers |
| Seventh | Ibrāhīm | Welcome and proximity to the inhabited House | Primordial patriarchal form, the House, Abrahamic continuity and perfected hospitality |

The meeting in a particular heaven **does not establish that the corresponding prophet's entire reality is permanently restricted to that level**. Qūnawī, *al-Nuṣūṣ*, Text Nineteen (online witness pp. 259–260, Gnamma QUN-NU-019-G), makes precisely this interpretive argument: the disclosed relationship belongs to the condition, attribute and vision in which the meeting appears. It cannot function as a complete spiritual census. His claimed further ascent narratives and reported early collections still require independent documentary verification.

### The vision: a genuine primary-source difference

**Muslim 176a** transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās that the Prophet saw **with his heart** (*raʾāhu bi-qalbihi*). [Primary text](https://sunnah.com/muslim/1/341).

**Muslim 177a** reports ʿĀʾishah answering Masrūq's question and rejecting the claim of ordinary ocular sight of God on this occasion; she relates the relevant Qurʾānic sighting to Gabriel, whom she reports the Prophet saw in his original form twice. [Primary text](https://sunnah.com/muslim:177a).

This is not a controversy that can be omitted in a spiritually serious account. A later spiritual author may expound “vision of God” as inner witnessing, unveilings, or a special beatific mode, but must specify what kind of claim is made and whether it is compatible with the particular Companion testimony being cited. Reporting both witnesses is not the same as declaring all accounts factually equivalent; it records the real exegetical and theological problem.

### The creaturely limit

The Qurʾān itself emphasizes “His servant” (17:1); no spiritual interpretation can erase that designation. In the Akbarian framework, higher apprehension does not make the historical Prophet an independently divine hypostasis. The summit of nearness is experienced by the servant God has elevated. Some descriptions of proximity concern spiritual rank and cannot be converted into a physical measurement of God's location.

## II. Why the seven heavens cannot be made a flat ladder

In the *Fuṣūṣ*, Ibn ʿArabī assigns named prophetic wisdoms to different prophets; these are modal distinctions in the ways divine reality is disclosed. The **Muhammadan comprehensiveness** is not a thesis that the previous prophets each possessed one virtue only, subsequently combined like parts in a machine. Different earlier prophetic realities may contain many perfections; their assignment to a particular heaven, under a particular disclosure, need not define their total rank.

Qūnawī's *al-Nuṣūṣ* Text Nineteen presents relations among spiritual origins, the Universal Pen/First Intellect, the Preserved Tablet, throne, Lote Tree and imaginal manifestations. These correlations belong to the author's metaphysical vocabulary; they are not modern astronomy or a physical coordinate system. Nor is the number of heavens a warrant to force every social institution into an exact seven-stage cosmic analogue.

Lings, *The Book of Certainty*, chapter II (printed pp. 24–25), similarly says the prophetic presence in a particular heaven represents a characteristic or a spiritual plenitude under one consideration. His interpretation of the Light of Muhammad above differentiated heavenly degrees is a Traditionalist spiritual exposition, not a direct hadith paraphrase.

The central philosophical lesson is **right differentiation without spatial confinement**. In a comprehensive human being, knowledge, worship, social care, political judgment and spiritual contemplation can be genuine and distinct without constituting disconnected personalities.

## III. Gabriel, the Lote Tree and the limit of mediation

Gabriel is the angel who conveys revelation and accompanies the ascent in the principal narrated sequence. The recurring question at each heavenly gate—who is with him and whether he has been sent for—emphasizes divine authorization, not the Prophet's private command over the heavens. This is a ritualized narrative form and should not be used to infer an independently self-powered cosmic ascent.

The Lote Tree and the visionary language in Qurʾān 53 offer a limit of ordinary description. In some Sufi interpretations Gabriel is a symbol for a particular mediation of revealed knowledge, while the Prophet's degree of nearness exceeds the form under which such mediation normally operates. But one must distinguish:
- what the Qurʾān says about the tree, sign and perception;
- what an authenticated narrative explicitly attributes to Gabriel;
- what al-Sulamī's early Sufi masters or Ibn ʿArabī infer symbolically.

A statement such as “Gabriel could go no further or he would burn” should never be presented without its actual source, textual status and interpretive type. The full Colby edition of al-Sulamī, including its original Arabic, has **not** been directly collated here.

## IV. Al-Sulamī's early mystical voices: thematic census with access limits

Abū ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Sulamī's *Laṭāʾif al-Miʿrāj*, translated and edited by Frederick S. Colby, *The Subtleties of the Ascension* (Fons Vitae, 2006), is **pre-Akbarian**, not a treatise of Ibn ʿArabī. Its 56 attributed sayings give an indispensable earlier history of how the journey was construed through prophetic uniqueness, spiritual etiquette, light, vision, secrets, love and the insufficiency of language.

The accessible published table of contents provides specifically titled sections—**Bearing the Vision in the Heart** (Ibn ʿAṭāʾ, p. 37), **Propriety of Witnessing** (anonymous, p. 41), **Seeing with God's Eyes** (al-Ḥallāj, p. 52), **The Ineffable Limit** (al-Junayd, p. 60), **Moses and Muhammad** (anonymous, p. 73), **Divine and Prophetic Attributes** (Abū Saʿīd al-Qurashī, p. 81), **A Secret Between Lovers** (Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, p. 82), **Abiding in the Presence** (al-Jurayrī, p. 86), **The Benefit of Ritual Prayer** (al-Ḥallāj, p. 88), and **After the Night Journey** (anonymous, p. 145). These are verified **titles and reported attributions from the contents**, not a claim that the complete interior arguments were read. [Publisher table of contents](https://lyon.ecampus.com/subtleties-ascension-lataif-almiraj-early/bk/9781887752787).

Colby's introduction, as cited in scholarly research, identifies four broad families of interpretation: unique rank, investiture with light, divine vision and ineffability. That taxonomy is a guide for full-text examination, **not** an exhaustive paraphrase of all 56 individual sayings.

The **urgent next scholarly check** is the Arabic/translation page-by-page collation of these short items and their attribution to early masters. This task remains openly incomplete because the full book is not accessible through the present research sources.

## V. Ibn ʿArabī's spiritual ascent: different accounts, not one

James W. Morris translates and studies *Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya* chapter 367 and compares it with *Kitāb al-Isrāʾ*, chapter 167 and *Risālat al-Anwār*. The publicly available PDF is an **unrevised prepublication version** of a later corrected journal publication: *Journal of the American Oriental Society* 107 (1987), 629–652, and 108 (1988), 63–77. Its bibliographic notice should be retained in citations. [Morris source](https://ibnarabisociety.org/wp-content/uploads/PDFs/Morris_The-spiritual-ascension.pdf), introduction pp. 1–8.

Morris observes a recurrent relation between ontological description and actual spiritual experience. Ibn ʿArabī treats the journey as a **complete movement of ascent and return** (*rujūʿ*); in his exposition a spiritually realized person may return to earthly life without displaying theatrical signs of sanctity. That is not merely the ethical injunction to be humble. It is a claim about the ability to witness the divine Names through the differentiated creaturely order while carrying out responsibilities.

The return is more important for a principial Sīrah than treating the ascent as a one-way escape from ordinary human dependence. A saint's inner process cannot be made identical to the Prophet's uniquely commissioned historical journey, but the Akbarian doctrine of inheritance concerns graded participation in the complete pattern. Nor does a later saint become a renewed law-giving prophet.

### Moses as the witness to practical experience

In Morris's translation (Futūḥāt ch. 367, section discussing the sixth heaven, around PDF pp. 42–43), Ibn ʿArabī gives Moses's counsel a particularly precise sense: the benefit of knowledge gained through **direct experience** (*dhawq*, *mubāshara*). Moses knows, from his historical dealings with his community, the human cost of repeated obligation and counsels the Prophet to seek a lighter prescription.

This is not an opposition between Moses's practical knowledge and Muḥammad's higher station. It is a striking representation of an elevated encounter in which the knowledge of one prophet's community informs provision for another. Its source is the canonical narrative itself; Ibn ʿArabī's explanation of *dhawq* supplies an additional spiritual interpretation. The Prophet's ﷺ return with five prescribed prayers actualizes the higher gift in a form repeated by ordinary human bodies.

### The invisibility of the realized return

Morris, near the discussion of the “returners” in his introduction and notes to chapter 367, recounts Ibn ʿArabī's reading that the Prophet showed no spectacular altered outward form upon returning from the ascent. The interpretive point is a higher **abiding in God while present to creation** rather than an endlessly visible ecstasy. This is not evidence for a detailed report of the Prophet's private emotional state at each moment after the ascent.

The comparison between an adept's *fanāʾ* and *baqāʾ* must be made in the author's own vocabulary and context. Extinction of self-assertion before God does not grant absolute social power, negate creatureliness, dissolve duties or authorize innovation in revealed obligations.

## VI. From vertical nearness to the actual prayer

The number of prayers is one of the event's clearest horizontal mediations. A revelation of extraordinary nearness yields five repeated obligations through which believers structure the day. The human subject bows and prostrates, employs bodily faculties and words, and joins congregations while remaining responsible to children, family and livelihood.

This illustrates the relation of **the ultimate and proximate ends**:
- The ultimate direction of prayer is worship of God and receptivity to His guidance.
- Its formative work involves attention, humility, remembrance, repetition, restraint and embodied action.
- Its communal effects may include regular gathering, common instructional vocabulary and mutual responsibility, but these effects are neither its primary end nor automatic guarantees of virtue.

The hadith of the **crying child** (Bukhārī 707–710, especially 709) provides an exact example of realized worship meeting a human claim without being abandoned. The Prophet shortens a prayer he had intended to prolong when a child cries and the mother's distress is at stake. The Miʿrāj and that later practical response should be read together *thematically*, not claimed as a single demonstrable historical cause of one another.

## VII. The intimate and the majestic: *uns*, awe, silence and love

An ascent toward the hidden realities is not adequately explained as an increase in spatial altitude. The early Sufi headings concerning the secret between lovers, the propriety of witnessing and ineffability suggest a much more delicate spiritual epistemology. The mature interpretation must distinguish:
- intimate familiarity with God's generosity (*uns*) from irreverent familiarity with God as an object;
- awe (*hayba*) and creaturely inadequacy from psychological paralysis;
- love from the identification of any creaturely affection as automatic divine unveiling;
- silence caused by the limits of words from the absence of possible knowledge;
- knowledge through direct experience from claims of competence in unrelated law or history.

Without direct inspection of the full al-Sulamī corpus, these are **research questions and broad pre-Akbarian themes**, not specific textual claims about what each named master asserted.

Al-Mahāʾimī's four dimensions of the Prophet (body, religious/legal, luminous spiritual, inward heart-centred) help orient the differences between what can be witnessed, taught, imitated and inwardly realized. The ascent does not discredit the body. It completes its significance under the order of servanthood.

## VIII. Hermeneutical safeguards and unresolved controversy

1. **Vision:** preserve Ibn ʿAbbās (Muslim 176a, vision in the heart) and ʿĀʾishah (Muslim 177a, rejection of ordinary ocular vision and explanation of Gabriel sightings). Do not harmonize without stating the terms of the proposed reconciliation.
2. **Chronology:** do not assert a single undisputed calendar date or make the Miʿrāj the direct event causing the ʿAqabah pledges.
3. **Bodily event versus later spiritual analogy:** the historical event's actuality in the received narrative is distinct from Ibn ʿArabī's accounts of the saints' inner journeys. A symbolic reading need not deny the historical bodily claim, and it should not erase it through reinterpretation without argument.
4. **Muhammadan rank:** not proof of an independent divine essence or of equality between a later realized saint and the Prophet.
5. **Concerning the other prophets:** assign them neither fixed spiritual limitations nor a rank based solely on their temporary placement in a particular narrative vision.
6. **Qurʾān–Sunnah–devotional layers:** keep revelation, ṣaḥīḥ hadith, later historical narratives and Sufi statements separately attributed. Avoid silently elevating poetic praise or unverified hadith to Qurʾānic status.
7. **Claims of an inaccessible inward state:** narrate what the accessible record gives; use *possible* higher interpretation explicitly as interpretation and remain willing to say “not established.”

## IX. A completed historical and principial argument

The Prophet's ﷺ journey is received as divine initiative upon a servant, traversing a cosmos in which different prophets appear under different determinations. Its summit reveals the limited reach of routine human perception and language, while its return conveys a repeated embodied obligation. Qūnawī's nonfixed heavenly correspondence, al-Sulamī's early mystical themes and Ibn ʿArabī's emphasis on return each illuminate a different aspect, and none is permitted to overwrite the primary report.

The complete movement makes intelligible the central task of the Sīrah: **the comprehensive prophetic orientation is not the abolition of finite human life. It becomes historically manifest through actual worship, instruction, right judgment, loving attention and the exercise of responsibility.** A later person can participate through true yet graded modes of following, but no later discipline, saintly state or public office reproduces the Prophet's final commission. The vertical is not exhausted by its social results; the social order is a genuine medium in which souls can be formed toward the vertical.
