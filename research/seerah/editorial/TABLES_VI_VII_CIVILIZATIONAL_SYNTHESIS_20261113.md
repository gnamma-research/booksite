### Book VI synthesis
Islamic civilization was not sustained by one continuous dynasty, political border or language. Teachers used mosques, households, madrasas and books; rulers financed institutions or imposed restrictions; merchants travelled through ports; jurists read and adjudicated cases. Each depended on different kinds of competence and authority. The common inheritance travelled through these differentiated arrangements, not because every arrangement embodied the same doctrine or virtue.

These cases show the importance of agency at an intermediate scale. The ruler Ghāzān's public conversion could affect court policy but did not convert all subjects by decree. A scholar such as Aḥmad Bābā could continue teaching after an invasion yet lose books and freedom. In Bengal, agriculture and religion interacted but neither dictated the other's inner meaning. Ottoman courts could preserve invaluable evidence while exercising oppressive power. The true lesson is found by comparing particular actions, institutions and their consequences rather than deducing an inevitable moral progress.

The Akbarian concept of a comprehensive Muhammadan inheritance offers a *later theological interpretation* of the relation between different lawful disciplines and spiritual ends. It is not a substitute for documentary proof of any single historical event. Philological, legal, administrative, spiritual and economic claims should be tested according to their appropriate standards of evidence.

### Book VII synthesis
Industrial power, international trade, colonial jurisdiction, print, mass education and new states developed through different historical processes. One colonially adjudicated family waqf dispute makes the shift in legal authority visible: a deed and juristic disagreement entered a court system empowered to interpret and enforce rules under its own standards, followed by statutory alteration. In education, Deoband and Aligarh organized resources and teaching for distinct purposes, while printed books changed the relation between source texts and the teachers who explained them.

The newest technologies make the distinction between access and realization especially clear. Search and manuscript comparison can greatly assist scholars and students, yet confidence in an edition requires actual witnesses; mastery requires instruction and practice; righteous action requires still other dispositions. The full Muhammadan exemplar joins aspects of human life in one authoritative prophetic life, but later individuals and institutions inherit differentiated responsibilities rather than a new prophetic commission.

The comparison between the medieval and the modern should remain open to evidence: neither assuming that every new instrument is a loss nor reducing the value of a spiritual tradition to technical efficiency. Human relationships, learned methods, just institutions and living worship remain the sites where inherited guidance is understood and acted upon.

## Source policy and follow-up
- **Event evidence:** Qurʾānic and ḥadīth sources for the Prophet; inscriptions, legal decisions, manuscript histories, court registers, contemporary accounts and documented institutional histories for later cases.
- **Source limit:** A cited reconstruction identifies a usable starting witness; not every primary manuscript or transcript was freshly collated for this supplement.
- **Interpretive layer:** Akbarian, Traditionalist and philosophical readings are named later perspectives, not invented motives attributed to historical agents.
- **Outstanding:** independent specialist review, full witness-level verification, cross-browser visual QA and completion of the broader claim ledger.
