# Sīrah and the Making of Islamic Civilization

## Narrative prose revision 1.4.1 — 46 matns and 46 shurūḥ

**Editorial status:** Stage-I source-critical integration: 46 narrative matns, 46 paired shurūḥ, nine detailed evidence dossiers and a renewed limitations audit. Selected primary texts and published recension comparisons have been closely examined, but the manuscript witness collation required for a full critical edition remains incomplete. The matn carries the narrative without source citations; the sharḥ carries evidence, variants, historical explanations and later spiritual interpretations. This is not a manuscript-critical edition of the primary Sīrah texts. The Sunni historical narrative is the organizing framework, while Shiʿi perspectives are represented fairly at consequential points without disproportionate polemics.

The recurring questions are how guidance forms persons; how persons form a community; how the community sustains law, learning and worship across generations; and how those practices travel, change or survive in wider civilizations. They are questions for the reader to follow through the historical material—not slogans to be repeated at the end of each chapter.

**Prose revision (9 November 2026).** This update replaces reader-directed and self-referential sentences with direct historical and analytical statements. It does not add a new manuscript collation. The original 1.4 text remains preserved in `source-edition-1.4.md`.

# Book I — The world into which the mission came

## 01. Prophetic beginnings: guidance addressed to human life

**Matn.** Ādam received knowledge, accepted responsibility, erred and repented. Nūḥ called a people attached to their accustomed ways; Ibrāhīm left inherited worship and placed his household under a covenant with God. Through Ismāʿīl and Isḥāq the prophetic family came to be remembered in Arabia and the Levant. Yūsuf's enslavement and eventual authority brought the family of Yaʿqūb into Egypt; Mūsā led the Israelites out of servitude and taught them a law ordering both worship and their dealings with one another. Dāwūd and Sulaymān united prophetic authority with kingship, while later messengers challenged rulers and communities that had neglected their obligations. These lives were transmitted as a history of the same divine summons meeting very different human circumstances. A household, a people in bondage, a settled kingdom and a scattered community each required guidance suited to its condition. By the time of Muḥammad, the stories of earlier prophets were already known in communities across the Near East. His proclamation would renew their central summons while addressing the particular claims of sanctuary, kinship and power in Arabia.

**Sharḥ.** The Qurʾān recounts Ādam's responsibility, Nūḥ's warning, Ibrāhīm's rejection of idols, Yūsuf's enslavement and eventual authority, and Mūsā's liberation and legislation (2:124–129; 7:59–93; 11:25–99; 19:41–58). These accounts affirm worship and moral accountability but neither date Ādam nor supply a continuous dynastic chronology. Jewish and Christian chronologies assign different dates to early patriarchs; those calculations do not establish dates accepted by Muslims. The prophets addressed households, oppressed peoples, ruling courts and settled communities under different historical conditions.

Muḥammad challenged inherited worship in Makkah as Ibrāhīm had challenged the idols of his people. In Madinah he judged disputes, organized collective obligations and taught law, but the institutions of his community differed from those of the Israelites under Mūsā. The prophetic missions shared a summons to worship God and act justly without reproducing identical political arrangements.

Jewish and Christian communities transmitted scriptural memories through families, worship, scribes, sanctuaries, teaching and translated texts. Exile, imperial administration, trade and the movement of teachers carried these traditions through different languages and settlements. Their documented histories explain how earlier accounts of prophecy remained known in the Near East and Arabia; they do not establish a continuous genealogy of political institutions from Ibrāhīm to seventh-century Makkah, nor do they constitute a material cause of revelation.

## 02. Land, settlement and the political worlds of the Near East

**Matn.** Along the Nile and the Tigris–Euphrates, farming communities learned to organize labour around rivers, store crops and support towns whose officials did not work the fields. Egypt and Mesopotamia developed kingship, record keeping, taxation, temples and armies; merchants and pastoral groups connected them to settlements beyond their direct rule. The Levant lay between these larger powers, with cultivable valleys, upland villages, coastal ports and routes toward Arabia. Its towns could prosper through exchange and suffer when armies demanded tribute or fought for the same territory. During the second millennium BCE Egyptian and other imperial powers contested Syria and Canaan; around 1200 BCE several palace-based political systems weakened or disappeared. New polities—including Phoenician cities, Aramaean kingdoms and the emerging kingdoms of Israel and Judah—developed amid the older fields, roads and sanctuaries. This matters to the later religious history because the inhabitants of these places did not encounter prophecy in an institutional vacuum. Their rulers levied resources, their households held land and debts, and their sanctuaries ordered communal life. The claims of the prophets addressed people embedded in these relationships.

**Sharḥ.** A settlement became a town through a combination of harvests, craft specialization, surplus storage and the ability to mobilize labour. Record keeping and royal courts made durable administration possible, but also allowed rulers to collect taxes, conscript workers and concentrate land. The Levant was especially exposed because competing states could reach it by land and sea. A change of empire therefore altered not just a ruler's name but security, tribute, local elites and the movement of goods. Pastoral groups and traders continued to cross ecological zones, negotiate protection and carry goods along routes beyond continuous state control. This provides a social setting for the later problems of kingship and justice in Israelite history.

River valleys supported offices that recorded irrigation, crops and levies, while travellers through arid corridors relied on negotiated access to wells and passage. Both demand cooperation, but the relevant political powers differ: a canal requires maintenance, surveying and control over fields; a caravan needs hosts, agreements and knowledge of routes. Makkah's sanctuary operated through local ritual protections and clan agreements, not the palace administration that governed many Mesopotamian cities. Egyptian and Mesopotamian rulers employed centralized offices, while many Arabian clans depended on negotiated protection and local alliances; both arrangements regulated recurring disputes in different ways. It also prevents the early chapters from functioning as a tour of famous ancient civilizations unrelated to the Sīrah.

Religious instruction could travel more readily than the fiscal records and irrigation systems tied to a particular territory. An irrigation register belongs to a particular land and administration; a remembered command, prayer formula or legal text can be taught beyond that locality. The latter still needs institutions—teachers, language, copies, patronage—but can survive a change of ruler. Judean communities maintained religious teaching after losing political sovereignty; centuries later, Muslim scholars circulated books and instruction across dynastic borders.

The evidence comes from separately dated archaeological and written corpora—Uruk administration, Egyptian records, second-millennium diplomatic texts, and later Iron Age inscriptions. It does not verify the chronology of Ādam or Nūḥ. Nor does resemblance between ancient caravan systems and Meccan alliances prove an unbroken institutional inheritance across two millennia. The narrower, useful connection is that religious instruction regularly met structures of property, dependence and political authority. The loss of Judah’s monarchy and sanctuary made the continued teaching of communal obligations under foreign rule a practical historical problem.

## 03. Israel and Judah: kingship, destruction and life beyond a kingdom

**Matn.** In the Israelite remembrance, Dāwūd and Sulaymān ruled a people responsible for worship and justice; prophets later recalled both rulers and subjects to the covenant. In the Iron Age Levant, the kingdoms of Israel and Judah occupied lands contested by stronger neighbours. Assyria conquered Israel's capital, Samaria, in 722/721 BCE. A century later Babylon imposed its power on Judah, took Jerusalem in 597 and destroyed its temple in 587/586 BCE, deporting members of the population while others remained. People who had associated worship with land, king and sanctuary now had to maintain their identity in dispersed communities. Persian conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE enabled a restored temple at Jerusalem, but did not bring all Judeans home. Priestly service, teaching of the Torah, festivals and household observances sustained Jewish communities under rulers of different languages and religions. During subsequent centuries Jewish communities would live from Mesopotamia to the Mediterranean and eventually in several Arabian settlements. By the Prophet's lifetime, Jews in Yathrib participated in local alliances and cultivation as well as scriptural traditions. Their presence belonged to a long history of Jewish life outside independent Jewish kingship, though the origins of particular Arabian groups were diverse and are not securely reconstructed.

**Sharḥ.** The transition from monarchy to dispersion changes the central historical problem. When the temple could no longer function and the king no longer secured the territory, continuity depended more heavily on scribal teaching, kin relations, calendar, prayer, law and ritual practice. The return under Persian supremacy restored a centre but not a self-sufficient sovereign state. This helps explain how identifiable Jewish communities could persist through Greek, Roman and Sasanian changes of rule. Jewish communities maintained religious institutions in Arabia, but the surviving evidence does not trace every family's ancestry to the Babylonian exile.

The changing medium of religious continuity is the decisive bridge. Royal patronage can maintain a central sanctuary; a dispersed community must teach children, preserve forms of worship, settle questions internally and recognize people of the same religious tradition elsewhere. Some of these activities developed over long periods, and their exact early chronology is contested. The conceptual connection to Islam's later history is nevertheless concrete: after the caliphate fragmented, Muslim communities too relied upon learning, law and travel beyond the reach of one political centre. Judean communities transmitted scripture through priests and scribes; Islamic madrasas formed much later under different political and educational conditions.

The Arabian connection must remain deliberately modest. Evidence that Jews were established in Yathrib in the Prophet's lifetime is not evidence identifying the specific route by which each family reached it. Jewish communities established roots in Arabia over different periods, but surviving sources do not establish one ancestry or migration date for all of them. The stronger line of explanation is how religious observance and distinctive communal law could exist under governments not controlled by those communities.

The Qurʾān recounts the missions of Mūsā, Dāwūd and Sulaymān (2:47–61; 21:78–82; 34:10–14); Kings, Chronicles and Ezra–Nehemiah preserve related but distinct histories, while Near Eastern evidence helps date the conquests of 722, 597 and 587/586 BCE. The Qurʾān and the biblical books recount some shared prophetic figures but differ in emphases, narrative structure and theological claims. Late-antique Jewish communities could be connected by learning and ritual despite local variations in language, social hierarchy and political status. This will later matter in Madinah, where the Prophet did not encounter an abstract “Judaism” but households and groups with land, alliances and particular obligations. Before that, the Persian and Greek worlds changed the languages and intellectual settings in which such communities lived.

## 04. Religious disciplines and the changing world of the first millennium BCE

**Matn.** After the destruction of Jerusalem, Jewish communities had to sustain worship and teaching without the independence of their former kingdom. Meanwhile the Persian kings linked the Levant, Mesopotamia and Iran through imperial government, and Greek cities developed their own modes of philosophical inquiry. Farther east, teachers in northern India debated ritual and liberation, while Chinese thinkers disputed the moral obligations of rulers and families. These developments unfolded across different centuries; they were not the work of a single generation or one demonstrable worldwide upheaval. In the western Near East, however, the lines of contact can be followed. Persian administration brought diverse communities under common rule; Alexander's conquests replaced it with Greek-speaking dynasties; and Greek became a language through which Jews, Egyptians, Syrians and others could explain their traditions to wider audiences. Jewish scripture was translated and interpreted in Greek, and schools of argument developed in cities where different religious communities met. The Roman world would inherit much of this linguistic and urban setting. It was within Jewish life under Roman authority—not in an undefined 'age of religions'—that the movement associated with ʿĪsā began. Elsewhere in Eurasia, religious and philosophical communities developed distinctive teachings, but the evidence does not establish direct transmission between all these movements.

**Sharḥ.** Karl Jaspers later grouped several developments under the label “Axial Age”; their different dates and settings do not establish one common historical cause. The dates of the Buddha, Zoroaster, Laozi and individual early Greek teachers are disputed to varying degrees. Connections must therefore be shown individually: an imperial road or shared administrative language can support movement of people and texts, whereas similarity between two doctrines does not by itself prove borrowing. Persia's administrative reach and the Hellenistic spread of Greek give stronger grounds for certain western Eurasian contacts than for a single transformation spanning India, China and Greece.

Guénon's argument also requires a correction often missing in its popular retellings. He considered the sixth century a threshold of what he called ordinary historical accessibility, but surviving Mesopotamian, Egyptian and other sources allow detailed chronology much earlier. His historical premise cannot be accepted without qualification, and his judgment of Buddhism or philosophy reflects his own conception of orthodoxy and intellectual hierarchy. The intellectually fruitful question is narrower: did some traditions respond to changing social conditions by differentiating ritual, practical discipline and contemplative knowledge? That can be studied separately in each tradition. It matters for the later Islamic account because philosophy, law, revelation and spiritual realization also came to be distinguishable modes of teaching, though their boundaries were drawn differently.

Guénon's metaphysical interpretation of sacred cycles and the dated history of individual teachers address different kinds of claims. Achaemenid government, Hellenistic cities and Greek scriptural translations created identifiable political and linguistic links across the western Near East. For China and India, the comparisons concern developing traditions of moral and contemplative training; their presence in one paragraph is not an argument that they directly shaped first-century Christianity or the seventh-century Ḥijāz. Guénon's sixth-century claim is a metaphysical-periodizing judgment with its own disputed historical assumptions, not an empirical explanation of the co-occurrence of named founders.

René Guénon's *The Crisis of the Modern World*, chapter 1, gives a different reading: he associates transformations around the sixth century BCE with an increasingly differentiated and outward historical phase of traditional civilizations. His claim concerns spiritual cycles and transformations in the accessibility of principial knowledge, not simply a chronology of founders. Guénon interprets the changes through spiritual cycles; the available dates do not demonstrate that a single rupture occurred across Eurasia. The Prophet's contemporaries inherited a world of earlier scriptural communities, philosophical traditions and rival institutions; none of those inheritances alone explains the emergence of his message. The immediate lineage leading to the seventh-century Near East passes through Hellenistic and Roman Judaea, Christian churches and the imperial frontiers.

## 05. Jesus and the communities that carried his message

**Matn.** In first-century Judaea, Roman governors, local rulers, priestly families and teachers of the Law operated within a population whose hopes and disagreements were shaped by worship at Jerusalem and memories of earlier prophets. Yaḥyā summoned people to repentance, and ʿĪsā ibn Maryam called Israel to sincere worship, mercy and righteousness. His disciples gathered around his teaching; after his earthly mission, communities devoted to him arose in Judaea and spread into Greek-speaking cities. Their members had to decide how Jews and non-Jews could share worship and discipline, what authority the apostles' teaching carried, and how the life of Jesus should be told. Letters, narrative accounts, readings and liturgies travelled with these communities. Jerusalem's destruction in 70 CE transformed Jewish religious life; Christian assemblies, meanwhile, multiplied around the Mediterranean and eastward. When imperial patronage followed Constantine in the fourth century, debates already underway concerning Christ's status acquired new councils, offices and political consequences. Greek, Syriac, Coptic and other churches did not interpret those debates identically. Monasteries became places of prayer, learning and hospitality, and missionaries and traders carried Christian life beyond the imperial frontier. By Muḥammad's lifetime the Christian people encountered by Arabs were neither a single doctrinal party nor a distant abstraction: they were neighbours, traders, rulers, monks and communities bound to different churches.

**Sharḥ.** The Islamic account of ʿĪsā and Maryam belongs to revealed history (notably Qurʾān 3:33–55; 5:110–120; 19:16–36). The earliest recoverable Christian histories rest on a different kind of evidence: first-century letters, Gospel narratives, later patristic writings and documents of councils. Christian creeds developed through councils and theological disputes; their later formulations cannot automatically be attributed to the earliest disciples. The controversy surrounding Nicaea (325), Ephesus (431) and Chalcedon (451) needs to be understood as sustained argument among Christian communities, not as a single moment when Christianity became homogeneous.

Christianity also changed the geography of learning. In Greek-speaking eastern cities, philosophical and theological argument shared a vocabulary capable of crossing political boundaries. Syriac translators and teachers moved between theological, medical and philosophical work, sometimes under rulers of different religions. This made them potential mediators when Muslim patrons later sought Greek learning in Arabic. The intellectual bridge is therefore not a vague statement that Islam was 'influenced by Christianity'; it is the documented existence of bilingual scholars, texts and schools available within the regions that Muslim armies and merchants later encountered. In the Sīrah itself the immediate issues were worship, prophecy and protection, not the ninth-century translation of Aristotle.

A concrete mechanism in Christian expansion is linguistic and urban. Letters to congregations could circulate along roads and shipping routes connecting Greek-speaking cities; local assemblies could read them, dispute their meaning and transmit instruction even without a common local bishop. Later councils and imperial patronage made certain disputes politically consequential. At the Arabian frontier, however, Greek, Syriac, Coptic and Geʿez Christian lives did not form one uniform audience for Islam. Each source needs to identify the community it actually describes.

The causal thread is institutional as well as doctrinal. Greek and Syriac writing made teachings travel; churches offered regular worship and communal leadership; monasticism provided spaces for disciplined life, copying and teaching. Imperial patronage offered resources and created new occasions for controversy, since bishops and emperors could have different interests. Aksum's Christianity and Syriac-speaking communities in Persian territory meant that the Prophet's contemporaries encountered Christian practices beyond Constantinople's control. Muslims encountered Christian neighbours, envoys, protectors, clergy and monks as well as arguments about the person of Christ. Their routes of movement traversed empires and Arabian borderlands where religion, commerce and military protection intersected.

## 06. The sixth-century crossroads of Rome, Persia, Ethiopia and Arabia

**Matn.** The world bordering sixth-century Arabia was divided between powerful courts that also depended upon local allies. From Constantinople the Romans administered Syria and Egypt; Sasanian kings governed Iran and Iraq; Arab federates guarded approaches, mediated trade and sometimes fought one another along the imperial frontier. To the south, conflict between Ḥimyar, Aksum and Persian interests drew Yemen into Red Sea diplomacy. The persecution of Najrān's Christians in the 520s and Aksumite intervention showed how Arabian religious disputes could involve neighbouring kings. Yet the Ḥijāz lay outside direct Roman or Sasanian administration. Its towns depended more immediately on local sanctuaries, families, oases and alliances, while travellers carried news from the wider world. Jews and Christians lived in different Arabian regions; local shrines and tribal devotions continued alongside them. In 602 the Romans and Sasanians entered a devastating war. Syria and Egypt fell under Persian occupation before Roman recovery in the 620s. Around the beginning of that war Muḥammad was approaching maturity in Makkah; around 610 his public mission began to take form. Neither empire appointed the chiefs of Quraysh or the alliances of Yathrib, yet the conflict would soon alter the political setting into which the Muslim community expanded. The Prophet's earliest hearers lived among households governed by Quraysh's sanctuary customs and clan protections rather than direct imperial administration.

**Sharḥ.** An empire's presence on the frontier did not make every caravan route a branch of its administration. The Ghassanid and Lakhmid networks involved military patronage, kinship and competition, while the Red Sea introduced Ethiopian, South Arabian and Persian interests with different reach. Literary accounts of Najrān and the Aksumite invasion need comparison with epigraphic and archaeological evidence; Valentina Grasso's analysis of sixth-century South Arabia cautions against reducing every political intervention to a single religious motive. Frontier rulers mobilized military allies, collected revenues and offered varying degrees of protection to religious communities.

The battle between imperial powers also reshaped what an Arabian leader might encounter as expansion proceeded. Muslim forces moving into Syria entered territory with military roads, bishoprics and taxation already organized; movements into Iraq encountered Sasanian estates and administrative practices. The conquest did not invent those systems and could not rule without engaging them. Yet the Prophet's first listeners were not Roman subjects receiving a centrally enforced imperial creed. Meccan believers depended on personal protection and voluntary allegiance; after conquest, Muslim rulers administered the taxes and offices of former imperial provinces.

The Roman–Sasanian war weakened both empires before the Muslim conquests, but the surviving evidence does not establish that it caused the Prophetic proclamation. It did weaken both imperial systems before major Muslim conquests. Makkah's particular situation instead depended on sanctuary customs, Quraysh's clan arrangements and regional trade. Early Muslim sources place the Year of the Elephant near the Prophet's birth, but their accounts of the expedition and its dating differ. Abū Ṭālib protected Muḥammad through kinship obligations even though he did not adopt the new proclamation.

Najrān records Christian persecution and Aksumite intervention, but it supplies no direct causal evidence for Muḥammad's proclamation in Makkah. Najrān documents the presence of Christianity, regional persecution and cross-Red-Sea intervention; it does not prove that the Meccan message arose from imperial conflict. The Roman–Persian war matters differently: it transformed the military position of the two states into whose provinces Muslim forces would later move. Thus the local origin of the mission and the geopolitical environment of its later expansion have distinct histories.

# Book II — The formation of believers in Makkah

## 07. The sanctuary, the clans and the obligations of protection

**Matn.** Makkah was organized around a sanctuary that drew people whose clans might otherwise have been rivals. Quraysh families maintained the Kaʿbah and its services, arranged trade, and relied upon agreements that made travel and exchange possible across tribal boundaries. A person's safety ordinarily depended upon kinship or a protector willing to defend him; those with few allies or enslaved status had much less room to refuse a powerful household's demands. The sanctuary could restrain violence at particular places and times without abolishing wider inequalities or feuds. Among Quraysh, Banū Hāshim held an established place, and Abū Ṭālib would protect his nephew Muḥammad even when he did not join his religious summons. Beyond Makkah, Yathrib was a settlement of orchards and fortified neighbourhoods where the Aws, Khazraj and several Jewish groups were linked by cultivation, alliances and longstanding disputes. These two towns offered different possibilities. In Makkah the first call would be heard under the protection and pressures of established clans; in Yathrib rival groups would eventually seek a leader capable of mediating and joining them. The Hijrah thus became possible through relationships developing long before the emigrants departed.

**Sharḥ.** Kinship and patronage determined who could challenge prevailing religious customs without immediately losing protection. A free man with an influential guardian, an enslaved person and a visitor without an alliance did not face the same risks when accepting a new religious allegiance. This social difference helps explain why early conversions followed ties of trust and household contact, and why some believers could resist pressure while others suffered violence. It would be anachronistic to describe Quraysh's sanctuary as a modern government. Tribal obligation, ritual privilege, negotiated protection and exchange could overlap without creating one centralized state. The Sīrah tradition's accounts of the Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl and the Prophet's protection by Abū Ṭālib are valuable evidence for how contemporaries remembered justice and patronage; the wording and sequence of those accounts need comparison across transmitters.

The protection system helps explain both the survival of the Prophet's mission and the injustice suffered by vulnerable converts. Abū Ṭālib's defence of his nephew was a recognizable kin obligation, even while religious disagreement persisted. A client or enslaved convert could lack a similarly effective protector. Accepting the new call was therefore not one uniform act of social defiance. The religion's claim of common accountability sat within highly unequal capacities to resist. Later migration did not remove the importance of kinship immediately; it placed inherited loyalties into new relations with people whose commitment rested upon worship rather than birth.

Mecca's exact commercial scale is disputed. Accounts of Quraysh's seasonal journeys record remembered patterns of commerce, whereas claims that particular Meccan families dominated long-distance luxury trade remain disputed. The protection system is more immediately relevant to the first believers: clan standing could shield a dissenting member while enslaved or client households faced coercion. That difference explains the uneven distribution of suffering without implying that the first converts belonged to one social class.

Quraysh organized commercial journeys, but historians disagree about the volume and geographical reach of Meccan trade; evidence does not establish that its clans controlled a vast trading empire. Qurʾān 106 emphasizes journeys, security and provision, while the institutional details come through later Sīrah and genealogy. A proclamation that challenged sacred objects and inherited status also threatened the loyalties through which clans maintained protection and authority. In Yathrib, meanwhile, the aftermath of conflicts such as Buʿāth provided a different political setting. The earliest instruction would begin in Muḥammad's household, where personal confidence and protection mattered before public authority existed.

## 08. Ḥirāʾ, Khadījah and the beginnings of a teaching community

**Matn.** Muḥammad grew up in the clan of Banū Hāshim, without his father, and after his mother's death under the care of his grandfather and then his uncle Abū Ṭālib. He married Khadījah, whose household joined affection, economic security and social standing. In middle life he withdrew at intervals to Ḥirāʾ, away from the crowded obligations of Makkah. There the first revelation confronted him with a command to recite and the knowledge of a Creator who teaches. He returned shaken to Khadījah, who listened, comforted him and sought counsel from Waraqah ibn Nawfal. Khadījah’s assurance drew on her long knowledge of his conduct and established the first intimate setting in which the experience was received and discussed. Revelation continued, and the new summons took shape through shared prayer, recitation, learning and the practical support of a small circle. ʿAlī, Zayd, Abū Bakr and others entered that circle through differing relationships to the household. In those gatherings believers learned to pray, give to others and govern their conduct in expectation of judgment. When the message moved into public hearing, the circle would be exposed to the protections and pressures of Quraysh's clans. Its beginnings in a household helped it survive those pressures.

**Sharḥ.** ʿĀʾishah's report in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (no. 3) recounts the first revelation, Khadījah's reassurance and Waraqah's recognition of the prophetic encounter. Khadījah's response is more than a gesture of reassurance: she names traits of conduct that can be tested in ordinary social relationships—kinship, hospitality, truthfulness and aid to the burdened. The report connects an extraordinary encounter to recognizable moral character without making a private experience into a publicly verifiable event of the same kind as a dated treaty. The initial call in Qurʾān 96:1–5 brings recitation, creation and knowledge together; passages such as 74:1–7 connect warning with purification and patience. Different reports arrange the earliest revelations and conversions differently. The surviving reports do not establish one unambiguous chronological order among the first converts.

There is an intellectual distinction between the event of revelation and the social process by which people learned to trust its messenger. The former, within Muslim belief, is God's address to the Prophet; the latter involves repeated teaching, memory, recognition of moral conduct and decisions taken by particular persons. Khadījah's role links those orders without merging them. Her reassurance addresses the Prophet personally, but the qualities she cites later become intelligible to others as grounds for trust. Similarly, an early follower's prayer is an act of worship and at the same time a repeated practice through which a shared form of life becomes recognizable.

Khadījah appealed to Muḥammad's established conduct, while Waraqah connected the encounter with earlier prophecy. In Bukhārī's opening revelation report, Khadījah's reassurance appeals to observable qualities of Muḥammad's conduct, while Waraqah places the encounter within remembered prophecy. That is not the same as independent archival corroboration of the private experience at Ḥirāʾ. In the transmitted account, Khadījah first reassured Muḥammad through her knowledge of his character, and Waraqah subsequently related his encounter to earlier prophecy. Khadījah, members of the household and other early believers heard the recitations, learned practices of worship and formed the first circle of instruction.

The transition from a revealed word to a growing community required human processes: someone had to hear it, learn its recitation, recognize the teacher's trustworthiness, practise prayer and make a judgment about allegiance. Khadījah's home, Abū Bakr's friendships and other networks of instruction each helped that process. Yet the new allegiance would eventually alter the meaning of those older ties. A believer might remain a son, client or merchant while refusing a form of worship expected by his household. That friction grew as teaching moved beyond familiar households into a public proclamation demanding changes in conduct.

## 09. The Meccan proclamation: worship, reckoning and new loyalties

**Matn.** As recitation reached people beyond the first household, it summoned them to worship one God and to answer for their lives after death. Wealth and ancestry could not secure a person's standing before God; powerful households now faced obligations toward slaves, orphans, debtors and hungry neighbours. Such teaching challenged ordinary habits of speech, possession, giving and judgment as well as established sanctuary rites. Quraysh's response was uneven. Some men and women of leading families accepted the call; others feared dishonouring ancestors, losing authority or unsettling inherited relationships. Those without a protective clan suffered most directly: Bilāl, the family of Yāsir and other vulnerable believers became known through reports of coercion. The new community learned endurance through circumstances that did not equally threaten all its members. Prayer, recitation, gifts and emancipation offered ways to practise new loyalties before the believers possessed collective political power. They could help and teach one another, but could not yet guarantee the security of everyone who joined them. When persecution made such vulnerability intolerable, some sought protection across the Red Sea. The movement's first institutional problem was therefore not how to govern an empire but how a community of conscience could preserve worship and people when its opponents controlled local protection.

**Sharḥ.** Meccan revelation joins resurrection and judgment to demands for generosity, truthfulness and care for the vulnerable. Passages such as Qurʾān 81:1–14; 90:11–18; 92:5–21 and 107:1–7 join eschatological accountability to care for the vulnerable and critique ostentatious worship. The Meccan proclamation did not prohibit wealth or commerce as such; it demanded truthfulness, generosity and accountability before God in their pursuit and use. Recurring oaths, warnings, questions and scenes of the Last Day gave believers memorable language for examining daily conduct.

Giving, emancipation, prayer and restraint affected both relations among households and the intentions and habits of the believers who practised them. A person asked to free a slave must relinquish property; a person asked to give food must resist retaining it; a person commanded to endure mockery must discipline anger. The practices do not prove that every participant attained the virtue intended, but they identify occasions in which character was tested and cultivated. When later authors discuss *malakah*, intention or purification, they supply analytical language for such processes. Later writers developed theories of habit and virtue around practices that early believers already undertook: giving, prayer, restraint and endurance.

When the proclamation made generosity and accountability obligatory, its claim reached the actual power relationships of a city. The master who owned a slave, the guardian handling an orphan's property and the rich merchant dispensing or withholding support faced newly articulated duties. The proclamation challenged exploitation, but Meccan masters and patrons continued to wield coercive power. The historical effect in Makkah was first the formation of a community that judged such conduct by different standards, often without the power to enforce them.

Later ethical writers such as al-Ghazālī describe the formation of stable dispositions through repeated action, examination of intention and correction of appetite. This offers a useful account of how practices may form character, but it is a later analytical reconstruction. Al-Ghazālī developed his technical account of moral psychology centuries after the earliest converts practised prayer, giving and patient endurance. Historically, the relevant evidence for persecution comes largely from transmitted reports about Bilāl, the family of Yāsir and other early believers; severity, chronology and the details of speeches need individual examination. Their different social protections made a common religious commitment costly in unequal ways. As persecution intensified and clan protection failed some believers, migration across the Red Sea offered an alternative source of safety.

## 10. Aksum: faith before political safety

**Matn.** Opposition in Makkah could not be met by one response, because the believers did not possess equal means of protection. Some were shielded by respected relatives; others were subjected to pressure, confiscation or violence. Groups therefore crossed the Red Sea to Aksum, where a Christian ruler was remembered as giving them refuge despite Meccan efforts to secure their return. The migrants had left their familiar homes but had not yet established a new Muslim polity. They lived under the justice and authority of another ruler, preserving worship and fellowship while explaining their beliefs to people of a different religious tradition. In Makkah the Prophet continued to teach despite increasing pressure on Banū Hāshim and his supporters. Separated from the believers in Makkah, the emigrants maintained allegiance to the Prophet through trust and reports carried across the Red Sea. Aksum demonstrated that believers could survive beyond the reach of Meccan clan sanctions, but it did not solve the problem of how the Prophet's mission might become public and secure within Arabia. That problem would return with force when the Prophet lost two of his closest protectors.

**Sharḥ.** The Aksum episode is usually narrated through Ibn Isḥāq as preserved by Ibn Hishām and other Sīrah collections. The dialogue reported between Jaʿfar ibn Abī Ṭālib and the Negus illuminates how later transmitters understood the Muslim–Christian encounter, especially the claims made about ʿĪsā and Maryam. Later Sīrah recensions preserve the speech in varying forms, without establishing a contemporary verbatim transcript. The religious setting is nonetheless plausible and important: Aksum was a Christian kingdom with Red Sea connections, and Arabia's western shore participated in a network wider than Quraysh.

Aksum also clarifies a difference between spiritual steadfastness and political capability. Perseverance may enable an individual to withstand loss, but it does not create a lawful jurisdiction under which his children can live. The migrants needed a ruler willing to refuse extradition and a place where worship could continue; those conditions were supplied by a polity not organized around their own community. The emigrants remained loyal to the Prophet while living under the Negus's protection across the Red Sea. Yathrib later supplied a different combination: local believers not only offered protection but undertook responsibilities of a common public order. Confusing these two forms of security obscures the development of the Hijrah.

The emigrants in Aksum could worship under the protection of a Christian sovereign without possessing jurisdiction over their hosts. That institutional difference matters more than anachronistic claims about modern citizenship or interfaith policy. The transmitted exchange before the Negus places Jesus and Mary at the centre of the emigrants' explanation of their faith; later Sīrah accounts preserve differing details of the speeches. Yathrib's pledges would introduce a new kind of protection—shared responsibility for defence and settlement within Arabia.

The institution of asylum mattered because it shifted the conditions of survival. Makkah's protection was commonly personal and tribal; in Aksum, the migrants depended upon a foreign sovereign who was not part of their kinship system. They did not have to become citizens of a modern pluralist state for the episode to show a real form of interreligious coexistence. Reports differ over the number of Aksum migrations, their dates and the identity of individual participants. The spiritual meaning of hijrah as leaving what distances one from God was developed in devotional interpretation, but the historical movement also involved parents, spouses, property and the uncertainty of return. Their experience prepares the later Hijrah without making the two migrations institutionally identical.

The long report from ʿĀʾishah through ʿUrwah and al-Zuhrī in al-Bukhārī 3905 also preserves a narrower Meccan episode: Abū Bakr received the protection of Ibn al-Daghinah, was initially permitted to worship privately, and then drew public attention by reciting in his courtyard. The attempted restriction on public recitation explains how an existing institution of *jiwār* could protect a person's body yet fail to protect the circulation of a new message. Women and children are described as listening, but no conversion figures follow from the report. The account records continuing allegiance and religious perseverance under a Christian ruler's protection. Dossier 1 examines the transmission and its limits. [Al-Bukhārī 3905](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3905).

## 11. From loss of protection to an alliance at al-ʿAqabah

**Matn.** Khadījah's death deprived the Prophet of the companion whose home had sustained the first circle; Abū Ṭālib's death removed an influential protector within Quraysh. Without that protection, he sought a hearing beyond Makkah, but his visit to Ṭāʾif ended in rejection. During pilgrimage seasons he continued speaking to visitors who came to the sanctuary. Among them were people from Yathrib, where rival groups had suffered years of conflict and where some were prepared to recognize a mediator and teacher from outside their local divisions. Several accepted his message and returned home; Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr then taught recitation and prayer among them. The first pledge at al-ʿAqabah concerned allegiance in worship and conduct; the second bound a larger group to protect the Prophet if he came to Yathrib. The difference was decisive. Believers had already found refuge in Aksum, but now a group within Arabia offered protection tied to a growing local community. Emigrants began departing in stages, leaving property and familiar alliances. The Prophet and Abū Bakr followed, travelling under concealment until they reached Yathrib. A religious movement formed under Meccan pressure was entering a town where it would have to organize public life.

**Sharḥ.** Sīrah and maghāzī reports recount the rejection at Ṭāʾif and the subsequent pledges at al-ʿAqabah, although they vary in reported speeches and participants. The first pledge concerned belief and moral conduct; the second also committed Yathrib's supporters to protect the Prophet against hostile forces. A promise to avoid theft, unlawful sexual conduct or infanticide concerned communal moral commitment; a promise to defend the Prophet in Yathrib implicated the signatories' households, allies and military obligations. The instruction by Muṣʿab was crucial between the two because the new allegiance required neighbours able to pray and learn together before their prospective leader arrived.

The second pledge committed Yathrib's supporters to protect the Prophet, extending their obligations beyond personal acceptance of the message. An instructed circle in Yathrib could receive the Prophet as a teacher, but a binding commitment to defend him entailed risks for its people and their allies. The supporters of Yathrib accepted the risk of armed conflict with Quraysh when they promised to protect the Prophet. Muṣʿab's period of instruction connects private adherence to public capability: people who had prayed together and learned the recitation could recognize a common cause when asked to make a demanding pledge. The Hijrah then gave that promise a real object, placing vulnerable newcomers and the Prophet himself under the hosts' protection.

Yathrib did not invite an unknown stranger into an empty political space. The Aws, Khazraj and Jewish groups had their own property, alliances and memories of violence. The Prophet's standing as a religious teacher and an outside arbiter could appeal to people seeking a settlement, but the motives of each participant cannot be reduced to a single desire for peace. The Hijrah also required courage, secrecy and trust under risk, themes central to later spiritual reading. The immediate historical consequence was more demanding: once the newcomers arrived, housing, food, disputes and protection could no longer be managed as private matters among a few Meccan believers. Those problems became urgent as soon as the emigrants arrived.

The stages of al-ʿAqabah matter because instruction preceded armed protection. Muṣʿab's work in Yathrib gave new believers common words and practices; the subsequent pledge imposed risks on households and allies whose conflicts long predated Islam. The accounts distinguish commitments to worship and moral conduct from the later undertaking to defend the Prophet against hostile forces.

# Book III — Madinah: how a religious fellowship became a public community

## 12. The Hijrah: neighbours, newcomers and a shared order

**Matn.** The migrants reached Yathrib with their religious fellowship intact but without much of the property, kin protection or livelihood that had supported them in Makkah. Their hosts cultivated dates, lived among fortified neighbourhoods and remained bound by obligations to their own families and allies. The Aws and Khazraj had a history of fighting, while Jewish groups possessed land and separate alliances. The Prophet founded a mosque for prayer, instruction and public consultation, and bonds of assistance connected emigrants with local believers. Sharing food and resources eased immediate hardship, but a town could not be governed by occasional generosity alone. Agreements made with participating groups addressed compensation for deaths, ransom, expenditure in defence and the security of allies. Existing kin groups continued to bear recognizable duties; these were now coordinated under wider commitments so that a claim or attack need not restart a private feud. Groups named as Jews retained their religious difference within the obligations actually specified in the surviving agreements. Madinah thus provided the migrants something Aksum had not: they participated in a community able to make common decisions, adjudicate disputes and defend its arrangements. As prayer and recitation became ordinary features of town life, marriage, debt, inheritance and trade presented further occasions for guidance.

**Sharḥ.** The key phrase in the Medinan document is the designation of a single community, *umma wāḥida*, alongside clauses retaining the emigrants and several groups of Yathrib within their existing arrangements of blood compensation and captive ransom. Ibn Hishām and Abū ʿUbayd preserve versions of the Medinan agreements; Michael Lecker compares their clauses, parties and transmission in *The Constitution of Medina* (2004, with later revisions). Saïd Amir Arjomand and other scholars argue over its composition and the distribution of its parts. The surviving recensions vary in clause sequence and participants, leaving the dates of individual provisions uncertain. The term “constitution” is convenient but potentially misleading: the transmitted text is a set of concrete obligations, not a modern theory of equal citizenship or centralized sovereignty. Participating groups assumed specific responsibilities for blood compensation, ransom of captives and contributions to common defence.

These provisions turned commitments of mutual aid into specified duties between groups that already possessed their own obligations. A believer can be generous toward a newcomer today but not tomorrow; an agreed rule about compensation and mutual aid makes some duties predictable and adjudicable. Neither law nor generosity replaces the other. Where kin groups retained responsibility for ransom and blood money, the agreement did not eliminate their internal authority; it directed their activity into a wider protected association. The Prophet was teaching worship while resolving issues that required enforceable cooperation. The Medinan community linked shared worship to concrete public duties of compensation, ransom, defence and adjudication.

Lecker's 2004 study and substantially updated 2024 edition warn against treating the transmitted *kitāb* as a modern constitution or assuming that every Jewish group in Madinah signed it. In the published text the emigrants and several Yathrib groups continue their customary blood-money and ransom responsibilities even as a wider *umma* is asserted. This combines continuity of local kin obligations with wider public coordination. The agreements retained named clan duties while placing them within wider obligations of mutual protection.

The social problem was acute. An emigrant had surrendered the protection and resources of his old household; an Anṣārī who helped him still had children, farms and obligations to relatives. Material generosity was therefore a test of allegiance, while the mosque made collective worship and teaching a regular practice. Qurʾān 59:8–9 describes the deprived emigrants and the hosts who preferred them despite their own need. The Qurʾān praises mutual assistance, while the Medinan agreements assign particular responsibilities to participating clans and allied groups. The Prophet's leadership united teaching with adjudication, but its historical forms remained responsive to local conditions. Public judgment increasingly concerned family obligations, property and markets, matters no less important to the community's development than warfare. The inner and outer are linked here in a demonstrable way: a disposition toward generosity mattered because real people needed food and shelter, while public obligations made mutual assistance more reliable than generosity alone.

The document's wording imposes a useful restraint on our terminology. A phrase designating a single community stands next to recurrent clauses retaining named clans' earlier compensation obligations. The legal relationship is one of enlarged coordination, not a documented abolition of kin groups. Equally, the Jewish clauses cannot be read as proof that every Jewish group of the oasis had accepted precisely the same terms. Lecker's study argues for narrower participation and separately negotiated arrangements; the recensions require collation before any one clause can be dated with certainty. Existing clans retained their identities but now shared responsibilities for defence, compensation and judgments that crossed earlier boundaries. [Ibn Hishām's text](https://islamweb.net/ar/library/content/58/584/); [Lecker's study](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-constitution-of-medina-muhammads-first-legal-document-2/).

## 13. Worship takes shape in the routines of a town

**Matn.** In Madinah religious allegiance entered the rhythm of domestic and public life. Congregational prayer gave the day repeated points of assembly; fasting marked a common season of restraint and provision; almsgiving directed wealth toward those in need; the orientation of prayer toward the Kaʿbah joined the new community to the sanctuary from which many had been expelled. Families asked about marriage, divorce, inheritance, household conflict and care for dependants. Traders needed rules for exchange, borrowing, debt and truthful dealing. A judgment in one household might affect a wider kin group, while relief for a poor newcomer could involve resources held by a prosperous neighbour. The Prophet's instruction therefore appeared in recitation, example, consultation and decisions arising from particular situations. People did not receive a completed legal encyclopedia at the Hijrah. Obligations developed as the community encountered circumstances that called for guidance. A person who fasted and prayed also bought and sold, inherited property, resolved anger and supported relatives. These were not unrelated occupations: the same allegiance was to govern the believer's conduct when worship ended and when private interests clashed.

**Sharḥ.** Medinan rulings developed at different times; tafsīr, ḥadīth and Sīrah preserve evidence for their sequence, though not every date is secure. Qurʾān 2:142–150 concerns the direction of prayer; 2:183–187 fasting; 4:7–12 inheritance; 2:282 debts; 4:35 familial arbitration. These passages have different occasions and literary forms. Not every later fiqh rule can be assigned directly to an identifiable day in Madinah. What can be shown is how the community's economic and domestic life continually generated questions for religious judgment. Daily prayer and fasting structured believers' time and repeatedly demanded attention, restraint and remembrance, although individual conduct still varied.

A marriage rule, for example, joins several orders: the parties' intentions, their consent and responsibilities, the property consequences of a union, the obligations of kin and the community's means of resolving disputes. Likewise, a rule about debts depends on trust, recording, witnesses and a procedure if someone defaults. Worship and commerce intersect because the same person must judge how to act in both, not because fasting automatically settles an unpaid loan. This helps later usūl al-fiqh make sense. The jurist's task is to draw from transmitted guidance the rules applicable to cases that recur in changing social forms, while the spiritual teacher asks what dispositions enable those rules to be lived rightly.

A marriage dispute or unpaid debt could bring households with competing loyalties before a shared authority whose judgment affected both parties. Qurʾān 2:282 articulates recording and witnessing for deferred debt, while 4:35 addresses arbitration in marital discord. Those passages do not establish that every Medinan transaction followed a uniform later fiqh textbook. They reveal why new public obligations required habitual instruction, practical judgment and means of recording claims.

A later example helps explain the system without retrojecting its terminology. Al-Ghazālī organized the *Iḥyāʾ* into worship, customary dealings, destructive traits and saving virtues. He could do so because ritual acts, everyday conduct and the condition of the heart had long been treated as related objects of religious instruction, even though his precise psychological scheme was eleventh-century. The Prophet's household reports and practical judgments are thus important historical evidence in addition to public campaigns. Conflict with Quraysh soon made the same problems of property, trust, discipline and judgment matters of military survival.

## 14. Badr: conflict tests allegiance and public judgment

**Matn.** Leaving Makkah had not ended the confrontation with Quraysh. The emigrants had lost property and protection, Meccan trading parties still passed through routes near Madinah, and both sides saw the other's growing alliances as a threat. In the second year after the Hijrah, a confrontation connected with a Quraysh caravan led to battle near Badr. A smaller Muslim force defeated an army sent from Makkah, killing important Quraysh leaders and taking captives. Victory gave the new community standing it had not possessed before, but also brought decisions that a circle of persecuted believers had scarcely faced: how to deal with prisoners, what to do with goods taken in war, and how to distribute the benefits and burdens of fighting. The victory strengthened Madinah's position while intensifying Quraysh's resolve to retaliate. Participation in common defence required believers to accept command and share risks with people outside their immediate kin. Those demands could not be settled by enthusiasm alone; they called for rules, judgment and forms of trust strong enough to survive the reversals that followed.

**Sharḥ.** The Qurʾān discusses Badr in passages including 3:123–127 and Sūrat al-Anfāl, which addresses fear, victory, captives, spoils and cohesion. Detailed troop movements, numbers and dialogues belong mainly to the later maghāzī tradition; they require comparison of versions, especially Ibn Isḥāq's material and relevant hadith. A battle's broad occurrence does not license treating each remembered speech as independently verified. Badr's importance as an institution-forming event is clearer when we ask who now bore responsibilities that had previously been limited to kin groups: commanders coordinated armed men from several backgrounds; the community faced captives and property disputes; alliances elsewhere in Arabia were affected by the perceived result.

Captivity offers a sharp case of institutional change. Before a public community could hold prisoners, conflicts of capture or ransom might be managed primarily through family or tribal retaliation. After Badr, the Prophet and the believers confronted prisoners from powerful Meccan households, whose treatment affected diplomacy and future exchange. Spoils raised related disputes among fighters who had taken different risks. These were not abstract problems added by later jurists: the community was learning to assign claims when common military action produced consequences beyond any one family's control. Later law systematized matters already encountered here, while the Qurʾānic address contested pride and entitlement arising from victory.

In Qurʾān 8 the aftermath of Badr is not celebrated merely as successful fighting. Disputes over spoils and the treatment of captives bring human desires into the immediate interpretation of events. That permits one to connect personal formation to public judgment without claiming that believers were uniformly free of rivalry. The Qurʾānic passages address fear, victory, captives and spoils; later maghāzī reports supply fuller speeches and troop counts whose transmission differs.

Victory confronted the fighters with decisions about spoils and captives, testing their restraint, loyalties and judgment. The participants faced fear, conflicting expectations and the temptation to read success as personal entitlement. Qurʾān 8 addresses disputes over spoils and requires obedience and collective responsibility after victory. Ibn Khaldūn's later analysis of solidarity offers one angle on how a threatened group could act collectively; revelation, shared worship and the Prophet's authority provide the actors' own grounds of commitment. Social solidarity can explain mobilization without exhausting religious conviction. Uḥud will demonstrate that collective confidence does not remove the need for disciplined judgment.

## 15. Uḥud and the Trench: discipline, siege and divided loyalties

**Matn.** Quraysh returned the next year with an army seeking revenge. Near Uḥud the Muslims initially held their ground, but the departure of some archers from an assigned position exposed them to attack; the Prophet was wounded and Ḥamzah was killed. The loss forced the community to confront how desire for gain and uncertainty about command could undo its common effort. A larger coalition later threatened Madinah. Defenders excavated a trench to deny the attackers an easy approach and endured a siege in which supplies, labour and the reliability of allies mattered as much as courage. The war also sharpened tensions among groups bound by different agreements in and around the town. Banū Qaynuqāʿ and Banū al-Naḍīr had been expelled in earlier conflicts. After the Trench, Saʿd ibn Muʿādh pronounced judgment concerning Banū Qurayẓah, with executions and captivity described in the transmitted accounts. By the end of these campaigns, Madinah had learned to defend itself through planning, common labour and coordinated action. It had also become clear that agreements, religious allegiance and ordinary kinship could pull individuals and groups in different directions under danger.

**Sharḥ.** Qurʾān 3:121–175 engages the events around Uḥud, and 33:9–27 addresses the Trench and its aftermath. These texts do not supply every detail or number in later historical accounts. Ibn Isḥāq, al-Wāqidī and later writers preserve differing materials whose relation needs criticism. The transmitted judgment of Banū Qurayẓah appears in Sunni historical and legal accounts; reports differ over particular speeches and numbers. It is unnecessary to transform this book into a monograph on the controversy, but equally unsound to present every later number as established observation.

At Uḥud some archers abandoned their positions; at the Trench the defenders maintained collective labour and vigilance during a prolonged siege. Uḥud tested whether fighters under immediate pressure would remain at assigned positions; the Trench demanded the patience of households over a sustained threat, with tasks that brought no instant victory or visible booty. Failure could arise through individual departure, exhausted supplies or divided allies. The same outward obedience therefore took different forms in battle and siege. The siege demanded vigilance, patience and restraint from its defenders; later spiritual authors examined those qualities in settings of moral discipline.

At Uḥud some archers abandoned their tactical assignment after an apparent advantage; at the Trench the defenders sustained collective labour and defence under scarcity and fear. The Banū Qurayẓah reports raise a third, distinct problem of alleged wartime breach and post-siege judgment. The later narratives of Banū Qurayẓah differ in reported numbers and speeches; those details have different levels of evidentiary support.

The two campaigns illustrate distinct social pressures. Uḥud exposes limits of discipline inside the fighting force; the Trench demanded coordination among households over time, with food, digging, sentries and readiness depending upon more than battlefield bravery. Military loyalty required repeated obedience to assigned positions, cooperation over supplies and dependable performance under danger. Later spiritual authors examined vigilance and intention as moral disciplines; their arguments do not establish why particular archers abandoned their positions at Uḥud. The changing regional balance after the siege created conditions in which negotiation became possible. The next decisive encounter with Quraysh ended in negotiation and a treaty at al-Ḥudaybiyyah.

## 16. Al-Ḥudaybiyyah: an agreement that changed the terms of conflict

**Matn.** In the sixth year after the Hijrah the Prophet led companions toward Makkah intending pilgrimage, but Quraysh blocked their entry. The two sides negotiated while the travellers remained outside the sanctuary. The agreement postponed the pilgrimage until the following year, established a truce and allowed tribes to enter alliances with either party. One clause required the return of certain fugitives from Makkah, a condition that distressed Muslims who had themselves suffered persecution there. As the terms were being settled, Abū Jandal arrived from Makkah in chains and was not released into the Muslim party's custody. The companions struggled to accept a restriction that seemed to surrender a persecuted believer. When the pilgrimage party hesitated to end its consecration, the Prophet followed Umm Salamah’s counsel to perform the sacrifice and shaving himself; the others then followed his example. Quraysh had nevertheless dealt with the Prophet as the leader of a community able to make binding commitments. Fighting between the two centres ceased for a time, movement and contact became less dangerous, and neighbouring tribes reassessed their alliances. The treaty did not remove every grievance. It changed the field in which grievances and loyalties would be negotiated, and its eventual breach would provide the immediate occasion for the return to Makkah.

**Sharḥ.** The fullest useful starting point is the extended account of al-Miswar ibn Makhramah and Marwān in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Book of Conditions, nos. 2731–2732, compared with the related concise report, nos. 2711–2712, and Ibn Hishām's Sīrah. Suhayl ibn ʿAmr contested the treaty wording, Abū Jandal's arrival tested its return clause, ʿUmar objected to the terms, and Umm Salamah counselled the Prophet to begin the concluding rites. The treaty versions differ in wording, particularly in the scope of the clause concerning the return of fugitives. Qurʾān 48:1–29, especially its use of *fatḥ*, requires tafsīr: victory is not identified simply with immediate military conquest. The traditional reports also associate the pledge beneath the tree with the negotiations (48:18).

The return clause poses a useful limit on easy claims that religious commitment automatically yielded political harmony. The companions' reported distress was intelligible: a person who had escaped persecution might be handed back to his former protectors. The agreement therefore bound the community to enforce a decision some members considered damaging. A treaty has political force precisely when it constrains conduct under adverse circumstances. Once concluded, it also created expectations of reciprocity, and the tribal alliance clauses meant that injuries to allies could carry wider consequences. The later breach is unintelligible unless these obligations are understood. The parties accepted a binding truce despite serious misgivings; honouring its terms required restraint and altered the political relations between Makkah and Madinah.

Bukhārī 2731–32 gives a connected sequence: blocked access to the sanctuary, negotiations, the return stipulation, Abū Jandal's appearance, ʿUmar's questions and Umm Salamah's counsel. The treaty demanded compliance with terms that many participants disliked, including the postponement of pilgrimage and the return clause. The wider causal mechanism is specific: a truce enabled contact and acknowledged alliances, while a later attack on an ally could become a treaty breach with broader consequences.

The mechanism of change was practical. A truce reduced the immediate cost of travel and contact, while recognized alliances expanded the obligations of both parties to third groups. Treaties create openings only when their rules affect people's choices: traders travel, emissaries speak, families visit, tribes negotiate. The unusual spiritual test lay in restraint in the face of humiliation, illustrated by Abū Jandal's situation and some companions' reaction. The accounts preserve objections to the treaty, showing that its terms did not appear advantageous to all participants. Later Muslim interpretation saw the outcome as a victory; the historical argument identifies the specific restraints and alliances that made new outcomes possible. Khaybar and the deferred pilgrimage would soon test the new freedom of action, since peace with Quraysh did not end all conflict in Arabia.

The transmitted episode links institutional restraint to actual human reactions. In al-Bukhārī 2731–2732, the arrival of Abū Jandal makes the return clause immediately painful; ʿUmar questions the decision; after negotiation the companions hesitate to sacrifice and shave. Umm Salamah's counsel changes how the order is carried out: the Prophet acts first, and the group follows. The movement from negotiated words to embodied compliance is part of the event's mechanism. The report records the participants' immediate difficulty in accepting the terms; it does not supply reliable figures for subsequent conversions. [Al-Bukhārī 2731–2732](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2731).

## 17. Khaybar, the deferred pilgrimage and changing relations beyond Madinah

**Matn.** The truce with Quraysh opened space for action elsewhere. A campaign against Khaybar ended with Muslim control over its fortified settlements. Its Jewish cultivators continued working the land under an arrangement giving them half the produce, enabling agricultural life to continue after the change of authority. The settlement required decisions about land, labour and income that were more complicated than distributing movable spoils after battle. The following year the Muslims performed the pilgrimage visit previously denied to them, entering Makkah for a limited period under the negotiated agreement. The journey reunited the emigrants with the sanctuary and demonstrated that the treaty's terms could be observed. Other envoys and delegations entered the Prophet's growing circle of relations, and people outside Madinah had to decide whether to form alliances, resist, or acknowledge his authority while maintaining aspects of local life. The community was no longer concerned only with sheltering its own emigrants. It increasingly needed to govern relationships with towns and agricultural communities whose wealth, commitments and religious identities differed from its own. Those arrangements would become more extensive once Quraysh's treaty network broke down.

**Sharḥ.** The Khaybar traditions are distributed across hadith, maghāzī and legal sources; Bukhārī's chapters on cultivation and the reports about the Prophet's dealings with Khaybar's inhabitants are important. Reports differ about the settlement's subsequent administration, and later jurists derived rulings on land and crop-sharing from the Khaybar precedent. Khaybar's agricultural settlement preserved cultivation while transferring control over land revenues. Agricultural land cannot be treated as a bag of captured goods: cultivation requires labour, access to water, seasonal effort and some expectation about shares. Continuing arrangements therefore put the new authorities in relation to people who did not all become part of the same religious household.

The agricultural dimension also gives the later Islamic law of landholding a historical problem to solve. Crop-sharing is a continuing relationship, not a one-time transaction: access to fields, division of produce and the labour of cultivators must be sustained season after season. New authority could enforce an arrangement while relying upon the knowledge of existing cultivators. The deferred pilgrimage instead tested whether Quraysh and the Muslims would honour the agreed truce and access arrangements. Placing the episodes together is useful only if their distinct effects are explained: one enlarged the community's fiscal responsibilities, the other demonstrated the public credibility of negotiated commitments.

A tangible contrast helps the two episodes belong together. The deferred ʿumrah tested whether public promises about access to the sanctuary would be honoured; the Khaybar agreement concerned agricultural labour over repeated harvests. Bukhārī 2329 transmits Ibn ʿUmar's report of an arrangement for half the produce of fruit or crops, with later provisions for the Prophet's wives also narrated. The reported initial agricultural settlement and later adjustments belonged to distinct stages of Khaybar's administration. Khaybar involved continued cultivation under an agricultural arrangement; the deferred pilgrimage depended on a negotiated agreement with Quraysh.

The deferred pilgrimage under the treaty also had a distinct function. It was an embodied return to a sanctuary that had shaped Meccan worship and identity, now undertaken by a community whose collective commitments had changed. Religiously it confirmed the continuity of the Kaʿbah with Abrahamic worship; politically it showed that an agreement could restrain old enemies while allowing public rites. Later narratives sometimes run treaty, Khaybar and pilgrimage together, but their institutional consequences should remain distinguishable. The linked expansion of alliance and obligation made the eventual breach between Quraysh-associated and Muslim-associated tribes especially consequential.

The reported agreement allowed local cultivators to work the land in return for a stipulated share of its produce. Ibn ʿUmar's report in al-Bukhārī 2331 and 4248 says that Khaybar's Jewish cultivators were to work the land for half of its produce. The two versions pass through the same Ibn ʿUmar–Nāfiʿ line, so their agreement is not independent documentary corroboration. This nevertheless grounds an explanation of why victory led immediately to questions of labour, possession, revenue and ongoing obligations. The record does not define the land-tenure status of every household or settle later juristic disputes about agricultural contracts. [Al-Bukhārī 2331](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2331); [4248](https://sunnah.com/bukhari/64/283).

## 18. The return to Makkah and the incorporation of Arabia

**Matn.** When a conflict between allied tribes broke the arrangements of al-Ḥudaybiyyah, the Prophet marched toward Makkah in 630 with a force large enough to limit organized resistance. He entered the city, the idols in the Kaʿbah were removed, and the sanctuary's worship was redirected to the one God. Meccan families who had opposed the mission now faced a ruler who was also their kinsman; accounts of the entry emphasize grants of security and acts of reconciliation, though particular exceptions and incidents require careful reading. The new position did not make every Arabian allegiance secure. A coalition including Hawāzin confronted the Muslims at Ḥunayn, and campaigns, settlements and distribution of property followed. Delegations came to Madinah from different regions, while teachers, collectors and appointed officials carried instructions beyond the Prophet's immediate presence. The challenge changed with the scale of allegiance: people needed to know how to pray, contribute, settle claims and maintain obligations after an envoy departed. The mission that had begun in a single household now involved a network of communities and local leaders, whose integration depended upon teaching and administration as well as military strength.

**Sharḥ.** The conquest is discussed in Qurʾān 110 and in hadith and Sīrah accounts; the Ḥunayn reversals in 9:25–27. The exact numbers, speeches and scope of amnesty are not all equally secure. The principal Muslim accounts describe Muḥammad's entry into Makkah and removal of idols; reports about individual exceptions differ and require separate evaluation. The Meccan sanctuary was not discarded: it was redefined within the worship taught by the Prophet. That mattered because pilgrimage, kin memory and the practical gathering of tribes were already rooted in it.

Integration also depended on the limits of central presence. A new Muslim household in a distant region could not consult the Prophet about every prayer, marriage or commercial dispute. Envoys and local teachers therefore had to communicate a core of practice while learning what questions the community actually faced. Tax collection brought another kind of encounter: people had to know the amount, purpose and authority of payment, and collectors had to be trusted not to abuse their power. Religious identity could expand rapidly after military victory, but the competence to live that identity required sustained instruction. After the Farewell Pilgrimage, the Companions carried the Prophet's instructions into communities that had not witnessed his conduct directly.

Incorporation is not an event completed by the removal of idols. After entry into Makkah, teachers, delegates and collectors had to make ritual competence and recurrent payments possible in regions distant from the Prophet. Newly allied groups could acknowledge the Prophet's authority before teachers and revenue agents established regular religious practice; formal allegiance alone did not establish inward conviction. The exact accounts of amnesty, individual exceptions and numbers of troops require report-level comparison.

Once many groups entered new relations, instructions required carriers. An envoy could convey belief and prayer, but regular contributions and disputes called for local collectors, teachers and judgments. The Prophet's dealings with delegations thus show the beginnings of a transmission problem: guidance could not depend indefinitely upon every person travelling to Madinah. Religious language, social custom and economic arrangements varied across Arabia. The Farewell Pilgrimage would give a public and widely witnessed example of worship while drawing attention to common obligations; the Prophet's subsequent death would make questions of transmission and authority unavoidable.

## 19. The Farewell Pilgrimage and the responsibilities that survived the Prophet

**Matn.** At the final pilgrimage the Prophet returned publicly to the rites of the sanctuary that had once been closed to his followers. Believers from different districts saw him perform the pilgrimage and heard instructions that joined worship to obligations toward other people. The rites ordered movement between Makkah, ʿArafāt, Muzdalifah and Minā; the address emphasized the inviolability of life and property, duties within families and the ending of inherited exploitative practices. Delegates and teachers carried these teachings to places whose inhabitants could not all attend a gathering in Madinah. The Prophet's household transmitted much that public assemblies could not preserve, including private worship, speech, conduct with children and the treatment of dependants. In his final illness, prayer and concern for the community continued while companions faced the prospect of his absence. When he died in 632, the revelation would not be renewed through another prophet. Worship, property obligations, local judgments and the communication of his teaching nevertheless continued. The community had to distinguish tasks previously united in one life: governing, preserving recitation and reports, issuing judgments and learning how to act. Different arrangements for those tasks became major features of subsequent Islamic civilization.

**Sharḥ.** The pilgrimage's actions are preserved through differing Companions, notably the extended report of Jābir in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Book of Pilgrimage, no. 1218 in common numbering. Qurʾān 5:3 is linked in the received tradition to the completion of religion, while legal details arise through multiple reports and juristic comparisons. Versions of the Farewell Sermon differ in wording, while repeatedly addressing the sanctity of life and property, inherited abuses and obligations between members of the community. The historical significance of embodied instruction is strong: when an act is publicly demonstrated before witnesses, its later transmission can draw upon what people saw as well as what they heard. But witnesses differ in attention and memory, explaining why later scholars examined reports and reconciled apparent discrepancies.

After the Prophet's death, legal adjudication, report transmission and spiritual instruction increasingly developed through different scholars and institutions. What did the Prophet teach or do? Who witnessed it? Which of those actions binds others, and under what conditions? Who has authority to judge a dispute now? What dispositions make the instruction inwardly fruitful? Hadith criticism addresses the reliability of transmission; jurisprudence its normative application; political theory the government of the community; spiritual instruction its realization in character. In the Prophet's lifetime these functions met in a single authoritative person, though many companions already served as teachers or agents. His death therefore did not create knowledge out of nothing, but changed how different kinds of authority were accessed and coordinated.

The Prophet's death made an analytical distinction unavoidable. His authority included receiving revelation, teaching its meaning, judging disputes, leading worship and directing communal affairs. The end of prophecy did not end any of the community's practical needs, yet the functions could not all be inherited in the same manner. Sunni accounts emphasize the formation of the caliphate and the wider transmission of knowledge by the Companions; Imāmī accounts place particular interpretive authority in the divinely designated Imams. Sunni and Shiʿi accounts of the Prophet's final instructions later informed distinct understandings of political leadership and religious authority. Al-Ghazālī, Shādhilī authors and Akbarian thinkers later explored prophetic inheritance as the formation of character or realization of spiritual wisdom. These are serious retrospective interpretations, not evidence that the first assembly at Saqīfah already used their technical distinctions. The demands of leadership and expansion made these distinctions urgent long before jurists and spiritual authors gave them their later technical forms.

The Farewell Pilgrimage provides a public model of worship, but a great deal of the Sunnah reached later generations through domestic life and instruction outside large gatherings. Reports through ʿĀʾishah, Umm Salamah and others matter particularly for private conduct and interpretation of what the Prophet intended. Later scholars' distinctions between common practice, judicial rulings and prophetic particularities are analytical responses to the differing settings of those reports; they are not a ready-made classification uttered at the final pilgrimage.

The later question of succession arises because different functions performed by the Prophet could be transmitted in different ways without implying that revelation continued. A transmitted report could preserve his practice; a judge could apply it; a teacher could cultivate virtues exemplified in it; political leadership required a decision about public allegiance and coercive power. Sunni and Shiʿi traditions developed different doctrines of legitimate succession, while later spiritual authors elaborated additional accounts of prophetic inheritance. Ibn ʿArabī, Qūnawī and Farghānī developed distinct technical accounts of *walāyah* through their own arguments and writings (Dossier 7).

# Book IV — Succession, conquest and imperial government

## 20. After the Prophet: allegiance and the survival of obligations

**Matn.** After the Prophet's death the Anṣār met at the Saqīfah of Banū Sāʿidah to consider leadership; Abū Bakr and other leading emigrants joined the discussion, and he received allegiance, followed by broader acceptance. The Prophet's family had not taken part in the initial meeting, and disagreement over leadership and certain property claims remained. Meanwhile, some Arabian groups questioned obligations that had bound them to Madinah, disputed zakāt payments or followed rival claimants to prophecy. Abū Bakr insisted that the public obligations established during the Prophet's life were not dissolved by his death. The campaigns of the Ridda, including the fighting against Musaylimah at al-Yamāmah, restored control over much of Arabia at considerable cost. The same period stimulated concern for preserving the recited text and for maintaining officials and commanders across distant communities. Abū Bakr's caliphate lasted only two years, but it settled an immediate question of government in practice: a common religious life would require leadership capable of acting after the Messenger's death. It did not settle every later theological question about who possessed rightful interpretive and spiritual authority.

**Sharḥ.** The surviving accounts identify disputes over allegiance, consultation, the Prophet's family and the authority of the first caliph. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, reports concerning the allegiance and ʿUmar's retrospective description of Saqīfah, and the traditions concerning Fāṭimah's claim and ʿAlī's later allegiance are essential Sunni witnesses. They require direct reading, not paraphrases that erase conflict. Sunni theology generally distinguishes the non-prophetic office of caliph from the Prophetic gift of revelation; Twelver Shiʿi theology argues that ʿAlī and the Imams possess a divinely designated leadership and authoritative religious guidance. Ghadīr Khumm and the relation of *mawlā* to *wilāyah* feature in the dispute, but a full evaluation of competing reports would displace the history's principal purpose. Sunni discussions generally ground Abū Bakr's caliphate in allegiance and communal obligation; Twelver Shiʿi discussions understand ʿAlī's authority through the doctrine of designation and the Imam's religious office.

The difficulty of succession was especially acute because the Prophet left several kinds of obligation in operation. A governor needed orders, a tax collector needed authorization, an army needed command, and a family needed to know whom to consult over a dispute. The early pledge to Abū Bakr addressed the public coordination of those tasks. It did not remove affection and distinct religious claims attached to the Prophet's household. Keeping these forms of concern separate clarifies both the practical necessity Sunni sources emphasize and the continuing Imāmī argument about divinely designated guidance. Conquests subsequently multiplied the need for commanders, governors, judges and officials able to administer distant provinces.

Abū Bakr's insistence on continued zakāt payments was a decision about whether public obligations survived the Prophet's death. Enforcing zakāt after the Prophet's death concerned public obligation; the grounds of authoritative succession remained a separate theological dispute. The Sunni account treats his caliphate as the first political continuation; Twelver Shiʿi doctrine gives ʿAlī a divinely designated place in the succession to religious guidance. Only the indispensable evidence and principal interpretive difference belong here; the disputed speeches and details of Fadak can remain in a focused note.

The Ridda involved more than a uniform attempt to renounce religion. The actions of different tribes concerned tax, allegiance, local autonomy and prophetic claimants in varying combinations. Keeping military and fiscal relationships functioning was crucial to sustaining the new association: without it, local commanders, teachers and communities would operate under incompatible claims of authority. Al-Bukhārī and later collections associate losses at al-Yamāmah with a collection of Qurʾānic material; other reports also attest written Qurʾānic passages before 632. The expansion after Abū Bakr built on this reconsolidation but exposed the community to institutions much larger than those of Arabia.

## 21. Conquest and the problems inherited from older empires

**Matn.** Abū Bakr had struggled to maintain the obligations of communities already associated with Madinah. Under ʿUmar, armies moving beyond Arabia confronted the established powers of Syria, Iraq and Egypt. The battles at al-Yarmūk and al-Qādisiyyah brought military victory, but governing the conquered lands demanded skills unlike those needed to win them. Towns still required grain; fields still needed irrigation; churches, monasteries and village communities had their own leadership; tax officials understood accounts in Greek, Coptic or Persian. Soldiers expected payment and the people who remained on the land expected to cultivate it. The new rulers could not make all these arrangements anew. They negotiated submissions, retained many local officials and gathered revenue through modified versions of older systems. Garrison settlements, especially al-Baṣrah, al-Kūfah and al-Fusṭāṭ, kept substantial bodies of troops together without dispersing them among every village. Victory thus brought the Muslims into daily responsibility for populations whose religious affiliations, languages and economic practices had not changed with their governors. These responsibilities created the setting in which later debates about land, taxation, public justice and the rights of non-Muslim communities became unavoidable.

**Sharḥ.** Conquest gave commanders territory, but administering that territory required decisions about cultivators, local officials, soldiers and revenue. Armies could acquire territorial authority in a campaigning season; provisioning troops and assessing land required continuing negotiation with cultivators, notables and former officials. Al-Balādhurī's *Futūḥ al-buldān* preserves accounts of treaties and settlements, but details vary across recensions and may reflect subsequent fiscal controversies. Administrative papyri and coins offer independent controls on gradual changes in government and language. Muslim governors adapted some existing fiscal practices and altered others; the conquered provinces did not immediately share one uniform tax code. The Syrian, Iraqi and Egyptian arrangements followed different trajectories.

The division between garrison and countryside helps explain why the conquerors' language and legal practices could spread without immediately making the subject population Muslim. Tax resources went to military stipends and government, while established cultivators and religious institutions continued many activities. The eventual change of language, conversion and local custom therefore took generations and differed by region. Governors in conquered provinces had to determine levies, recognize land claims, uphold agreements and restrain soldiers far from Madinah. The moral language of justice was inherited from the Prophetic teaching, but its application now involved a more complex set of agents and documents. Hugh Kennedy and the *New Cambridge History of Islam* document the variation among conquest settlements; surviving treaty reports do not preserve one uniform agreement.

Dated receipts and correspondence from Egypt preserve administrative practices that continued or changed under Muslim rule. A landholder and a soldier encountered power differently: the soldier needed regular provisions; the cultivator needed a predictable demand and protection from arbitrary seizure. Their interaction connected military policy to harvest cycles and irrigation. Nor is the word *jizyah* consistently used for one identical early fiscal arrangement in all sources; terminology and practice need local comparison. This matters to the later juristic tradition because arguments about the rights of cultivators and protected communities emerged in the aftermath of real negotiated settlements, some remembered through legalizing retrospective narratives. After victory, officials collected revenue, cultivators worked the land, and commanders supplied troops; these recurring duties sustained political control beyond the battlefield.

## 22. The garrison, the treasury and the provincial community

**Matn.** In the new garrison towns, soldiers lived with their families, received stipends and gradually made places for merchants, freed persons, jurists and teachers. Their quarters often followed tribal affiliations; common prayer and military command therefore existed beside familiar bonds of kin and patronage. ʿUmar's registers of payment recognized differences of service and standing, and the distribution of revenue required information about households, campaigns and rights of recipients. In Iraq, keeping extensive cultivated land under continuing taxation, rather than dividing it all permanently among victorious soldiers, sustained payments beyond a single generation. Arab tribal groups had become provincial communities, but the money that sustained them came increasingly from settled agricultural populations. At the same time, new Muslims entered these communities through conversion, marriage, alliance and clientage. Their claims to social equality and fiscal treatment did not always fit the privileges of the earliest military settlers. The government's efforts to maintain cohesion created problems that accompanied the Umayyad period: who counted as a member of the ruling community, how service was rewarded, and whether the treasury belonged to an army, a dynasty or the wider body of believers.

**Sharḥ.** The *dīwān* was a register and payment system, not the equivalent of a modern civil service or a census of all residents. Accounts of garrison foundations and stipend rankings vary, while later settlement and fiscal needs altered administrative practice. Fred Donner traces the interaction of military recruitment, settlement and revenue administration during the early Islamic conquests. The precise policy on Iraqi land is known partly through retrospective juristic accounts in which allocation of conquered lands had become a legal problem.

Ibn Khaldūn later argued that group solidarity helps establish military authority, while durable rule also requires revenue and administration across wider territories. Yet he cannot substitute for the details of seventh-century stipends and provinces. A converted Persian client in al-Kūfah, a landed Christian family in Syria and an Arab soldier receiving payments did not enter the same arrangement. Soldiers, cultivators, clients, officials and scholars held different claims on revenues, property and public authority. It also explains why towns of conquest became centres of learning: they housed people who needed judgments on marriage, sale, slavery, inheritance and disputes involving communities with different practices. Students learned from Companions and successors amid these recurring problems rather than in a social vacuum.

Differences in military stipends shaped household income, prestige and competition for public resources. If an early participant in the campaigns held a recognised claim on the treasury, a later convert or client might not possess an equivalent claim even when sharing the same worship. That tension helps explain debates about the *mawālī* and the eventual pressures on Umayyad government. Clients and provincial populations encountered unequal arrangements, although access to stipends and public office varied across regions and periods. Some were wealthy and influential, and recruitment and fiscal practice changed. The closer historical explanation follows a particular garrison's register, its sources of revenue and its social composition. The relation between inherited solidarity and religious universalism becomes visible through conflicts over payment and status. It also clarifies why instruction mattered: a growing town needed means to identify valid testimony, settle competing property claims and teach new residents obligations that had developed first among a much smaller community.

## 23. Civil war and the limits of loyalty

**Matn.** Expansion increased the powers of provincial commanders and the resources at stake in their appointments. Complaints against ʿUthmān's government sharpened until rebels killed him in Madinah in 656. ʿAlī received allegiance while others demanded that the murderers first be punished; in the disorder no agreement existed about who could enforce that punishment or command the armies needed to do so. Fighting followed near al-Baṣrah and later at Ṣiffīn against Muʿāwiyah's Syrian forces. Arbitration, proposed to halt the conflict, divided ʿAlī's supporters and led to further violence with the Khārijites. ʿAlī was killed in 661. Al-Ḥasan then made a settlement with Muʿāwiyah, bringing an end to that phase of warfare. Supporters divided over allegiance to a caliph, responsibility for ʿUthmān's killing, obedience to commanders and the Prophet's family's claims to leadership. Those disputes survived the immediate fighting and later shaped accounts of legitimate authority, grave sin and rebellion.

**Sharḥ.** The broad order of events is well attested in early Muslim historiography; the words attributed to individual participants, precise motives and details of arbitration require more caution. Al-Ṭabarī brings together reports from transmitters with different affiliations, and these are not independent eyewitness accounts merely because they appear side by side. Sunni histories recognize the leading Companions' standing while preserving accounts of disputed decisions, armed conflict and deaths among their supporters. Shiʿi accounts give the rights and designation of ʿAlī a different constitutional and theological meaning. Sunni and Shiʿi arguments about legitimate authority developed amid disputes over retaliation, military allegiance and the duties of government.

The civil war also had geographical and material mechanisms. The garrison army of Iraq and the Syrian command were organized forces with their own leadership, pay and attachments. Muʿāwiyah could rely upon a developed provincial military apparatus; ʿAlī had to govern amid divided coalitions and disputes about the killing of ʿUthmān. Later kalām arguments about a grave sinner and political obedience grew in a world that remembered those choices. Rival factions invoked justice, but disputed who possessed authority to punish ʿUthmān's killers and whether political allegiance must precede retaliation. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Balādhurī preserve differing accounts of the first fitna, especially in reported speeches and motives; their transmissions do not independently establish each participant's private intentions.

Later theological classifications of the first fitna do not establish the exact beliefs or motives of every participant in the original conflicts. The battle at Ṣiffīn and the division over arbitration help explain how political disagreement prompted debates over the relation between judgment and divine law. The outcomes of the battles do not establish that every participant held the fully developed doctrines later associated with Khārijites, Sunnis or Shiʿa. The extant speeches are transmitted through literary and partisan filters. The strongest causal account connects the troops' alliances, demands about the killing of ʿUthmān and the procedural problem of retaliation. The later theological writings can then be read as responses to the remembered crisis, with their own dates and purposes. The disputes over allegiance, retaliation and ʿAlī's authority shaped later Sunni and Shiʿi understandings of legitimate government.

## 24. Damascus, Karbalāʾ and the making of an imperial administration

**Matn.** Muʿāwiyah ruled from Damascus with Syrian military support and an administration able to draw on older provincial practices. His nomination of Yazīd placed hereditary succession at the centre of a dispute that the settlement with al-Ḥasan had not finally resolved. Al-Ḥusayn refused allegiance, travelled toward Kūfah after appeals from supporters, and was killed with members of his family and companions at Karbalāʾ in 680. The event gave the claims of the Prophet's household an enduring place in Muslim religious memory. Civil war continued until ʿAbd al-Malik consolidated authority. Under him and his successors, the state issued new coinage, enlarged the role of Arabic in administration and invested in prominent buildings, including the Dome of the Rock. Governors and armies extended authority toward the Iberian Peninsula, Central Asia and Sind. The resulting empire was increasingly visible as a single political order, but its provinces differed in language, law and social composition. The government could regulate revenue and command troops more readily than it could determine what every believer should accept as authoritative religious teaching.

**Sharḥ.** Al-Ḥusayn and his companions were killed at Karbalāʾ; reports of speeches and exact casualty figures vary among later transmitters. The death of al-Ḥusayn and his companions is foundational to Shiʿi historical memory, while Sunni accounts also affirm the wrong and grief of the event, even as theological readings diverge. Al-Ṭabarī's use of Abū Mikhnaf and later treatments by al-Mufīd overlap in transmission; agreement between them cannot automatically be counted as independent attestation.

Umayyad coins, inscriptions and buildings record official policies and dynastic claims that later chronicles recount through their own traditions. Currency reform mattered because weights, taxes and payments connected distant provinces through common state symbols and procedures. Arabic administrative change was uneven, and the presence of Arabic in registers did not immediately displace Greek, Coptic and Persian from society. Nor should political consolidation be equated with universal religious acceptance. Schools of Qurʾānic recitation, ascetic teaching and early legal discussion grew within and sometimes against the horizons of state power. Al-Ḥajjāj's governorship and the experience of Iraqi opposition also reveal coercive dimensions of the imperial project. The distinction between the caliph's political authority and a teacher's credibility becomes more intelligible through these particular institutional pressures.

The Dome of the Rock supplies a particularly revealing contrast between official and social evidence. Its Arabic inscriptions present theological claims in a public imperial setting; its construction dates and physical form can be investigated independently of late court anecdotes. Coin inscriptions and administrative documents trace changes in how the state represented and financed itself. Yet none of these tells us what every market trader believed or how every household taught the Qurʾān. That difference matters: an empire may adopt common public signs while local traditions continue in distinct languages and social groups. In later decades the *mawālī*, tax burdens and provincial grievances entered contests over rule. The Abbasid movement arose within a consolidated Umayyad empire where political stability had not settled competing claims to leadership and status.

## 25. The Abbasid revolution and a change of ruling centre

**Matn.** By the middle of the eighth century, the Umayyad empire possessed formidable fiscal and military resources but faced opposition among groups dissatisfied with provincial rule, status and the distribution of authority. In Khurāsān, networks of Arab settlers and others were mobilized in a movement promising rule associated with the Prophet's family. Under Abū Muslim's command, its armies advanced west and defeated the Umayyads in 750. The Abbasids became caliphs, and the centre of government moved toward Iraq. The revolution did not redistribute authority equally among its supporters: the new rulers built a dynasty with its own claims, commanders and administrators, while ʿAlid expectations persisted. Al-Manṣūr chose a new site on the Tigris for Baghdad, bringing the court closer to Iraqi agricultural revenues, eastern communication routes and the skills of Persianate secretarial households. What had begun as a contest over legitimate rule became the foundation of a metropolis where political power, commerce and scholarship would meet. The earlier garrison cities remained significant, but the new capital drew specialists from several regions and languages into a shared urban economy.

**Sharḥ.** The Abbasid movement recruited support across several groups, including Khurāsānian military and political networks, and did not represent only non-Arab converts. The movement involved overlapping interests in Khurāsān, including established Arab settler communities, patron-client networks, religious expectations and military organization. Its slogans did not prescribe a single later theory of authority. Consult al-Ṭabarī alongside studies of the Abbasid revolution by M. A. Shaban and Elton Daniel, while recognizing disagreements in their reconstructions.

Khurāsānian support and the establishment of Baghdad in Iraq shifted the caliphate's military and administrative centre eastward. Iraq sat near productive agricultural districts and routes connecting Iran, the Gulf and Syria; Baghdad's foundation under al-Manṣūr in 762 deliberately concentrated administration. But courts do not create intellectual life by decree. The city inherited teachers, translators and merchants from older centres, while paper, libraries, private patrons and students increasingly made scholarship possible beyond the immediate court. Baghdad's scholarly activity depended on administrators, merchants, patrons, booksellers, translators and teachers, not on its foundation alone. Dynastic legitimacy, in turn, continued to be debated among jurists, Alids and different provincial populations; the Abbasid revolution settled who ruled, not every question of who could teach or interpret revelation.

# Book V — How knowledge and spiritual formation acquired lasting institutions

## 26. Baghdad and the work of a learned city

**Matn.** Baghdad drew court officials, traders, physicians, craftsmen and students to a place where goods and ideas arrived by river, road and caravan. The treasury paid armies and officials, but private households and patrons also supported books, teachers and specialized work. Physicians inherited Greek and Syriac medical learning and adapted it to new patients and institutions; mathematicians and astronomers addressed problems arising from calculation, observation and earlier sciences. Translators could work across Syriac, Greek and Arabic, while jurists and transmitters met learners who came to study accounts of the Prophet and their implications. Paper and book markets made it easier to reproduce, annotate and circulate substantial texts. Yet the city's different enquiries did not form one institution: some occurred in courts, others in mosques, private houses, bookshops or medical circles. The caliphs could sponsor scholars and also coerce them. As the city expanded, its intellectual authority depended increasingly on the quality of transmission and argument within communities of learning as well as the favour of those who ruled it.

**Sharḥ.** Hārūn al-Rashīd's court and al-Maʾmūn's patronage are important, but the familiar story of a single 'House of Wisdom' producing all the sciences suppresses the multiplicity of patrons and workplaces. Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq's translation projects, al-Kindī's philosophical circle and the mathematical writing associated with al-Khwārizmī had distinct aims and constituencies. Dimitri Gutas, *Greek Thought, Arabic Culture*, and research on medicine and translation distinguish court translation from wider patterns of scholarly patronage. The adoption of paper expanded the possibilities for copying without making manuscripts cheap for every reader.

Baghdad gathered translators, booksellers, physicians, administrators and patrons who sustained scholarly work through different institutions. Secretarial administration required numeracy and records; medicine attracted patrons and clinical expertise; juridical differences demanded teachers able to transmit and reason; rich households could sustain independent specialists. State patrons, merchants and scholarly specialists jointly sustained Baghdad's translation and book culture, a relationship central to Hodgson's account of the period. But the learned world was not always harmoniously plural. In the *miḥnah*, caliphal demands about the createdness of the Qurʾān brought public theology and state coercion together. Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal's refusal and later memory of the episode exemplify a struggle over the boundary between the ruler's command and a scholar's religious authority. It would be misleading either to call this a conflict between 'religion' and 'science' or to identify all speculative inquiry with official coercion.

The history of translation is most persuasive when treated through a defined task. Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq's work on Greek medical texts involved comparing copies, choosing technical equivalents and revising earlier translations; a practitioner then had to interpret the resulting Arabic work in relation to patients and other medical knowledge. The procedure differed from al-Khwārizmī's mathematical writing or a jurist's evaluation of a report, even when they shared bookshops and patrons. For this reason 'Islamic science' is a historical umbrella, not the name of one single method. During the *miḥnah*, caliphal authorities sponsored intellectual inquiry while pressuring scholars to affirm a particular doctrine about the Qurʾān. Intellectual life became durable through a plurality of communities capable of criticising, transmitting and sponsoring knowledge, although their relative independence varied. Individual translations passed through named patrons, scribes, translators and readers, often with several stages of revision.

## 27. Travelers, households and the authentication of transmitted learning

**Matn.** A student wishing to learn the Prophet's teaching could begin with a parent or a local teacher, but difficult reports often sent students to other towns. They asked who had heard a narration, compared its wording, and learned how particular authorities prayed, judged or answered questions. The journeys formed a network in which Madinah, Makkah, Kūfah, Baṣrah, Damascus, Egypt and later Khurāsān were connected by repeated encounters. Teachers sometimes disagreed because they possessed different reports or understood an action's circumstances differently. As traditions multiplied, scholars recorded transmitters' lives and examined interruptions, discrepancies and defects in chains and texts. The collections associated with al-Bukhārī and Muslim emerged within this world of travel, questioning and written compilation. Women as well as men preserved and taught reports, beginning prominently with members of the Prophet's household and continuing through later scholarly families. These practices made religious memory portable, yet they also demanded a discipline of verification: a report's presence in a book did not alone determine what it meant or how it should be applied.

**Sharḥ.** The *isnād* was an explicit claim about routes of transmission, not a mechanical guarantee of truth. Some students transmitted from personal audition, some from reading or authorization, and scholars debated these methods. Differences among *ṣaḥīḥ*, *ḥasan*, *ḍaʿīf* and other classifications developed through particular critical conventions; the two canonical Ṣaḥīḥs were not produced under one uniform ancient state procedure. The introductory material in Muslim's *Ṣaḥīḥ*, al-Bukhārī's chapter headings and later discussions by Ibn al-Ṣalāḥ help explain the criteria. The historical date of a report and a ḥadīth critic's judgment of its acceptability answer separate questions. Harald Motzki compares chains and wording to reconstruct stages of transmission; scholars contest some of the conclusions drawn from those comparisons.

The household was not marginal to this process. ʿĀʾishah was asked about private worship, domestic conduct and the circumstances of legal pronouncements because these subjects often lay outside male public observation. Later women transmitters and teachers, documented in biographical dictionaries, were associated with scholarly households and institutions, although participation varied by region and century. Households, mosques and traveling students preserved Prophetic practice across cities without relying on a single caliphal archive. Traveling students and scholarly correspondence gradually connected distant communities while leaving room for local practices and disagreements.

Report critics compared transmissions from different cities to identify common wording, variations and possible shared sources. If two students in different cities transmitted what they said was the same Prophetic report, the critic might compare the named links in their chains, the wording of the two texts and reports about the reliability and contact of the transmitters. Agreement might reflect shared earlier transmission rather than independent corroboration; difference might arise from abbreviation, memory or a genuine alternate wording. Inclusion in a collection did not settle a report's wording, original setting or legal application. A jurist might accept a report's transmission yet debate whether an action was obligatory, recommended or context-specific. Jurists then assessed whether reliable reports established obligation, recommendation, permission or a ruling specific to one occasion. Women such as ʿĀʾishah transmitted knowledge through identifiable households and teaching relationships; their roles and opportunities varied among regions and generations.

Transmission became accountable precisely because different students could preserve different wordings or levels of detail. A particularly strong example comes from the *Muwaṭṭaʾ*: El Shamsy compares the recensions of Yaḥyā al-Laythī, Abū Muṣʿab and Ibn Bukayr with quotations in al-Shāfiʿī's *Umm*. Some variants alter only phrasing; others affect the degree of agreement a position claims, the narrator chain, or the teacher's attached explanation. Different recensions preserve variations in wording, attribution and juristic comment, so no single printed recension reproduces every form in which a teaching circulated. [El Shamsy, 2021](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/a14wz-9hc46).

## 28. Legal schools emerge from the need to decide recurrent cases

**Matn.** Merchants still sold on credit, spouses disputed entitlements, heirs divided estates and judges faced disagreements between neighbours. New places brought new customs and documentary practices, while transmitted guidance had to be applied to circumstances not described in identical terms by earlier reports. Teachers in Iraq, the Ḥijāz, Syria and Egypt developed methods for reaching and defending judgments. Abū Ḥanīfah's students preserved and extended Iraqi legal discussions; Mālik gathered reports and the practice of Madinan scholars; al-Shāfiʿī examined the authority of different kinds of proof; Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal became a prominent model of reliance on transmitted evidence. Over generations, their successors compared opinions, taught precedents and produced manuals. The resulting schools preserved characteristic procedures without freezing every judgment at the founder's death. They allowed communities far from the first centres to ask similar questions in a shared vocabulary, even while arriving at different rulings. Lawyers and judges thereby acquired influence independent of any single dynasty, although appointments, patronage and local social hierarchies continued to shape their work.

**Sharḥ.** Disputes over sales, agency and liability required jurists to apply transmitted rulings and broader principles to new transactions. Suppose a merchant gives goods to an agent who sells to a third party, then disputes the price or liability for loss. A jurist needs to know the contractual terms, local usage, rules of agency and the relation of evidence to general principle. Competing schools may differ because they classify the transaction differently, trust different reports or apply analogical reasoning under different restrictions. The legal concepts are formed precisely to make difficult cases teachable and repeatable. Al-Shāfiʿī's *al-Risālah*, Mālik's *Muwaṭṭaʾ*, works transmitted from Muḥammad al-Shaybānī and early Ḥanbalī collections arose in different settings; later school manuals reorganized their arguments and rulings.

The legal schools were historically made by students, teachers and later jurists. For this reason a school's 'position' in a late manual cannot simply be attributed to its eponymous founder. Theological commitments, juristic reasoning and actual courts intersected without being identical. The Sunni four-school map was also not a description of every Muslim juristic tradition: Imāmī and Zaydī law developed distinct authorities, and other early schools later lost institutional continuity. For broad historical interpretation use Wael Hallaq, *The Origins and Evolution of Islamic Law*; for the necessary critical note, compare its reconstructions with primary texts and other modern models of legal emergence. Jurists trained students to classify acts, resolve conflicts among reports and reason from principles when inherited formulations did not settle a new case.

A revealing manuscript-level comparison concerns Mālik's *Muwaṭṭaʾ*. Its transmitted recensions differ, and the version associated with Muḥammad al-Shaybānī sometimes sets Mālik's Medinan positions beside Ḥanafī objections. Teachers carried texts between cities, compared their variants and argued about the rulings attributed to earlier authorities. Later western recensions had their own histories of circulation and authority. A student who learns one line of argument about sales, inheritance or ritual practice may therefore enter a dialogue already shaped by several teachers. The school becomes a discipline when these arguments can be preserved, criticised and extended while remaining recognisably related. Wael Hallaq's histories of school formation provide a model for examining this institutional process, but individual recensions and their manuscript witnesses remain the primary controls.

One *Muwaṭṭaʾ* report describes Ibn ʿUmar sprinkling water in his eyes during *ghusl*, while recensions differ over Mālik's accompanying juristic judgment. One recension preserves the practice without Mālik's judgment; al-Shāfiʿī quotes Mālik's comment that it was not accepted practice, while two other recensions give comparable statements that it was not obligatory. The recensions distinguish Ibn ʿUmar's reported conduct, Mālik's interpretation of its legal force and the rulings attributed to later schools. Students and jurists used such variants to decide whether reported conduct established an obligation, a recommendation or a permitted practice. [El Shamsy's aligned references](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/a14wz-9hc46), pp. 367–368.

## 29. Endowments, colleges and the social life of scholarship

**Matn.** A teacher could attract students through learning and reputation, but lessons required rooms, books, food and sometimes long years free from earning a living. Rulers, merchants and wealthy families established endowed mosques, hostels and colleges to support teachers, students, Qurʾān readers, jurists and visitors. The Niẓāmiyyah founded in Baghdad in the eleventh century became a prominent example of organized patronage. Other towns developed institutions suited to local political and family arrangements. Endowments could secure a scholar's work beyond a patron's lifetime, yet appointments to teaching chairs also became prizes in contests among households and officials. Learning thus acquired durable places while remaining dependent on personal instruction and recognition. A student still needed a teacher who could explain difficult passages and authorize their transmission; a building did not automatically certify understanding. The college, household library, mosque and Sufi lodge therefore became connected, sometimes competing settings in which law, grammar, recitation, theology and spiritual conduct were taught.

**Sharḥ.** The institution of *waqf* connected property to an intended continuing purpose under legally defined conditions. Its resources could pay stipends, maintain lodging and support copying; they could also sustain the prestige of the founding household. Michael Chamberlain documents how medieval Damascene colleges operated through endowments, elite patronage, scholarly households and personal relationships rather than modern university bureaucracy. In Damascus, endowed institutions were bound to elite strategies, offices and ceremonies, while much learning still occurred personally and privately. George Makdisi's account of institutionalized education is an important comparison, not an uncontested template.

The Prophet's mosque accommodated worship and instruction; later endowed colleges financed teachers and lodging through arrangements developed under different political conditions. The Prophet's mosque sustained public worship and instruction; later cities with large populations required more differentiated means of financing teachers and accommodating traveling students. An endowment is a historical institutional solution, not an originally complete blueprint present in seventh-century Madinah. It can, however, be evaluated within the religious language of charity, useful knowledge and obligation to others. The college also exposes a tension between credential and formation: a person may acquire the right to occupy a chair through patronage without demonstrating wisdom, while an influential teacher may have no such office. Teachers cultivated *adab* and stable capacities through particular curricula, exercises, personal correction and habits of study, although their methods differed among institutions.

A student at an endowed college could receive a stipend, lodging and instruction tied to the founder's stipulations. The property yield might provide a regular stipend, which made years of study feasible; a founder's stipulations might also determine which legal school occupied the teaching position. A respected scholar might then receive students because of the office, because of established expertise or because both reinforced one another. Chamberlain's Damascus evidence is important because it permits those mechanisms to be separated. Endowments, appointments, scholarly households and personal teaching relationships shaped who could study and who obtained authority to teach. Teachers and students also disagreed over whether paid office and public prestige encouraged learning, competition or vanity. It cannot be answered by assuming every patron was either altruistic or self-interested.

## 30. Translation, theological controversy and the uses of reason

**Matn.** Muslims confronted disagreements not only over actions but over what could be known about God, the world and human responsibility. Earlier debates about free choice, divine justice, attributes and the status of the Qurʾān developed among theologians who used argument to defend positions drawn from revelation and inherited instruction. Greek works on logic, natural philosophy and metaphysics became available in Arabic through translators working with Syriac and Greek learning. Thinkers such as al-Kindī and al-Fārābī fashioned new accounts of knowledge and its demonstration; physicians and mathematicians pursued different kinds of inquiry under the same broad literary language. Theologians and philosophers met on questions of causation, the soul and the beginning of the world, but did not necessarily mean the same thing by knowledge or proof. Al-Ashʿarī and al-Māturīdī emerged from different disputational settings and gave enduring Sunni theological vocabularies to these controversies. The encounter made the disciplines more technically precise while leaving unsettled which claims demonstration could establish and where revelation decisively guided judgment.

**Sharḥ.** Philosophers and theologians disputed whether created causes act with inviolable necessity and how such causation relates to miracles and divine agency. Different answers presuppose different accounts of nature, regularity and possibility. It would be a mistake to summarize all Ashʿarīs as holding a single identical philosophy of causation or to equate the whole of falsafah with denial of miracles. The problem is debated in al-Ghazālī's seventeenth discussion of the *Tahāfut*, but it had a longer prehistory in kalām and philosophical accounts of nature. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's 'Occasionalism' entry gives a useful map of disagreement even about how to read al-Ghazālī's ultimate position.

Arguments about God's attributes, moral obligation and human action also arise from different sources. Early Muʿtazilī positions are not interchangeable, and Sunni Ashʿarī and Māturīdī schools later developed their own internal debates. Ibn Sīnā's distinction between essence and existence and his proof of the necessary existent entered many later theological treatises, despite strong criticism of other Avicennan doctrines. The crucial historical change is not that 'Greek reason defeated' or 'Islam rejected reason.' Methods of inquiry moved across disciplines, were adapted to particular problems, and became objects of criticism by new authors. Primary controls include al-Ashʿarī's *Maqālāt*, al-Māturīdī's *Kitāb al-Tawḥīd*, al-Fārābī's works on the sciences, Ibn Sīnā's *Shifāʾ* and al-Ghazālī's *Tahāfut*. Differences in authorship, date and formulation can change the meaning of a philosophical proposition and the objections later commentators raise against it.

The *miḥnah* offers an early case of theological language becoming an instrument of public authority. During the *miḥnah*, caliphal officials demanded declarations about the Qurʾān's createdness, making conformity to a theological position a condition of public service. Muʿtazilī theologians developed arguments about divine justice and unity beyond the court's coercive policies; later Sunni theologians also employed rigorous dialectical argument. Later Sunni theologians used intricate logical methods while rejecting the imposition of a doctrine by a caliph. The controversy involved distinct claims about divine speech, legitimate proof and the ruler's authority to enforce a doctrinal declaration. These are distinct but interacting processes.

## 31. Al-Ghazālī, al-Rāzī and the remaking of philosophical theology

**Matn.** As knowledge became more specialized, the scholar who mastered one discipline could no longer assume that its methods answered every question. Al-Ghazālī studied legal reasoning, theological controversy and philosophy, then examined both the power and the limits of their claims. His later teaching joined the discipline of outward action to the examination of intention and character. Ibn Rushd, writing in al-Andalus, defended particular philosophical demonstrations against objections raised in al-Ghazālī's critique. In the eastern lands, Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī interrogated Ibn Sīnā's arguments about existence, definition, knowledge and the soul; Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī later answered and reorganized parts of that critical legacy. Their works entered new teaching chains through commentary and gloss, often in the same institutions that taught grammar, law and theology. A student could therefore meet rival accounts of the human intellect and causation within a shared curriculum of religious learning. Commentators preserved rival arguments, formulated objections and replies, and taught students to test the force of a demonstration rather than assume agreement.

**Sharḥ.** These are two interacting but distinct intellectual histories. In *Tahāfut al-falāsifah*, al-Ghazālī objects to identifiable claims associated with the philosophers, including an argument about causation and miracle; Ibn Rushd's *Tahāfut al-tahāfut* responds to the critique. The disagreement cannot be reconstructed adequately by the bare sentence 'reason and revelation were reconciled' or by assigning one universal causal theory to al-Ghazālī. His *Iḥyāʾ* is another undertaking, concerned with the ordering of worship, everyday action, destructive motives and saving virtues. Al-Ghazālī's critique of philosophical theses and his treatment of worship and character pursued related concerns within different genres and arguments.

A second history develops through the criticism of Ibn Sīnā's *al-Ishārāt*. Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī uses detailed commentary to expose problems in essence, existence, definition and demonstration; al-Ṭūsī's replies help establish a continuing debate. The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* entry on al-Rāzī documents particular positions and the care needed in inferring his considered conclusions from dialectical writings. Their commentaries preserved technical disputes about essence, existence and demonstration through successive generations of study. Commentaries became intellectual instruments: by setting out a lemma, objection, response and further objection, they required students to inhabit the logic of a question before accepting a resolution. That is relevant to the larger history of formation because the discipline of judgment can be cultivated through argument as well as through repeated moral actions. The achievements and failures of those pedagogic practices must still be judged from actual texts and institutions, not an idealization of scholasticism.

The dispute over essence and existence turned on what definitions establish about things outside the mind. Ibn Sīnā argues that a thing's essence can be understood without thereby affirming its existence; al-Rāzī questions what such a distinction establishes about real beings and whether a definition yields knowledge of an external essence. The reply cannot be assessed by saying merely that the later thinker 'synthesized' theology and philosophy. Al-Rāzī's dialectical writings sometimes test a thesis without endorsing it, so particular lemmas and objections determine which position he actually adopts. The later classroom practice of commenting line by line preserved these difficulties for students. It also introduced a risk of treating subtle distinctions as verbal contests detached from demonstration; whether that happened is a question about particular curricula and commentaries, not a verdict on late scholasticism as a whole.

## 32. The work of the heart becomes a teachable discipline

**Matn.** A believer could fulfil an outward obligation and still struggle with vanity, anger, envy or attachment to reputation. The first generations had learned to examine intention through prayer, almsgiving, fasting, companionship and restraint. As Muslim communities grew, teachers gathered such questions into more systematic instruction. Al-Muḥāsibī asked how a person could recognize motives that concealed themselves from self-examination; al-Junayd was remembered for disciplined sobriety in spiritual life; al-Qushayrī preserved teachings, terms and biographical examples for later students. Al-Ghazālī arranged the ordinary life of worship, eating, work, marriage and fellowship alongside destructive and saving dispositions. Prayer, trade, speech, anger and hope all engaged the same person's intentions and character. Thus the concern for the inward life did not arise as a substitute for law or communal obligation. Teachers developed particular methods for addressing what correct outward action might leave unformed. Their techniques varied, and the later organized paths were still taking shape; what endured was the question of how knowledge and repeated action become a stable character.

**Sharḥ.** Al-Ghazālī's *Iḥyāʾ* supplies an unusually clear architecture of the question: devotional acts, habits of ordinary living, qualities that destroy and qualities that save. Qurʾān 91:7–10 concerns purification of the soul, 107:4–7 rebukes ostentatious worship, and the ḥadīth of intention relates actions to their purposes. A jurist might determine whether a required donation was validly made, while a teacher of the heart could ask whether the giver sought recognition and became hostile when unnoticed. They need not issue contradictory verdicts: validity, motive and character are different objects of examination. Al-Muḥāsibī's *al-Riʿāyah* pursues self-monitoring, and al-Qushayrī's *Risālah* records a vocabulary of states and stations. Al-Muḥāsibī, al-Qushayrī and al-Ghazālī developed different analyses of intention, spiritual states and disciplined conduct in their respective works.

The category of *malakah* can help explain the historical pedagogy, provided it is not attributed indiscriminately to the first Companions. Repeated action can establish ease and stability, but a habit formed through vanity is not the same thing as a virtue. The spiritual author therefore asks about knowledge, intention, repeated practice and the social circumstances that reinforce or undermine a disposition. This bridges the Sīrah's early concern with prayer and mutual obligation to later manuals without pretending that the manuals already existed in the Prophet's lifetime. Prayer, self-examination and companionship developed distinctive vocabularies as teachers addressed recurring problems of intention, action and conduct.

The practice of self-examination takes place under conditions that can foster or frustrate it. A wealthy person may have more opportunities to give but also more occasions for display; a judge may face a temptation to regard being right as proof of being virtuous; a scholar may confuse mastery of a text with self-knowledge. Al-Ghazālī's discussion of blameworthy traits becomes relevant to such people precisely because their ordinary roles carry moral risks. Al-Ghazālī treated prayer, trade, social duties and concealed motives as connected aspects of moral formation. Early warnings against vanity do not establish an unbroken institutional lineage to later Sufi lodges. The stronger connection is the persistence of concrete religious problems and documented practices through which teachers addressed them.

Al-Ghazālī's own preface to the *Iḥyāʾ* explains why he combines worship, ordinary dealings, destructive qualities and saving qualities: the visible action and the condition of the agent are complementary objects of *ʿilm al-muʿāmalah*. Thus a person's prayer, debt, household conduct and anger all become occasions for diagnosing the relation between knowledge, intention and action. Al-Qushayrī's discussion of *ikhlāṣ* distinguishes acting for other people's approval from admiring oneself for acting well; Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh's aphorism gives concise form to the difference between the outward configuration of an act and its animating sincerity. Their terminology is later; their texts help explain why the history of religious practice generated disciplines of moral formation. ## 33. From the teacher's company to enduring Sufi networks

**Matn.** Students seeking spiritual instruction gathered around teachers whose conduct, teaching and prayers they trusted. Such relationships could remain informal, but travel, endowments and the movement of disciples gradually allowed them to outlast a single circle. By the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, residences and lodges supported instruction, hospitality, remembrance and service; later lineages developed recognizable methods and patterns of authorization. The Qādirī, Suhrawardī and Shādhilī traditions became influential through different regional paths, not by one centrally organized movement. In North Africa and Egypt, al-Mursī and Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh transmitted the teaching of Abū al-Ḥasan al-Shādhilī; Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh connected remembrance and reliance upon God with ordinary work. Ibn ʿArabī, writing in the same broad period, examined prophetic wisdom and the possibilities of human spiritual realization in a different metaphysical register. His interpreters, including al-Qūnawī and al-Farghānī, elaborated technical accounts of disclosure and Muhammadan perfection. These traditions overlapped in persons and settings without collapsing into one doctrine, and their spread depended upon teachers able to transmit both practices and ways of understanding them.

**Sharḥ.** Alexander Knysh, in *The New Cambridge History of Islam*, and Devin DeWeese's study of Sufi organizational patterns distinguish early ascetic circles from the diverse forms of *ṭarīqah* that acquired more durable organization from the twelfth century onward. Authority could descend through personal initiation, family succession or local custom, and a lodge might serve worship, hospitality, storage or public prestige. A lineage claim is a historical claim requiring examination; a devotional chain is not automatically a documentary record of uninterrupted administrative continuity.

The Shādhilī teachers addressed remembrance and reliance upon God in the circumstances of earning, family life and human relationships. Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh's *al-Ḥikam* and *Laṭāʾif al-minan* do not simply prescribe withdrawal: they address the intentions and misconceptions of people who must act, earn and encounter others. They treated ordinary duties as occasions for testing intention, reliance and remembrance. Ibn ʿArabī's *Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam*, by contrast, assigns distinctive wisdoms to prophets and asks about the relation between divine self-disclosure and human realization; Qūnawī and Farghānī develop this speculative vocabulary further. The Akbarian discussions of Muhammadan reality concern prophetic wisdom, metaphysical disclosure and spiritual realization, not merely commendable behaviour. Spiritual transmission had become a set of institutions and an intellectual tradition; their historical emergence and the truth of their metaphysical claims are distinguishable questions. Sufi teaching lineages developed historically through identifiable teachers, while Akbarian texts elaborated metaphysical accounts whose claims require separate philosophical argument.

A second distinction keeps the history accurate. Spiritual practices such as remembrance and self-accounting, the institutional form of a later *ṭarīqah*, and a metaphysical doctrine of *walāyah* are not interchangeable. Ibn ʿArabī discusses command, station and witnessing within a theory of realization; Qūnawī's *Miftāḥ al-ghayb* develops an ontological treatment of *wujūd*; Farghānī later systematizes the Muhammadan Reality in his commentary on Ibn al-Fāriḍ. The relevant sequence is a documented development of teaching and concepts, not evidence that every early community member used the later terms. # Book VI — An international civilization with many centres

## 34. Cairo, Damascus and Jerusalem: government, learning and the Crusades

**Matn.** While scholars and spiritual teachers increasingly travelled across regions, political authority was divided among rulers whose territories and religious allegiances differed. The Fāṭimids founded Cairo in 969 and established Ismāʿīlī institutions of rule and learning beside the older centres of Iraq and Syria. Seljuk military rulers later entered Baghdad and governed extensive territories while acknowledging Abbasid caliphal prestige. Rival courts recruited soldiers, built hospitals and colleges, endowed mosques and sought scholarly support. In 1099 a crusading army captured Jerusalem, establishing Latin Christian principalities among Muslim and Eastern Christian populations. Recovery demanded more than an isolated battlefield victory. Nūr al-Dīn worked to consolidate Syria, while Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn took power in Egypt, ended the Fāṭimid caliphate there, and assembled forces able to recover Jerusalem in 1187. Trade, truces, pilgrimage and argument continued across the frontiers throughout the wars. The cities of Cairo and Damascus became important homes for jurists, scholars, craftsmen and Sufis, whose teaching relationships were not exhausted by the campaigns. The long conflict joined military resources, religious legitimacy and urban institutions without making the history of all those institutions reducible to crusading warfare.

**Sharḥ.** Muslim and Christian rulers fought, negotiated truces, exchanged envoys and traded with one another; neither population acted as one undifferentiated political bloc. The Latin principalities interacted with diverse eastern Christian communities and with Muslim dynasties that themselves fought rivals. Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn's military success depended on prior Syrian consolidation, control of Egypt's resources and treaties with local powers. Ibn al-Athīr, Ibn Shaddād and Latin chroniclers preserve accounts of the campaigns from differing political and religious positions. No single court chronicle is a transparent record of all participants.

This case also clarifies what an 'international Islamic society' meant. A traveler passing from Cairo to Damascus encountered different rulers but could find Arabic books, legal teachers, markets, hospitals and spiritual guides across the frontier. Political fragmentation sometimes increased patronage competition. Fāṭimid Ismāʿīlī institutions and Sunni endowments promoted different understandings of religious authority while sharing some urban and administrative forms. The coexistence of disagreement and common social practices needs explanation, not rhetorical celebration. Crusading warfare shaped taxation, fortification, migration and religious preaching; it did not create the scholarly networks from nothing. For orientation consult the *New Cambridge History of Islam*, volumes 2 and 3, and specialist biographies of Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn.

Al-Azhar's institutional history began in Fatimid Cairo and changed under subsequent governments and scholarly communities. Founded in Fatimid Cairo, the mosque became associated with a particular ruling dynasty's religious and educational project; its subsequent life passed through different political and doctrinal settings. It would be inaccurate to describe every later Egyptian scholarly institution as simply a continuation of Fatimid propaganda, yet a building's history can register changes in authority that a list of dynasties conceals. Similarly, military and charitable endowments under Nūr al-Dīn and Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn tied urban property to public aims, while actual teachers and students used those places in diverse ways. The wars shaped what cities could finance and whom they could protect. Endowed institutions financed teaching under differing patrons, just as individual campaigns depended on particular alliances, revenues and military aims.

## 35. Al-Andalus and the Maghrib: cities, scholarship and the loss of a political world

**Matn.** An Umayyad prince who escaped the Abbasid overthrow established a new ruling house at Córdoba in 756. Over generations al-Andalus became a western centre of Muslim government, agriculture, crafts and learning, connected across the Mediterranean to North Africa. Its rulers faced Christian kingdoms to the north and rival Muslim claimants within Iberia; its cities brought Arabic-speaking Muslims, Christians and Jews into relations of exchange, rivalry and unequal legal standing. Córdoba's caliphate later broke into competing city-based kingdoms, while the Almoravids and then Almohads connected parts of Iberia to powerful Maghribi states. Jurists carried Mālikī teaching across the straits; grammarians, physicians, philosophers and poets travelled between courts and scholarly circles. As northern Christian kingdoms expanded, Muslim authority contracted, leaving the Nasrid kingdom of Granada until its conquest in 1492. Muslims who remained in Christian-ruled territory encountered changing guarantees, pressures to convert and, ultimately, expulsion in the early seventeenth century. Books and teachers nevertheless crossed from Iberia into North Africa, and the intellectual history of the western Islamic lands continued after the loss of political rule. Scholars and merchants sustained learning and exchange across the western Mediterranean while warfare, restrictions on religious life and displacement changed the communities that produced them.

**Sharḥ.** The institutional trajectory is more informative than a rise-and-fall label. Umayyad rule in Córdoba was initially an emirate and only later a caliphate; the caliphal order ended in 1031, after which different *ṭawāʾif* competed for revenues, military support and legitimacy. Almoravid and Almohad movements emerged within North African conditions and intervened in Iberian politics, bringing their own doctrines and ruling practices. The Nasrid regime at Granada persisted for more than two centuries while balancing warfare, diplomacy, taxation and economic relations with its neighbours. María Viguera-Molins describes the collapse of the Cordoban caliphate in 1031 and the subsequent competing kingdoms, while archaeological studies document the continuing urban and agricultural life of al-Andalus.

Learning followed related but distinct pathways. The *Muwaṭṭaʾ* circulated in versions associated with western transmitters; Mālikī jurists acquired durable authority in the Maghrib and al-Andalus. Scholars could write Arabic commentaries and teach across the straits even when the regions were politically divided. Ibn Rushd's philosophical and legal writings belong to this western history, while Ibn ʿArabī's early life in al-Andalus and later movement eastward illustrates a transregional scholarly-spiritual trajectory. Ibn Rushd and Ibn ʿArabī pursued distinct philosophical and spiritual projects within the changing political and scholarly conditions of al-Andalus.

Christian, Jewish and Muslim relations varied across cities, institutions and centuries. The concept *convivencia* is a subject of historiographical debate, not a license to erase coercion or shared daily life. The forced conversions imposed after Granada and the expulsion of Moriscos during 1609–14 are historically distinct from the conquest of 1492 itself. Communities and books displaced toward the Maghrib could survive political collapse, yet the material cost to families was severe. Archaeology, local documents, Muslim and Christian chronicles and scholarly biographies preserve different evidence for political change, religious practice and learning in the western Mediterranean.

## 36. Persianate language, commerce and the circulation of books

**Matn.** Muslim societies had long shared Arabic as the language of revelation and many learned disciplines. Across Iran and Central Asia, Persian also became a vehicle for state correspondence, courtly writing, ethical instruction and poetry. Its influence travelled with administrators, poets, merchants and Sufi teachers into Anatolia and India. A student might read Arabic grammar and jurisprudence, learn Persian literary models and hear local languages at home. Courts cultivated prestige through histories and poetry, while traders carried goods and correspondence along roads joining Khurāsān, Baghdad, the Gulf and the Indian subcontinent. The use of paper, books and professional scribes allowed a work to outlive the place where it was composed. Yet transmission required readers who could interpret a technical term, patrons able to finance copying and teachers who corrected mistakes. The world of shared books and spiritual idioms therefore extended beyond any single sovereign's territory. Persianate culture did not replace Arabic learning; the two frequently occupied different but related roles in the education, administration and devotion of the same persons.

**Sharḥ.** Hodgson distinguishes Islam as a religion from the broader Islamicate culture of poetry, commerce and royal architecture; some authors nonetheless understood those cultural practices as means of religious realization. His categories themselves require qualification, especially where authors understood philosophy or poetry as an integral way of realizing religious truth. Shahab Ahmed argues that many Muslim authors understood philosophy, poetry and other Islamicate practices as integral to their religious lives.

Manuscript colophons, ownership records, citations and teaching certificates document particular routes by which texts passed among scholars. Niẓāmī's Persian narratives, Saʿdī's ethical works, or the legal and Sufi texts carried to South Asia are possible case studies, but their particular routes cannot be assumed from later popularity. Manuscript dates, ownership notes, colophons, reading certificates and library inventories provide stronger evidence. The intellectual consequence of bilingual learning also deserves attention: a technical Arabic discipline could be explained in Persian and eventually local vernaculars, changing who could enter a tradition without necessarily changing the tradition's formal terms. Such processes explain the civilization's adaptability more concretely than the phrase 'cultural synthesis.'

One route of inquiry is through the reconstruction of a book's movement. A Persian text copied at one court might reach India with a secretary, be read in a learned household and be cited in a later ethical manual. A documented textual route requires dated manuscripts, ownership notes, biographical notices or explicit citations; similar vocabulary alone does not establish transmission. Literary Persian also provided an intermediary language for local translations and commentaries. Students who learned Arabic legal terminology through Persian explanations could participate in a wider scholarly community while continuing to speak vernacular languages at home. Persian also mediated technical learning in administration, law and spiritual instruction among multilingual communities. It is about the practical mediation by which technical learning became teachable across regions without requiring every learner to begin with the same mother tongue.

## 37. Mongol conquest, conversion and the survival of scholarly worlds

**Matn.** In the thirteenth century Mongol armies destroyed cities, displaced populations and dismantled established regimes across Central and Western Asia. Baghdad was taken in 1258, ending the ruling Abbasid court there. Yet other Muslim centres survived, and new rulers needed scribes, merchants, physicians and administrators to govern the territories they had conquered. Mamluk armies stopped a Mongol force at ʿAyn Jālūt in 1260, while Cairo and Damascus continued to support judges, schools and the movement of books. In Iran, Mongol rulers first patronized several religious communities; Ghāzān's conversion to Islam in 1295 marked a major development in Ilkhanid political life. Rebuilding, new endowments and courtly histories followed among continuing wars and fiscal pressures. Older scholarly practices could thus be adopted by conquerors who had initially stood outside them. Teachers and merchants also moved along Mongol-controlled routes, even as warfare made many local livelihoods precarious. The loss of a capital did not extinguish Islamic learning, but the recovery of learning depended on surviving people, books, towns and resources, rather than on an abstract continuity immune to violence.

**Sharḥ.** The difference between a dynasty's conversion and a population's religious transformation is essential. Peter Jackson's *The Mongols and the Islamic World* and the *Encyclopaedia Iranica* account of the Mongols trace the varied trajectories of the Ilkhanids, Jochids and Central Asian khanates. Aḥmad Tegüder had already ruled as a Muslim Ilkhan; Ghāzān's accession later established a more durable public Islamic orientation at the court. Rashīd al-Dīn wrote under Ilkhanid patronage, while Mamluk and other chroniclers recorded the same conflicts through different political allegiances. Motives given to a conqueror by an official chronicler are evidence for political representation as much as for private conviction.

The mechanism of institutional persistence is visible in Cairo and Iran. A *waqf* could support a school across changes of dynasty, but endowments might be ruined or reallocated; a jurist's students could copy his work even when his patron was defeated. Mongol administrators also adopted Persian fiscal and literary practices for practical reasons. An Ibn Khaldūnian model can illuminate the transition from conqueror solidarity to the difficulties of administering settled societies, while local documents decide how far the analogy holds. After the Mongol wars, scholars continued to produce works in theology, logic, astronomy and Sufi thought, although warfare altered their centres, resources and patrons.

A later chronicler who says that Ghāzān's accession brought Islam to the Ilkhanate is reporting a major change in public royal identity, not the conversion of everyone under his rule. Aḥmad Tegüder and Muslim Mongol soldiers preceded Ghāzān's accession and therefore demonstrate earlier Islamic affiliations within Ilkhanid society. Later Mongol rulers' patronage of scholars, foundations and monuments also served political purposes, but religious conviction cannot be disproved merely by identifying those interests. The contrasting Mamluk and Ilkhanid reports should therefore be compared as partisan accounts of a contested transformation. Courts, colleges, manuscripts and commercial routes carried learned and devotional practices through changing dynastic conditions. Mongol campaigns devastated cities and scholarly households, while surviving people and institutions later rebuilt teaching and textual circulation under new rulers.

## 38. The Middle Niger: markets, kings and independent scholars

**Matn.** South of the Sahara, Muslim merchants had long crossed deserts to exchange salt, metals, textiles and other goods. Their routes linked North African towns with settlements and courts along the Niger. Rulers sometimes welcomed Muslim traders and employed literate specialists before most of their subjects adopted Islamic practices. Mali became a powerful kingdom, and Mansa Mūsā's pilgrimage in 1324 brought its wealth and connections to wider notice. Under later Songhay rulers, towns such as Timbuktu and Jenne supported jurists, merchants, teachers and book copying. Scholars acquired standing through knowledge, family networks and their ability to mediate disputes; some sought autonomy from rulers while relying on the same commercial communities for resources. Rural devotional life, local languages and courts did not always develop at the same pace. West African Muslim scholarship therefore emerged through local institutions and debates, not merely through the arrival of books from the north. Its authors participated in wider Mālikī and Sufi conversations while writing about the needs of their own societies.

**Sharḥ.** The *Tārīkh al-Sūdān*, learned biographies, manuscripts, archaeological evidence and reports by travellers such as Ibn Baṭṭūṭah preserve different aspects of the Middle Niger's history. Zachary Wright traces West African scholarly authority through teaching communities and inherited learned relationships, which did not depend wholly on appointment by royal courts. Northwestern University's West African Arabic manuscript collections and catalogues also make local textual production accessible.

Timbuktu supported learned families, libraries and teaching circles whose organization differed from modern universities; surviving manuscript inventories vary greatly in age and provenance. The age, provenance and content of particular libraries need separate examination. A study of 31 private libraries found variation in the age and provenance of collections, limiting claims of uninterrupted scholarly transmission across every household. The surviving libraries preserve evidence of local scholarship but differ in the periods and networks they document. Trade supported towns and literacy; rulers patronized institutions; scholars organized teaching and defended authority within those changing conditions. West African scholars used Arabic and Mālikī learning within local commercial, political and educational arrangements, adapting inherited disciplines to their own institutions.

Aḥmad Bābā (d. 1627) wrote on Mālikī learning and scholarly biographies, and his career was disrupted by Moroccan intervention in Timbuktu. His works on Mālikī scholarship and learned biographies belong to a world that maintained ties with North Africa; his career was also affected by Moroccan intervention in Songhay and the removal of scholars from Timbuktu. Aḥmad Bābā's scholarly career and forced removal after Moroccan intervention connected the independence of learned households with struggles over political power. The surviving works document learned circles and libraries in particular towns; they do not establish Cairo-style colleges everywhere or a uniform date for the manuscript collections. Their ownership and contents vary. Dated manuscripts and biographical records document particular West African scholars and libraries without establishing identical educational institutions in every town.

## 39. Across the monsoon seas: ports, households and the Malay world

**Matn.** Muslim merchants travelled the Indian Ocean with sailors and traders of many religions, following seasonal winds between the Red Sea, Persian Gulf, East Africa, western India and Southeast Asia. A merchant entering a port needed lodging, partners, trusted weights and a means of settling disputes; resident communities built mosques, married locally and taught their children alongside commercial work. In the fifteenth century Melaka became a major harbour where rulers protected visiting merchants, organized customs and governed a population whose residents changed with the monsoon. The ruling court's Islamic identity connected it to other Muslim sovereigns, but the port's prosperity also depended on Chinese, South Asian and island Southeast Asian exchanges. Malay-language instruction and texts in Arabic-derived scripts developed within these changing communities. Islam moved through merchants, families, teachers and courts, with different sequences in different places. The spread of shared religious practice therefore depended on practical relations that made sustained teaching and settlement possible, while local languages and institutions gave it forms unfamiliar to early Arabia.

**Sharḥ.** Kenneth Hall's study of the eastern Indian Ocean documents Melaka's warehouses, resident merchants, harbour offices and rules for customs and commercial protection. A. C. S. Peacock's 2024 study adds Arabic, Persian and Turkish accounts, inscriptions and navigational sources that connect the port to wider Muslim geographical knowledge. These witnesses do not settle the exact conversion dates of early rulers; the evidence for that sequence is famously inconsistent, as Christopher Wake noted. Muslim, Hindu, Buddhist and other merchant communities participated in Indian Ocean commerce; Muslim merchants did not monopolize its ports.

Indian Ocean port communities linked mercantile trust, resident families, worship and instruction through sustained settlement. Trust among commercial partners is not identical to faith, but people who lived together could build prayer spaces, commission teachers and sustain scholarly ties. A port administration that protected foreigners did not by that fact produce Muslim conviction; it furnished conditions in which a community could reproduce itself. Malay literary and legal manuscripts record Islamic teaching and court practice in forms that differed among ports and ruling houses. The transition from an Arabic book to Jawi instruction was likewise mediated by language, translators, scribes and patrons. Local rulers, merchants, scribes and teachers shaped how Islamic practice entered Malay institutions and vernacular texts.

Melaka's *syahbandar* officers, customs regulations and arrangements for visiting merchants make an excellent causal case. They helped reduce uncertainty about lodging, weighing, protection and market transactions for temporary residents. Some of these residents formed more durable households and communities in which religious learning could continue between trading seasons. Harbour privileges supported trade and settlement, but dated inscriptions, legal texts and local chronicles give differing evidence for the conversion of particular rulers and communities. Peacock's Arabic and Persian material locates Melaka within broader Muslim geographical knowledge, while Malay and Portuguese accounts supply different perspectives. Holding these kinds of evidence apart prevents commerce from being treated either as proof of spiritual transmission or as an explanation that makes spiritual teaching unnecessary.

## 40. Bengal: fields, settlements and changing religious communities

**Matn.** In eastern Bengal the expansion of Muslim communities did not follow the map of early military conquest. Much of the lower delta was wooded or marshy, its waterways shifting and its agricultural settlements growing at different times. As political authorities claimed territory, cultivators cleared land and organized villages; religious teachers and patrons sometimes participated in these efforts by establishing shrines, places of prayer and local charitable ties. A family newly settled in a district might become attached to a saintly household through landholding, work and inherited practice rather than through a single dramatic conversion. Muslim courts and Persianate administration operated beside Bengali language and local custom. Over generations, devotional practices, agricultural communities and political boundaries changed at different rates. These intertwined processes help explain why large Muslim populations came to inhabit eastern Bengal, including districts far from the first centres of Turkish military power. The religion acquired local continuities through settled households and remembered teachers while remaining connected to wider Muslim texts, pilgrimage and law.

**Sharḥ.** Richard Eaton's *The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier, 1204–1760* is the key test case. He uses inscriptions, monuments, administrative texts and local literary records to relate political, agrarian and religious frontiers without collapsing one into the others. A later essay clarifies four overlapping processes, adding the eastward movement of Sanskritic institutions to political, agrarian and religious change. These distinctions are historical hypotheses supported in particular places and periods, not a universal theory of conversion.

A rigorous microhistory would trace one named village or shrine, the associated land grant if preserved, the date and location of an inscription and subsequent changes in surrounding settlement. It would ask whether a saint's remembered career is independently dated, whether followers occupied adjacent fields, and whether a later text projects a more coherent religious community onto an earlier landscape. The spiritual question is inseparable from this context but not reducible to it: why did people regard particular teachers and places as bearers of blessing and trustworthy guidance? The archive may establish their social position without proving or disproving their inner spiritual realization. In parts of Bengal, agriculture, land grants, shrines and religious instruction developed through overlapping local settlements rather than one uniform sequence.

A land grant associated with a religious figure in Bengal can carry several meanings at once: authorization to cultivate, a claim to revenue, support for a place of worship and later memory of a revered founder. Eaton's historical method asks which meanings can actually be dated from the document or its material setting. If cultivation moves into a forest frontier, the establishment of a village may alter the resources and social ties through which a religious community reproduces itself. Yet neither the clearing of land nor the grant proves a person's inward conversion. Land grants and mosque foundations document settlement and patronage but seldom disclose the private convictions of every household involved. A genuine integrated history resists the equal and opposite temptations of reducing Islam to economics or treating agricultural conditions as irrelevant to its local life.

## 41. The Ottoman settlement: courts, colleges and a growing imperial household

**Matn.** Ottoman rulers grew from an Anatolian principality into an empire whose capital, after the conquest of Constantinople in 1453, linked the Balkans, Anatolia and the eastern Mediterranean. Military forces, taxation and palace households sustained rule over Muslim, Christian and Jewish populations. Mosques, courts, colleges and charitable foundations gave towns recognizable Islamic institutions, while local communities retained different forms of worship and internal authority. Judges applied learned Ḥanafī legal traditions alongside sultanic regulations addressing revenue, order and administration. Scholars moved through educational and judicial appointments, acquiring prospects for service that tied some of them closely to the state. The conquest of Arab provinces in the early sixteenth century added older centres of Muslim scholarship and the great pilgrimage routes to Ottoman rule. Yet the empire could not teach religious life by decree: provincial mosques, households, Sufi teachers and book circles sustained practices that sometimes supported and sometimes criticized official policy. Religious institutions both helped organize the empire and provided resources that outlived individual sultans.

**Sharḥ.** The Ottoman *ʿilmiyye* offered a relatively structured hierarchy of appointments but cannot be equated with a single centralized clergy. Gilles Veinstein's chapter in *The Cambridge History of Turkey* documents how judges, mosques, Friday sermons, colleges and pious foundations became part of Ottoman governance from its early period, while scholars were originally recruited from other regions as well. Later institutions changed over time; do not project the sixteenth-century hierarchy back onto the fourteenth century.

A judge in an Ottoman town faced questions raised by actual families and markets, while sultanic *qānūn* regulated matters such as tax and administration. The relationship between juristic tradition and imperial rule varied by subject and province; a law code is not evidence that everyone obeyed it. Endowments supported services but also elite households. Sufi lodges and the pilgrimage connected people across frontiers, including Arab provinces with established scholarly traditions. Rulers patronized colleges and courts, but jurists, household teachers and spiritual lineages retained forms of learning that governments did not wholly control. Ordinary subjects encountered the empire through courts, markets, taxes, village administration and worship, not solely through the decrees of Constantinople.

An Ottoman court register provides a different view of the state than an imperial legal code. In a register, litigants present claims about inheritance, debts or property, and the qāḍī records settlements within a social world in which witnesses, custom and political order matter. The imperial law code reveals how the ruler intended to regulate some activities. These documents may agree or differ without either being irrelevant. A madrasa certificate, meanwhile, records a different kind of authority, that of learned transmission. The empire's durability required all three—imperial command, local adjudication and teaching—but their relations varied. Ottoman court records, the *ʿilmiyye* hierarchy, endowments and regional Sufi institutions document distinct and sometimes competing forms of authority.

## 42. Safavid Iran and Mughal India: rival confessional and imperial settlements

**Matn.** Across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, two other great states established different relationships between religious authority and government. In Iran, the Safavids made Twelver Shiʿism central to imperial identity and supported scholars able to teach its law and traditions; this process altered the religious institutions of towns and provinces over generations. In India, Mughal rulers governed populations with multiple languages, religious affiliations and local landholding arrangements. Persian served important administrative and literary functions, while legal scholars, Sufis, temples, shrines and regional elites occupied different positions within the court's world. Mughal policies varied from Akbar's experiments in courtly religious discussion and imperial inclusion to Aurangzeb's patronage of Ḥanafī legal compilation. In both empires, rulers required reliable revenue and armies, while religious teachers depended upon patrons and communities whose loyalties did not always match the state's priorities. The result was neither one universal early-modern Islamic order nor a simple division between spirituality and politics: different institutions formed through different bargains among scholarship, authority and the needs of diverse populations.

**Sharḥ.** The Safavid adoption of Twelver Shiʿism was a state project with long and uneven social consequences, not an instant change in the convictions of all Iran's inhabitants in 1501. The arrival and support of scholars from regions with established Imāmī learning are part of that history. The relation of rulers to jurists also changed between Ismāʿīl I and later Safavid reigns. Mughal policies toward religious communities, revenue and scholarship differed among rulers and provinces, and changed across time. Akbar's courtly debates, revenue administration and imperial household were not interchangeable institutions; the *Fatāwā-yi ʿĀlamgīrī* is a major compilation whose practical reception demands separate research.

Safavid Isfahan supported philosophers and theologians including Mullā Ṣadrā, while Indian scholarly networks developed curricula in law, ḥadīth, logic and spiritual discipline. Philosophers, jurists and spiritual authors within each empire disputed one another's positions rather than expressing a uniform imperial doctrine. Shāh Walī Allāh's eighteenth-century work later connected South Asian hadith, law and spiritual concerns in a setting shaped by Mughal fragmentation. Comparative studies of the three empires should account for revenue systems, military organization, confessional policy and local communities before making large claims about decline or renewal. Safavid patronage of Twelver Shiʿi scholarship gave Iran institutions different from those sustained by Ottoman Sunni and Mughal scholarly networks.

# Book VII — Modern change in the conditions of religious life

## 43. Oceans, finance and the changing balance of power

**Matn.** While the great Muslim empires governed extensive territories, European states and commercial companies expanded their maritime activities. Portuguese fleets entered the Indian Ocean at the end of the fifteenth century; Dutch and English companies later established trading posts, fortified ports and systems of credit and coercion. They encountered a crowded commercial world already served by Arab, Indian, Persian, African, Armenian, Jewish and other merchants. Some Asian rulers cooperated with Europeans, others resisted them, and regional trade did not immediately become European property. Over subsequent centuries, however, Atlantic commerce, new financial institutions, industrial production and military changes altered the resources available to competing states. Ottoman, Iranian and Indian governments faced fiscal and administrative pressures that had different local histories. The problem was no longer simply that one dynasty had weakened while another rose; technologies of production, credit, warfare and communication were changing the relations among distant societies. Religious institutions in Muslim lands would meet these transformations through reforms of government, learning and the organization of law.

**Sharḥ.** Hodgson's 'Great Western Transmutation' describes changes in technology, communication, production and social organization that accompanied and exceeded Europe's overseas conquests. Atlantic credit, maritime trade and continuing Asian commercial networks developed together, challenging claims of a sudden and universal European monopoly. The familiar claim that Europeans simply monopolized the Indian Ocean from 1500 is false; studies of Gujarat and the Gulf document cosmopolitan communities and continuing Asian participation into the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Nor should a single Ottoman defeat be made the beginning of a uniform Muslim 'decline.'

Material change mattered to intellectual and religious life through identifiable intermediaries. A state unable to finance armies might reform taxes, borrow money or reorganize offices. A trading company that acquired territorial power could alter courts and property claims. Printed books and eventually telegraphy changed the speed and scale at which arguments moved. These were uneven transformations: an endowment-supported teacher in Cairo, a village jurist in India and a merchant family on the Swahili coast experienced them differently. Guénon criticized modern conceptions of knowledge and quantity, a metaphysical interpretation distinct from documentary evidence about finance, commerce and administration.

Surat's maritime trade demonstrates why European arrival did not produce instant European control. Merchants of different religions and regions financed vessels, exchanged credit and relied on commercial partnerships; European companies entered these existing networks while later acquiring territorial power in different ports. Political control over a harbour, a monopoly over a commodity and control of an inland agrarian region are three different achievements. In Europe itself, changing relations among taxation, debt and military expenditure affected how states competed. Hodgson's 'transmutation' and traditionalist critiques of modern civilization address different questions: the former seeks historical mechanisms of change; the latter evaluates the intellectual and spiritual meaning of modern modes of life. Their conclusions can be compared only after the subject of each has been specified.

## 44. Colonial courts and the transformation of Islamic law

**Matn.** In parts of South Asia, the British East India Company acquired the power to collect revenue and administer justice in territories where Mughal legal and fiscal traditions had previously operated. Colonial officials consulted Muslim jurists and translated authoritative books, but gradually established courts that classified cases through new procedures and state-defined categories. The scope of Islamic law was reduced and reorganized, especially as penal and commercial law came under new codes and Muslim family matters were assigned to a distinct personal-law field. Elsewhere, French rule in Algeria and British occupation of Egypt rearranged property, education and judicial authority through different institutions. A local scholar could still teach jurisprudence, but a colonial court might decide what counted as applicable 'Muslim law' through manuals, precedents and officials trained in another legal system. The changes affected the social meaning of legal expertise as well as the content of particular rulings. They also encouraged efforts to preserve teaching networks and to reform curricula, since inherited ways of educating jurists no longer guaranteed influence over public institutions.

**Sharḥ.** Colonial authorities reorganized courts and legal procedures differently in India, North Africa and other Muslim regions. British Indian 'Anglo-Muhammadan law' applied selected doctrines under colonial procedures and later concentrated much Muslim law in personal status; the University of Exeter's Privy Council Papers historical guide gives an accessible account of the changing scope. Wael Hallaq argues that colonial state structures transformed juristic authority; local court records and studies of codification document how these changes operated in particular jurisdictions. Sohaira Siddiqui examines changes in legal theory and authority under colonial rule alongside the translation and codification of particular legal manuals.

Colonial courts transferred parts of legal classification and procedure from older juristic networks to officials working within new state institutions. A juristic ruling had once emerged in a field populated by teachers, muftis, judges, litigants, rulers and local practice; colonial government could retain the vocabulary of Muslim law while relocating final authority over procedure and legal classification. That shift did not erase all juristic agency, and different courts followed different trajectories. It did, however, produce new educational problems. Madrasas might strengthen instruction in the inherited texts, while new colleges trained students for changed administrative professions. The divergence between moral formation, scholarly qualification and public jurisdiction became acute. Al-Attas later argued that education should order knowledge according to its proper purposes, while colonial officials and modern colleges changed the institutional settings in which law and learning acquired authority.

The Bengal judicial reforms associated with Warren Hastings in 1772 provide a dated institutional point of entry. Officials attempted to retain some Hindu and Muslim legal doctrines while placing their application inside a new hierarchy of Company courts. Over the nineteenth century, codes, appeals and professional practices altered the role of classical juristic reasoning in public judgments. The effect cannot be summarized as the abolition of the Sharīʿah on one day. In other places, such as Egypt and Malaya, colonial and local arrangements developed differently. The source method here is to choose one class of cases—for example inheritance—and compare its doctrinal treatment in a juristic text, the translated rules used by officials and a particular court judgment. Only then can one assess which parts of interpretation and jurisdiction changed.

## 45. Renewal through teaching, reform and print

**Matn.** Scholars and teachers responded to political and educational change by returning to inherited texts, developing new institutions and seeking wider audiences. The printing press allowed Qurʾānic commentaries, legal manuals, devotional books and polemics to reach readers who might never have entered a major teaching circle. In India, Deoband developed a madrasa founded in 1866 to preserve and renew learned disciplines under colonial conditions; Aligarh pursued a different educational path through English-language and modern instruction. In Cairo and other centres, reformers debated legal reasoning, religious schooling and the relationship between inherited disciplines and new sciences. Sufi communities, independent teachers and local scholars also continued older forms of transmission while adapting to railways, steamships and expanded pilgrimage. Such changes brought more readers into religious argument but sometimes weakened the authority of personal explanation: a printed excerpt could circulate without the teacher who had supplied its context. Schools and teachers now had to explain inherited doctrines to audiences formed by print, modern examinations and new divisions of professional and religious study.

**Sharḥ.** Reformers disagreed over inherited curricula, state schooling, legal authority and new sciences; their programmes crossed any simple traditional–modern division. Deoband, Aligarh, reformist writers in Cairo, Ottoman educational institutions and West African learned networks pursued different objectives. Some expanded hadith study; some sought a reorganization of legal authority; some defended established Sufi instruction; some integrated administrative or scientific subjects. Printed editions also altered textual practice: a student's stable printed page differed from a manuscript exemplar annotated through personal reading, but neither medium guaranteed correct understanding.

Al-Attas connects education to *adab* and the ordering of knowledge; Guénon criticizes the modern priority given to quantity. Their arguments interpret modernity at a different level from the institutional histories of particular schools. Al-Attas and traditionalist authors later offered distinct critiques of knowledge and modernity; neither expressed the intentions of every nineteenth-century Muslim teacher. The historical evidence involves school registers, syllabi, printed editions, biographies, correspondence and institutional records. The better question is which students each new arrangement served, what it enabled them to do, and what forms of knowledge became easier or harder to transmit. The answer will vary across classes, languages, genders and regions. Print, mass schooling and institutional examinations changed how teachers explained texts, corrected mistakes and assessed students' understanding.

Deoband and Aligarh represented distinct institutional responses among many forms of South Asian Muslim education. Deoband emerged through a specific scholarly and financial arrangement, while the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College developed its own residential and institutional aspirations. Women, merchant communities, village teachers, Sufi networks and other regional schools also shaped religious learning. Print made a legal opinion or devotional text more widely available, yet public circulation could intensify disputes over wording, authority and context. Examinations, lessons and student writings reveal differences between memorizing formulas, explaining a text and applying its reasoning to unfamiliar cases. This links the formation of *malakāt* to actual instructional practice without pretending that one curriculum was uniformly successful.

## 46. Nation-states, migration and the new scale of Muslim life

**Matn.** The twentieth century saw the Ottoman sultanate and caliphate abolished, colonial governments challenged, and many Muslim-majority states established or transformed through independence, partition and revolution. Borders divided communities that had long travelled through imperial, commercial and scholarly routes; migration then formed new congregations in Europe, the Americas and elsewhere. Mass schooling brought religious instruction to large populations while altering the places where qualified teachers were trained. Radio, television and digital media gave preachers and readers an audience far beyond their own towns. Pilgrimage by air connected Muslims who spoke different languages and lived under different legal systems, but shared recognisable rites and texts. Governments variously administered mosques, family law and universities, while private associations, scholars, families and spiritual lineages sustained additional kinds of authority. A person now might learn prayer from family, jurisprudence from a university course, religious guidance from an online lecture and spiritual discipline from a local teacher. The recurring question of the Sīrah—how knowledge becomes trustworthy action and enduring community—had acquired new conditions, not a single new answer.

**Sharḥ.** Muslim-majority states, minority communities and religious institutions experienced distinct political and educational developments; no single pattern describes them all. Independence in India and Pakistan in 1947, Indonesian independence during 1945–49, the dissolution of Ottoman political institutions and distinct African decolonizations took place under different conditions. State ministries, traditional schools, Sufi networks, universities and migrant associations cannot be assigned one universal effect. The transformation of broadcast and digital communication also has contrary possibilities: access to large textual corpora expands, while the authority of a circulating quotation can become detached from the circumstances of its transmission.

In Madinah, believers developed arrangements for shared obligations and adjudication; later institutions addressed related problems through different legal and communicative structures. In seventh-century Madinah, a new community needed ways to place trust in reports, resolve disputes and act together; later hadith, legal and spiritual traditions elaborated forms of authority that could travel beyond their first setting. Today the material conditions of access and repetition have changed again. A transmitted text, its juristic interpretation, its practice in daily life and later spiritual explanations remain distinct even when one person engages all four. Digital circulation expands access to religious material without guaranteeing correct interpretation or lasting habits. It yields concrete research questions: what does a student know after repeated short videos, what can they independently verify, who corrects their misunderstanding, and how does learning change conduct over time? A student's ability to identify sources, explain their meaning and apply what they learn supplies concrete evidence of understanding.

Online archives lower the cost of finding religious texts, but understanding them still requires linguistic, historical and interpretive skills. A scanned manuscript, a search result from a hadith database, a short devotional clip and a university lecture each give different access to evidence and guidance. Digital texts and short lectures often circulate without their original context; checking the source, language and commentary remains necessary for accurate interpretation. Teachers still identify errors that access to a document alone cannot correct. Mass media and searchable digital texts expanded access to religious information, while interpretation and durable habits still required sustained practice and correction. Digital archives broaden access to sources while placing greater demands on independent verification and sustained study.

# Critical apparatus, worked cases and independent audit

## Editorial purpose and limits

This is a **narrative-critical study edition** of the Sīrah and later Islamic history. It is not a diplomatic edition of an early Sīrah manuscript, and it does not claim to establish a new critical Arabic text of Ibn Isḥāq, al-Ṭabarī or another primary author. The matn presents a continuous narrative without numbered textual citations. The sharḥ tests its consequential claims through primary witnesses, historical context, later argument and appropriately identified contemporary scholarship. Its principal Sunni narrative frame makes room for the distinct Shiʿi theories of religious authority and historical memory where they materially shape the sequence; it does not make their controversy the organizing subject.

Four different questions must not be confused: what a revealed text says; what a transmitted historical account reports; what can be corroborated or reconstructed from multiple witnesses; and what a philosopher or spiritual teacher argues that the event means. They often illuminate one another but possess different standards of justification. The matn's chronological use of earlier prophets follows sacred history; biblical numerical chronologies and modern reconstructions, where relevant, belong in the sharḥ. Guénon's interpretation of the sixth-century BCE religious transformations is compared as a philosophy of sacred history, not presented as a verified causal event.

The 46 entries are not supposed to contain equal amounts of material. The Prophetic chapters take the reader through decisions and changes of circumstance. The later chapters are organized around regional or institutional processes with sufficient narrative room to avoid a list of dynasties. A chapter is successful when a reader can answer *what created this difficulty, what people did about it, why that response had consequences, and how we know*. If it cannot answer those questions, another paragraph of names will not remedy the defect.

## Dossier 1 — Meccan protection, public recitation, and the Hijrah: one report read closely (chapters 8–11)

The early Muslim community cannot be understood by simply saying that its members were persecuted and then migrated. They belonged to households whose members enjoyed unequal degrees of protection. Some could withdraw into a powerful clan, others were dependent clients, and some had no effective protector. Religious instruction therefore travelled through existing households even as it called into question the inherited basis of standing within them. The clearest way of seeing this interaction is through the long report transmitted from ʿĀʾishah by ʿUrwah b. al-Zubayr, from Ibn Shihāb al-Zuhrī, in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī*, no. 3905, chapter on migration to Madinah. The chain matters: it is a report from an early family and scholarly network, not a series of contemporaneously dated depositions for every episode it narrates.

The report describes Abū Bakr setting out toward Aksum when opposition made life in Makkah difficult. At Bark al-Ghimād he met Ibn al-Daghinah, a tribal leader who offered him *jiwār*—public protection. Ibn al-Daghinah justified the offer by referring to Abū Bakr's care for relatives, dependants, guests and people in distress. Protection was thus negotiated through a reputation for social obligation recognizable even to people who did not share his belief. The Meccan notables accepted the arrangement on the condition that Abū Bakr worship quietly within his house. When he began reciting in a prayer-space in his courtyard, women and children gathered to listen; his public recitation unsettled the notables, who pressed the guarantor to restrict him. Abū Bakr refused to confine worship in the demanded manner and returned the guarantee. The passage joins religious emotion, domestic space, public attention, kinship prestige and the enforceable conditions of protection in one narrated sequence. It gives a much better explanation of the social stakes of preaching than a generic statement that the old elite feared Islam.

The crucial difference between private and public recitation was not a change of doctrine. A man reading aloud in view of other households created an audience not fully controlled by patriarchs or clan elders. The report presents women and children attending to the recitation; it does **not** give demographic figures for conversion or license a claim that all women or children adopted the message. It shows how a practice that was legally tolerable within the home became politically contentious at its threshold. In this setting, sincerity, steadfastness and courage were not abstract virtues added by later writers: they were manifested in a decision whether to surrender protection in order to continue a practice. Yet the subsequent precise interior state of Abū Bakr is known only insofar as the report describes it; later accounts of spiritual stations should not be made to stand in for historical testimony.

The same report proceeds to the prospect of a new destination, describes the Prophet's permission to migrate, Abū Bakr's preparation of mounts, the flight and cave, and arrival in the oasis. Its story of preparation is not evidence that every migrant undertook identical measures. Its value for the narrative lies in the sequence: protection at Makkah had limits; Aksum offered refuge under a Christian ruler but not a Muslim public order; Yathrib, through instruction and pledges, promised a place in which believers could defend and administer collective obligations. The Hijrah was therefore at once flight from danger, obedience to guidance, and the beginning of new burdens upon hosts and newcomers.

**Textual control.** The report is [al-Bukhārī 3905](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3905); the Arabic chain and extended narrative can also be checked in [the numbered Arabic transcript](https://equranlibrary.com/hadith/bukhari/1317/3905). The *matn* of chapters 10–11 should tell the episode. The *sharḥ* must distinguish the narrated facts, the inferred social mechanism (control over audiences and protection), and the spiritual reading (the testing of constancy). Open tasks: compare parallel reports of *jiwār* and the ʿAqabah pledges in Ibn Isḥāq/Ibn Hishām and al-Ṭabarī before treating speeches and precise sequences as independently confirmed.

## Dossier 2 — The Medinan *kitāb*: obligations before constitutional theory (chapter 12)

When the emigrants arrived in Yathrib, they entered an already inhabited and politically divided oasis. Households owned date gardens and had to manage labour, water, blood-compensation, captivity and military alliances. The survival of the new fellowship demanded a working answer to practical questions. Who pays compensation when a member of one group causes a death? Who helps ransom a captive? Who contributes to defence if a neighbouring coalition attacks? The transmitted *kitāb* attributed to the Prophet addresses precisely such problems. Its clauses are preserved in Ibn Isḥāq's material through Ibn Hishām and in another transmitted recension associated with Abū ʿUbayd's *Kitāb al-Amwāl*. The existence of recensions calls for comparison; it does not justify either dismissing the whole document or treating one modern clause-numbering system as an original feature of the text.

The opening describes participants as **«أُمَّةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ مِنْ دُونِ النَّاسِ»**—a distinct community or association. But subsequent clauses do not erase inherited group membership. They refer to the emigrants and named Yathrib clans retaining their arrangements for collective compensation and ransoming captives. The recurrent expression **«يَتَعَاقَلُونَ مَعَاقِلَهُمُ الأُولَى»** makes precisely that continuity visible. An injured family would not suddenly receive compensation from an anonymous modern treasury; actual groups still bore responsibilities while entering a wider system of obligation. For the newly arrived emigrants, arrangements of mutual support had to operate alongside these obligations. Religious allegiance did not automatically furnish food, land or enforceable claims. It created grounds on which people could undertake and judge those claims together.

The document also contains clauses about Jewish participants, their religion, expenditures and mutual aid. In one recension the brief wording **«لِلْيَهُودِ دِينُهُمْ وَلِلْمُسْلِمِينَ دِينُهُمْ»** appears. This is important evidence of specified religious differentiation, but it must not be taken to imply that every Jewish group in the oasis had signed the same instrument, or that its provisions amounted to a modern charter of equal individual citizenship. Michael Lecker's detailed study argues that participation was more restricted than popular presentations suppose and that some major groups had separate arrangements. The 2024 publication of his revised study contains separate chapters on Muslim and Jewish participants, the treaties, dating, unity, recensions and preservation. These are disagreements about **which persons and obligations** were included, not merely arguments over terminology.

This illuminates a transition that religious or political slogans conceal. The religious *umma* emerging around the Prophet included believers bound by worship and allegiance. But its public security could require arrangements with groups of different religion. Religious fellowship and political protection were therefore partly overlapping, partly distinct associations. The Prophet's adjudicating role operated at that intersection. The *kitāb* presents conditions for making cooperation predictable; it does not show that every clan ceased to function or that disputes ended. Nor does it establish by itself when each later Medinan command concerning marriage, property, fasting or inheritance was revealed. Those commands should be established separately in the relevant textual histories.

## Dossier 3 — Al-Ḥudaybiyyah: a difficult treaty as a test of communal judgment (chapter 16)

The Prophet's party approached Makkah with the purpose of pilgrimage rather than a declared attempt to take the city. Quraysh's obstruction forced a choice: withdraw without entering, fight, or seek an enforceable arrangement. The narrative in al-Bukhārī 2731–2732 gives political deliberation and spiritual trial as one event, not two unrelated lessons. The party manoeuvred to avoid cavalry, halted near al-Ḥudaybiyyah, and exchanged envoys. Negotiations culminated in Suhayl b. ʿAmr's arrival and a text whose disputed wording dramatized the difficulty of recognizing parties while each asserted different religious claims.

The terms deferred the intended pilgrimage, regulated a subsequent return visit, established a truce and addressed the treatment of fugitives from Makkah. The clause concerning a person arriving from Quraysh without a guardian's permission was especially painful when Abū Jandal, Suhayl's son, appeared in chains. It is insufficient to say that the treaty was a strategic success; that description leaves out what the participants thought they were surrendering. The report includes ʿUmar's distressed questions and the Prophet's insistence that his trust in the outcome did not exempt him from carrying out the agreed decision. The episode's immediate object was not trade expansion or later conversion statistics, but an encounter between fidelity to revelation and the difficulty of obeying a command whose effects were not yet visible.

A second scene explains the transition from agreement on paper to actual public conduct. After the terms had been made, the Prophet instructed the pilgrims to sacrifice and shave. The report describes their hesitation. Umm Salamah advised him to perform the rites himself without further exhortation; the Companions then followed. This is a rare sequence in which a political agreement, domestic counsel, ritual action and collective discipline are causally linked. The prior blockage of a religious goal generated grief and confusion; a visible act resolved the immediate practical impasse more effectively than repeating an order. The source allows us to say this. It does not license the stronger claim that we can identify every individual's motive for hesitation.

The familiar judgment that al-Ḥudaybiyyah proved an opening (*fatḥ*) belongs to the transmitted religious interpretation and to the subsequent sequence of events. The truce helped alter the terms in which groups could approach the Muslim community, and later alliance violations preceded the march on Makkah. Yet the exact growth of adherents between 628 and 630 cannot be calculated simply from this one account. A treaty opens opportunities; the inference that it mechanically caused every subsequent conversion would outrun the evidence. Nor should the restrictions on returning women be treated as permanently identical to the initial terms: the report relates subsequent revelation and a distinction in their treatment.

**Limited comparison of two published narrative recensions.** The version of Ibn Isḥāq preserved by Ibn Hishām (2:319, passages headed *What troubled the people at the treaty and the arrival of Abū Jandal*, and *The Prophet sacrificed and shaved, and the people followed*) describes Abū Jandal arriving in irons during the writing, the public distress at returning without entering Makkah, and the Prophet's sacrifice and shaving after the treaty. On seeing him, the Companions proceeded to sacrifice and shave. This part of Ibn Hishām's published presentation does not recount Umm Salamah's intervening counsel. Al-Bukhārī 2731–2732 describes the hesitation after three instructions and attributes the solution to Umm Salamah's advice that the Prophet act first without further speech. These accounts share the crucial sequence—disappointing treaty, sacrifice and shaving by the Prophet, communal imitation—but they give differently detailed explanations of the intervening action. The narrower comparison confirms the *reported* role of visible Prophetic example; it does not demonstrate that the earlier redaction denied Umm Salamah's role. Other transmissions linked to Ibn Isḥāq preserve it. The published texts have been compared here, **not** their complete manuscript witnesses or all chains. Primary passages: [Ibn Hishām on Abū Jandal](https://www.islamweb.net/ar/library/content/58/1280/), [Ibn Hishām on sacrifice and shaving](https://islamweb.net/ar/library/content/58/1282/من-شهدوا-على-الصلح), and [al-Bukhārī 2731–2732](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2731). For an additional Ibn Isḥāq-associated line mentioning Umm Salamah, compare [al-Dhahabī's presentation](https://islamweb.net/ar/library/content/60/6317/قصة-غزوة-الحديبية).

**Source and limit.** [Al-Bukhārī 2731–2732, *Kitāb al-Shurūṭ*, chapter on treaties](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2731) is the detailed base witness; compare [the continuation at 2732](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2732). Qurʾān 48:1–29 and 60:10 belong in the *sharḥ*, where their theological and legal roles can be distinguished from what the narrator reports. A limited comparison with the published Ibn Hishām presentation has now been made; full manuscript comparison, systematic isnād-cum-matn analysis, and dating of subsequent tribal breaches remain outstanding. This dossier establishes a coherent, source-grounded explanation of a difficult event; it does not constitute a full isnād-cum-matn collation.

## Dossier 4 — Khaybar: cultivation, revenue and the limits of political simplification (chapter 17)

The military settlement at Khaybar created a problem familiar in agrarian conquests: an army could acquire claims to land without possessing the expertise or labour to cultivate its orchards immediately. Evicting all cultivators could remove the productive workforce and reduce the harvest; leaving production unchanged could sustain families whose relations to the new authority had altered. The transmitted arrangement answered that problem through terms of cultivation and a share of produce. This institutional detail matters because a victory in a chronicle does not explain how a garrison or community lives in the following season.

Ibn ʿUmar's report in al-Bukhārī 2331 states that the Prophet assigned the Khaybar lands to their Jewish cultivators so that they would work and cultivate them, taking half the yield. A related formulation appears at al-Bukhārī 4248. A short operative phrase in the Arabic is **«وَلَهُمْ شَطْرُ مَا خَرَجَ مِنْهَا»**. This is concrete evidence of a crop-sharing arrangement. It tells us something about the distribution of produce, and by implication the need to maintain agricultural work after conquest. It does not tell us the complete land register, the duration of every household's tenure, the legal status of every parcel, or the motives of each party. Nor does it prove a general policy toward all religious minorities in all later Islamic states.

The two Bukhārī passages should not be called two wholly independent contemporary documentary witnesses. Both transmit the report through the Ibn ʿUmar–Nāfiʿ line with different later narrators and slight wording variation. The distinction matters because *corroboration through related transmission lines* differs from independent material corroboration. Later jurists debated sharecropping, labour, fiscal rights and the conditions of such arrangements. The chapter on jurisprudence should show what problem later legal reasoning needed to resolve instead of presenting Khaybar as the unqualified source of a single doctrine of tenancy.

The case also corrects a one-dimensional account of public authority. Worship gave believers a moral understanding of their obligations, but it did not automatically determine the harvest, the proportions of produce, the appointment of collectors or the settlement of disputes. Those activities required terms, agents and procedures. A legal-historical *sharḥ* can explain these without portraying the Prophet's decisions as mere economic necessity: the reported choice is both a practical judgment under particular circumstances and an action that later jurists studied within a broader religious framework.

**Source and limit.** [Al-Bukhārī 2331](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2331) and [4248](https://sunnah.com/bukhari/64/283), both in the Ibn ʿUmar–Nāfiʿ transmission. The causal connection between cultivation and fiscal continuity is an inference from the agricultural and military setting, not a direct statement of the report. Further work: variants in al-Muwaṭṭaʾ and Muslim's collection, administrative reports and later juristic discussion of *musāqāt* and *muzāraʿa*. Those materials have not been comprehensively collated here.

## Dossier 5 — The *Muwaṭṭaʾ*: when teaching became a text without becoming motionless (chapters 27–29)

The transmitted works of early jurists were not published like modern single-edition books. A student heard a teacher, studied a written copy, sometimes read it back for approval, and passed on a certified recension. A teacher could continue to expand, omit or reorganize material in subsequent lessons. The resulting variants do not by themselves mean that the book had no author; nor do they mean that all surviving copies represent precisely one unchanged dictation. This is central to the emergence of Islamic scholarly authority: a ruling was preserved through human learning practices, and differences among those practices became material for further learned comparison.

Mālik b. Anas's *Muwaṭṭaʾ* is an unusually well-documented case. Ahmed El Shamsy's detailed study compares the surviving complete recensions of Yaḥyā b. Yaḥyā al-Laythī, Abū Muṣʿab al-Zuhrī and Ibn Bukayr with quotations of Mālik preserved in al-Shāfiʿī's *Kitāb al-Umm*. In one case about compensating an injury, the position remains almost identical while a verb describing earlier authorities' activity changes. In another, the vocabulary used in a reciprocal gift exchange varies by one word without apparently changing the legal reasoning. These are minor differences. But the examples concerning a rule's degree of scholarly acceptance matter more: phrases equivalent to “the rule in our view” and “the agreed rule among us” attribute different degrees of collective authority to a position. They are not interchangeable editorial ornament.

A particularly instructive example concerns Ibn ʿUmar's reported practice of sprinkling water in his eyes when performing the major ablution. Yaḥyā's recension preserves the practice without an explicit attached judgment by Mālik, while al-Shāfiʿī quotes Mālik as saying it was not accepted practice; Abū Muṣʿab and Ibn Bukayr preserve comparable statements that the action was not obligatory. El Shamsy's comparison supplies exact identifiers: *al-Umm* 8:696; the Yaḥyā recension no. 113; Abū Muṣʿab no. 124; Ibn Bukayr no. 128. This is an example where absence or presence of a teacher's comment can materially affect what a reader believes the teacher required. A lay reader selecting the most familiar recension might never see the interpretive problem.

A second example concerns chapter organization. El Shamsy shows that the chapter on testimony appears in markedly different positions within the Book of Judicial Rulings across the recensions. This is not evidence that one text's hadith statements were all invented; it demonstrates that the pedagogical arrangement of material also belonged to the history of transmission. A reader studying from chapter headings should therefore distinguish Mālik's teachings, later or concurrent editorial arrangement, and a school's retrospective classification of its authorities. The text's authority was produced and maintained by identifiable practices: reading, hearing, comparison, disputation, correction and regional teaching.

The historical consequence becomes clear through the expansion of legal schools. A school was not simply a student's uncritical reproduction of Mālik's or Abū Ḥanīfah's every opinion. Jurists compared precedent, authenticated reports, distinguished locally received practice from transmitted statement, and trained successors who could apply or criticize inherited reasoning. The *Muwaṭṭaʾ* recensions reveal an early layer of these processes; they cannot by themselves explain every later Mālikī development, still less the origin of all madhhabs. Nor is Muḥammad al-Shaybānī's work best treated as a straightforward equivalent of the three complete recensions: El Shamsy describes it more precisely as a partial critical commentary.

**Textual apparatus and outstanding task.** Ahmed El Shamsy, [“The Ur-Muwaṭṭaʾ and Its Recensions,” *Islamic Law and Society* 28 (2021), 352–381](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/a14wz-9hc46), especially pp. 361–369 for examples and pp. 376–377 for chapter-order differences. The article gives exact modern-edition identifiers and compares specific witnesses, including at least one relevant manuscript. This edition has examined his detailed published comparison; it **has not independently consulted every Arabic recension or the manuscript facsimiles**, so these remain secondary-mediated variant reports, not a new diplomatic transcription. ## Dossier 6 — Learning to act: al-Ghazālī, al-Qushayrī and the Shādhilī explanation of inward formation (chapters 9, 13, 32–33)

The Sīrah contains people praying, reciting, sheltering emigrants, sharing resources, resisting persecution, disputing wealth and accepting judgments. Later disciplines of spirituality were not already present in their later technical form. Nevertheless, the actions provided enduring questions: what makes an act of worship sincere? What habits allow a person to give while facing scarcity? Why can outward conformity coexist with vanity, anger or cruelty? To answer them we must distinguish (a) narrated practices and Qurʾānic demands, (b) the historical development of explicit psychological and pedagogical methods, and (c) later metaphysical interpretations of spiritual realization.

Al-Ghazālī's introductory plan in the *Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn* is a model of genuine intellectual integration. He divides the work into worship, ordinary dealings, destructive qualities and saving qualities. These are not four unrelated collections. Worship establishes recurring acts that orient the person to God; customary dealings expose the person to property, family and social obligations; destructive qualities name dispositions that can corrupt either activity; saving qualities identify the habits of attention, intention and love through which action acquires a proper spiritual quality. Al-Ghazālī's own distinction between outward acts and the inward activity of the heart is therefore an account of **how one moral agent participates in several sorts of obligation**. It does not mean that an eleventh-century psychological framework was explicitly formulated by every Companion.

In *al-Risālah al-Qushayriyyah*, the chapter on sincerity transmits Abū ʿAlī al-Daqqāq's distinction between guarding one's act against consideration of other people's approval and guarding it against self-admiration. This is more precise than the sentence “Sufis taught sincerity.” It identifies two different obstacles: one can behave well in order to be praised by others; one can also inwardly admire one's own religious achievement even in solitude. A teacher must therefore ask more than whether the observable act took place. This produces a practical method of examination, not merely a vocabulary.

Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh makes the relationship particularly concise in *al-Ḥikam*: **«الأَعْمالُ صُوَرٌ قَائِمَةٌ»**—acts are standing forms—while their animating reality is the secret of sincerity. The contrast is not between useless outward law and valuable inward experience. It is between an act considered only by its visible configuration and the act considered as deliberately directed toward God. A debt can be repaid outwardly while the agent seeks admiration for generosity; a person's prayer can conform to required forms while attention and intention remain disordered. Jurisprudence and spiritual formation therefore concern distinguishable properties of the **same** action. The statement must not be used to excuse violations of the outward obligations that it seeks to animate.

The historical continuity lies in practices and questions, not in asserting uninterrupted identity of institutional form. Early prayer, fasting, generosity and repentance are directly attested in Prophetic teaching. The *Iḥyāʾ*, the *Risālah* and later Shādhilī circles made methods of assessing and cultivating dispositions more explicit. Organized Sufi lineages, teaching relationships, litanies and endowed residences are developments with their own documentary histories. What persists through these changes is the problem of integrating outward conduct and inward purpose; the shape of its solution must be studied in each author rather than assumed universally identical.

The later explanations are identified as retrospective spiritual readings of early practice, not unreported words in the mouths of the Companions.

## Dossier 7 — Prophetic inheritance and *walāyah*: do not collapse practice, doctrine and ontology (chapters 19, 32–33)

The finality of Muḥammad's prophethood does not mean that instruction, judgment, sanctification or learned transmission ceased at his death. But those continuities are not one undifferentiated inheritance. A judge issues rulings by reference to authoritative evidence and established procedures; a ḥadīth transmitter preserves reports under a discipline of criticism; a spiritual teacher guides repentance and remembrance; a metaphysician asks what makes prophetic knowledge and human perfection possible. The historical institutions that sustained these activities often overlapped, while their claims to authority differed. The Sunni–Shiʿi discussion of succession can be acknowledged where necessary without making that controversy the foundation of the entire spiritual history.

Al-Ghazālī and al-Qushayrī treat *walāyah* in a field of spiritual practice and sanctification. Ibn ʿArabī makes *walāyah* central to a larger theory of divine knowledge, prophetic inheritance and realization. Souad Hakim's analysis of *al-Futūḥāt* emphasizes that the way of spiritual attainment can be considered through human conduct under divine commands and through witnessing; she explicitly cautions against imposing a single mandatory ladder of stations on Ibn ʿArabī. Thus obedience and spiritual realization are connected, but the relation is not that an act automatically produces a metaphysical degree as a measurable result. The act is a site of divine address and human response; realization cannot be reduced to a behavioral checklist.

Qūnawī's opening treatment of *wujūd* in *Miftāḥ al-ghayb*, also incorporated into *al-Nuṣūṣ*, introduces another order of explanation. In William Chittick's translation and commentary, Qūnawī distinguishes the one *wujūd* of the Real from the multiplicity of its relations and loci of manifestation. The passage concerns how plurality and knowledge are intelligible without multiplying the Real as such. It does **not** straightforwardly answer the historical question of how a particular family in Madinah learned to pray, or whether a treaty clause was accepted by a particular tribe. To import this metaphysics directly into those narratives would confuse ontological interpretation with documentary history.

The expression *al-ḥaqīqah al-muḥammadiyyah* likewise needs historical differentiation. The revised Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy article on Ibn ʿArabī observes that he used the term in passing, while Farghānī offered its first detailed systematic explanation. Farghānī's exposition is connected to Qūnawī's teaching on Ibn al-Fāriḍ's *Naẓm al-sulūk*, first interpreted in Persian and then extensively reworked in Arabic. We must therefore not treat Farghānī's mature metaphysical language as a verbatim formulation of the first Muslims, nor even as the detailed terminology of every passage in Ibn ʿArabī himself. The historically sound account traces a chain of intellectual formulation; the theological and metaphysical account then assesses its arguments on their own terms.

One fruitful integrative question remains: how can a religious act be at once legally valid, ethically virtuous, spiritually sincere and understood by later thinkers as a locus of divine disclosure? These are different formal considerations of a single act, not four separate events. A competent commentary should start with the event's evidence; identify the legal and moral questions it raises; then reconstruct the later spiritual argument with its own premises and textual witnesses. There is room for a traditionalist cyclical interpretation of sacred time, but Guénon's historical cycles cannot be used to supply missing isnāds or infer undocumented causes of seventh-century decisions.

## Dossier 8 — International Islam: two regional mechanisms that must not be blended (chapters 38–40)

The spread of Islamic teaching through Africa and Asia was neither one centrally directed conquest nor a single wave of Sufi preaching. The processes differed with agriculture, settlement, trade, court patronage, writing systems and the density of scholarly institutions. Bengal and Melaka can be compared precisely because they challenge the assumption of a universal sequence in which a ruler converts, his subjects follow and Islamic institutions automatically appear.

In eastern Bengal, agrarian settlement, the extension of rice-growing into forest and delta environments, and the establishment of local religious institutions developed over long periods. Richard Eaton's study *The Rise of Islam and the Bengal Frontier, 1204–1760* argues that political control, agrarian expansion and processes of Islamization did not follow identical frontiers or identical dates. This is a causal thesis to be evaluated against settlement patterns, inscriptions, grants and regional history—not a claim that cultivation mechanically generated religious belief. It invites the sharḥ to ask who cleared land, how local lineages acquired legitimacy, which religious institutions endured and what language people used for instruction.

Melaka was a maritime entrepôt rather than a delta agricultural frontier. Seasonal trade connected diverse merchants and resident communities; courts negotiated passage, customs and disputes, while teachers and families circulated religious practices and texts. The political endorsement of Islam increased some institutional opportunities, but cannot be assumed to have instantly converted every visitor or resident. A careful chapter therefore moves from the practical need for trust and settlement to the possibility of sustained instruction, then tests actual court and literary evidence. Arabic, Persian and Malay-language contact should be documented rather than inferred from a generic picture of trade.

These examples illuminate Hodgson's distinction between Islamic religious life and the broader Islamicate social world. The same route could transmit commodities, words, legal practices and devotional instruction, each by different agents and at different speeds. It does not follow that all such transmission can be labelled the same kind of religious conversion. The chronology and supporting evidence remain regional.

Peacock, *Islam and the “Land Below the Winds”* and specialist studies of Melaka; maritime-historical scholarship on the Malay trading world. Only selected secondary studies have been checked here, so the critical apparatus should not claim a complete collation of Bengali or Malay inscriptions.

## Dossier 9 — Colonial institutions: the transformation of authority must be specified (chapters 43–45)

The transformation of Islamic legal and educational institutions under colonial and modern states cannot be explained by the word “decline.” Courts, schools, state budgets and codified statutes changed who had the right to settle disputes, how judgments were recorded, how jurists were trained and which bodies could enforce their conclusions. These developments occurred differently across the Ottoman domains, British India, Egypt, French North Africa and later nation-states.

A strong commentary distinguishes at least three relationships. First, a jurist giving an opinion (*fatwā*) is not identical to a judge issuing an enforceable decision (*qaḍāʾ*). Second, a large juristic literature containing rival opinions is not the same kind of instrument as a statute that selects a particular rule for officials to administer. Third, the practical scope of a religious obligation for an individual need not be coextensive with the jurisdiction allowed to a state court. These differences explain why an alteration in the state's judicial organization can transform religious authority without ending private study or devotion.

The Ottoman *Mecelle* (1869–1876) is one case in which selected Ḥanafī civil-law material was arranged into articles for courts. Its emergence should be studied alongside Tanzimat-era administration and the legal constraints of its own time, not treated as a complete codification of all sharīʿah or as an instant replacement of every older court. In British India, colonial courts' selection, translation and enforcement of legal opinions produced different institutional consequences. Deoband's curriculum and Aligarh's educational programme responded to overlapping but different questions: how to sustain religious scholarship and how to train persons for a new state and professional order. Those responses should be explained through their respective institutions rather than placed along an unexamined line from tradition to modernity.

For spiritual life the issue was likewise institutional. An endowed teacher, a mosque preacher, a state employee, a privately appointed shaykh and an author of a printed manual could reach students through different networks. Printing and later broadcasting made texts more accessible to people outside established teacher-student chains, while also altering the ways readers evaluated credentials. Religious learning did not uniformly disappear or thrive: its forms of supervision, funding and audience changed. The same distinction persists in digital communication today.

**Limits and later work.** This dossier establishes the variables a case study must measure: jurisdiction, training, revenue, procedure, enforcement, linguistic medium and access to instruction. Detailed Ottoman archives, provincial colonial records and specific madrasa syllabi have not been collated as a cross-regional data set here. No single political explanation should be applied uniformly to all societies or periods.

**Unfinished primary-text work:** no continuous collation of early Sīrah recensions, no systematic isnād-cum-matn analysis, no diplomatic Arabic edition of the Medinan text, no manuscript-based reconstruction of selected law-school transmission chains, and no full clause-by-clause comparison of Akbarian metaphysical treatises. ## Three rounds of independent editorial self-assessment

### Round 1 — Narrative continuity

**Test.** Does every major turn grow out of a concrete historical problem? The Sīrah sequence (1–20) now links household instruction, unequal protection, asylum, pledges, migration, domestic worship, common defence and treaty relations. Chapters 21–26 explain how conquest created administration, settlement and revenue needs, and how those pressures led to new centres of government and learning. Chapters 27–33 connect transmission, jurisprudence, institutions, philosophical method and spiritual formation. The regional chapters (34–42) each begin from a distinctive configuration, rather than one unexplained wave of Islamic expansion.

**Result.** Improvement is substantial, but chapters 1–6 necessarily use different chronological registers and remain panoramic. Chapters 38–42 are partly simultaneous regional histories, so transitions must be made explicit in a timeline. The contemporary chapter is a starting framework rather than an account of every country.

### Round 2 — Explanatory and evidentiary sufficiency

**Test.** Can the commentary identify witnesses appropriate to its claims, and avoid using a later interpretation as direct evidence for a seventh-century event? The four inherited Sīrah dossiers contain precise textual anchors, and the five added dossiers give controlled comparisons for jurisprudence, learned institutions, philosophical theology, Bengal/Melaka and colonial law. The regional sharḥ chapters cite specific published scholarship, with source distinctions and known uncertainties.

**Result.** The apparatus is improved but not manuscript-critical. A named scholar's secondary reconstruction is not a substitute for reproducing and collating primary passages. Source density is uneven: the Indian Ocean and Bengal cases have better modern controls than the early West African and Safavid sections. Political and sectarian differences are described proportionately rather than allowed to dominate the narrative.

### Round 3 — Narrative density, spiritual coherence and teaching value

**Test.** Does the matn invite reading, or merely pack facts? Does the sharḥ reconstruct mechanisms or attach author lists? Are later spiritual doctrines identified as later, without severing them from the practical religious questions they address? Several weak transitions from edition 1.2 were replaced by episodes of money, movement, teaching, cultivation and institutional judgment. The metaphysical and spiritual sections distinguish the disciplines of inward reform from the later systematization of the Muhammadan Reality.

**Result.** The expanded chapters can now be studied as episodes and compared through the dossiers. However, some new sharḥ paragraphs remain too compressed for specialist verification; the edition still needs annotated maps, dated manuscript cases and classroom testing. Its most important test is not whether it has 46 titles but whether a reader can explain the difference between, for example, a harbour's commercial protections and a community's transmission of faith.

## Overall editorial judgment and next bounded steps

The 46-chapter architecture is **provisionally suitable for an advanced narrative study**, not a final proof of every historical or metaphysical claim. The opening Sīrah sequence is more concrete than in version 0.6; the later world is no longer squeezed into seven general surveys. Source controls and methodological limits are visible in the sharḥ and dossiers rather than interrupting the matn.

Remaining tasks are bounded: (1) establish parallel Arabic witnesses for al-Ḥudaybiyyah and the Medinan agreements; (2) collate one juristic transmission across recensions and downstream manuals; (3) trace a named Shādhilī or Akbarian teaching chain through dated books and attestations; (4) add dated material evidence for one West African and one Southeast Asian case; (5) obtain historical and spiritual-studies reader feedback. The manuscript should be retested after those changes; repeated self-audits by the same author cannot substitute for independent source expertise.

## Revision 1.3 — final structural correction

A final audit identified the missing history of al-Andalus and the Maghrib. The expanded outline consequently has **46**, not 45, matn–sharḥ pairs. New chapter 35 addresses Córdoba, the taifa periods, North African interventions, the Nasrid kingdom, the continuity of legal and intellectual learning, and the aftermath of Granada. The change is substantive: it restores the western Islamic lands as historical agents rather than treating them as a footnote to the eastern caliphates.



## Revision 1.4 — source-critical validation and remaining deficiencies

### What was directly checked

- The extended al-Bukhārī narrative of Abū Bakr's protection, courtyard recitation and migration (no. 3905): the causal claim is about the limits of kin protection and public recitation, not a quantified conversion movement.
- The transmitted Medinan *kitāb* in Ibn Hishām, compared against scholarly discussions of other recensions: its explicit compensation, ransom and alliance clauses are the evidentiary centre. No universal claim about the membership of all Jewish groups is made.
- Al-Bukhārī 2731–2732: the clause affecting Abū Jandal and Umm Salamah's counsel are integrated into the pilgrimage-treaty narrative. The long report is one transmitted complex rather than multiple independent documentary records.
- Al-Bukhārī 2331 and 4248: the Khaybar cultivation terms are quoted in short excerpt and their shared Ibn ʿUmar–Nāfiʿ transmission identified.
- Ahmed El Shamsy's published comparison of the *Muwaṭṭaʾ* recensions: the example about the washing of eyes in *ghusl* and differences in status-phrases were checked in his documented collation and given their printed witness identifiers. This is **not** an independent manuscript collation.
- Al-Ghazālī's introduction to the *Iḥyāʾ*, al-Qushayrī's chapter on *ikhlāṣ*, and an aphorism of Ibn ʿAṭāʾ Allāh were inspected as separate works. The Akbarian development was verified through specialist published translations and studies, not direct comparison of Arabic witnesses.

### Four-pass critique

**Narrative test.** The matn still proceeds through 46 coherent episodes without citation interruptions. The strongest case-based chapters are 10–12, 16–17 and 27–28. The later accounts of philosophical argument, Persianate networks, East Africa and modernity remain too broad for all their mechanisms to be established at this granularity. The revised matns of chapters 16 and 17 now contain a visible action or economic arrangement rather than only a conclusion about institutions.

**Evidence test.** The nine dossiers separate explicit words of a source, the historical mechanism inferred from them, and claims that need independent corroboration. They do not claim that two passages from the same transmission line establish independent witnesses. Lecker's interpretation of Medinan participation and El Shamsy's recension comparisons are attributed to those authors. Manuscript facsimiles and every extant chain were not collated, so *critical edition* here denotes a critical **study edition**, not a definitive reconstructed archetype.

**Conceptual/spiritual test.** Practice, disposition, pedagogical institution and metaphysical formulation are distinguished. The *Iḥyāʾ*, *al-Risālah*, *al-Ḥikam*, *al-Futūḥāt*, *Miftāḥ al-ghayb* and Farghānī's commentary are neither assumed contemporaneous nor collapsed into one terminology. The philosophy of *walāyah* requires extended textual analysis beyond this source dossier and is therefore marked as provisional.

**Compression test.** The matns remain navigable and generally narrative; the commentaries have received primary-based amendments rather than an added sequence of unrelated author names. Some repetition between a chapter sharḥ and its corresponding dossier is intentional for standalone consultation, but the dossiers are more detailed and identify precisely which claims remain unproved. A full editorial review should use a second independent reader for flow and source checking.

### Priority unresolved tasks before a definitive critical edition

1. Obtain and compare the full Ibn Hishām and Abū ʿUbayd recensions of the Medinan document from specified printed editions and, where accessible, manuscript witnesses; make a diplomatic Arabic clause apparatus including omissions and word differences.
2. Collate the al-Ḥudaybiyyah narrative across al-Bukhārī, Ibn Hishām/Ibn Isḥāq and early maghāzī transmissions; distinguish independently transmitted details from common source strata.
3. Produce original-script parallel columns for the cited Mālik variants using the Yaḥyā, Abū Muṣʿab and Ibn Bukayr recensions and the *Umm*, with exact page and report identifiers and image verification.
4. Reconstruct one limited conceptual argument in Ibn ʿArabī–Qūnawī–Farghānī through the primary Arabic texts, separating doctrinal lineage from retrospective interpretations of the Sīrah.
5. Have specialists independently review the Arabic text extracts, chronological claims and theological descriptions, and record corrigenda. No internal self-audit is a substitute for that review.

**Result:** The v1.4 historical explanation is more thoroughly evidenced at six pivotal transitions, but the full 46-chapter scope remains an advanced teaching synthesis with selected critical dossiers, not a completed primary-text critical edition of Islamic history.
