# After the *Asfār* — Narāqī's three modes of cognition and the direct knowledge of oneself

**Working contextual translation dossier · 1 November 2026**  
**Direct author:** Mullā Muḥammad Mahdī al-Narāqī (d. 1795), *Jāmiʿ al-afkār wa-nāqid al-anẓār*, **vol. II**, within the **chapter on God's knowledge** (*al-faṣl al-thānī fī ʿilmihi taʿālā*), **first inquiry** (*al-baḥth al-awwal fī ithbāt kawnihi ʿāliman bi-dhātihi wa-bi-kull mā ʿadāhu*), under the **third way** (*al-wajh al-thālith*) and its **first preliminary premise** (*al-muqaddima al-ūlā*), treating kinds of cognition, printed **pp.25–27**.

**Original Arabic:** [p.25](https://najafdesertlibrary.com/book/جامع-الأفكار-وناقد-الأنظار/v/2/p/25) · [p.26](https://najafdesertlibrary.com/book/جامع-الأفكار-وناقد-الأنظار/v/2/p/26) · [p.27](https://najafdesertlibrary.com/book/جامع-الأفكار-وناقد-الأنظار/v/2/p/27). These are online edited transcriptions of an Arabic text; no Arabic manuscripts were independently examined. The full *third argument* later proceeds through additional premises and is **not** completely translated here. Its next discussion moves toward the classification of being in itself and being for another through **Bahmanyār's *al-Taḥṣīl***. The book's contents list the third proof and its preliminaries at printed pp.11ff, a second preliminary beginning at p.27, and the resolution of the third proof near p.81.

## 1. The philosophical question—not the keyword “mental existence”

Narāqī is not merely defining *wujūd dhihnī*. He is preparing a demonstration that the First is knowing by examining what it is for a knowing subject to have an object present. The argument must distinguish:

1. a sensible or acquired representation of something outside the knower;
2. cognition of an abstract universal through an intellectual faculty not reducible to material inner senses; and
3. direct, **nonrepresentational presence** of the knower to itself, or of certain objects linked in a special way to the knower.

This matters *after the Asfār* because knowledge by presence, intellectual self-disclosure, and the status of bodily faculties became sites of important reception and reconfiguration. It should be read as a later *development* alongside—but **not as a duplicate** of—the Tajrīd sourcebook's Question 07 (mental existence) or Question 24 (divine knowledge).

### Historical location within the larger demonstration

- **What came before:** the second sort of cognition, and the claim that the intelligible form **in the knower** is what is known primarily; what exists outside is known through its form, therefore derivatively.
- **What the present pages answer:** two objections to a distinct immaterial intellectual faculty, followed by a positive case for nonrepresentational self-awareness and knowledge by direct presence.
- **What follows:** an extension to the subject's body and faculties, then a new premise contrasting different modes of *being* and their relation to cognition; the proof of divine omniscience is not yet reached.
- **Causal/ontological stakes:** the division prevents an automatic identification between the presence of a representational form *in a mind*, the presence of the subject *to itself*, and the concrete extra-mental existence of the represented thing.

## 2. Connected Arabic and new English translation

The extracts below follow the continuous argument **in page order**. They retain objection/reply markers in their original voice. The Arabic excerpts are selected anchor paragraphs **not a full diplomatic transcription**; the translations cover the central claims and the complete objections/replies in this bounded argument, not the entirety of printed pages 25–27.

### A. [p.25] Representation: what is primarily known?

> المعلوم بالذات في هذا القسم هو الصورة الحاصلة الموجودة في الذهن دون ذي الصورة الموجود في الخارج؛ نعم! هو معلوم بالعرض، لأن المنكشف أولا وبالذات عند العقل هو هذه الصورة، وإنما تنكشف ذو الصورة بواسطتها.

**Translation:** “In this class, what is known *in itself* is the form obtained and present in the mind, not the extramental thing whose form it is. Admittedly, that thing is known *incidentally*, because what is first and primarily disclosed to the intellect is this form; the bearer of the form is disclosed only through it.”

**Development:** He distinguishes **the known object itself as external**, the **intelligible form**, and the **act or relation of possessing that form**. One can use “knowledge” for the first-order knowable form or for the apprehending of that form; the two usages are related but not synonymous. This is an inherited Avicennian distinction reworked within his account of divine knowledge.

### B. [p.25–26] Why posit an intellect distinct from the imagination?

Narāqī argues that cognition of forms entirely abstracted from material conditions cannot be adequately ascribed to bodily faculties whose operations are particular. Differences among perception of images, apprehension of particular meanings, combination and preservation justify differentiating imagination, estimation, compositive imagination and memory. Understanding the universal requires a distinct immaterial power, the rational soul.

**Objection (begins at the end of p.25):** Why is that inference sound? Differences among bodily faculties can be inferred from cases where one capacity suffers impairment while another survives. No analogous impairment demonstrates that rational apprehension belongs to a wholly distinct faculty.

**Narāqī's answer, p.26:** “The application of that inference to establish the rational soul is clearer than its use to establish bodily faculties: non-rational animals possess the perceptions associated with outer and inner senses yet do not, on the argument's premises, apprehend universals. He mentions the view that children and persons described by some writers as cognitively limited also lack this kind of comprehension. Conversely, in some people certain bodily modes of apprehension become impaired while their apprehension of universals and immaterial objects remains.”

**Second objection, p.26:**

> فإن قيل: يمكن أن يكون إدراك الكليات لبعض القوى الجسمانية من المتخيلة أو الوهم إذا قوى لأجل كثرة الفكر والتخيل!

**Translation:** “If it is said: ‘Perhaps apprehension of universals belongs to one of the bodily powers, such as imagination or estimation, once that power has grown strong through much thinking and imagining!’”

**Reply:**

> قلت: إن أريد بالقوة الموجبة للتعقل الحاصلة لبعض تلك القوى التجرد؛ ففيه: أنه لا معنى لتجرد هذه القوى مع انطباعها في الأجسام ... وإن أريد بها شيء آخر ... إن اشتداد الجسمانيات وقوتها إنما يصير منشأ لزيادة الإدراكات والأفعال الجسمانية، لا إدراك الكليات والمجردات

**Translation:** “I reply: If by the enhancement enabling intellection in such powers you mean immateriality, there is no sense in calling these powers immaterial while they remain impressed in bodies; besides, this concedes what we wanted to prove, namely that intellection of universals belongs to what is immaterial. If you mean something else, an increase in the strength of bodily powers explains an increase in bodily acts and perceptions, not the apprehension of universals and immaterial things.”

**Logical status:** This is a conditional argument depending on a strong distinction between *material particulars* and *universals*. It is not an experimental disproof of sophisticated animal cognition; Narāqī is working inside the technical psychology of acquired forms.

### C. [p.26–27] The third mode: direct knowing without an added representation

> الثالث من أقسام الإدراك: « إدراك القوة العاقلة للأشياء بأنفسها لا بتصور زائد على ذاتها »، فإن كل عاقل يدرك نفسه لا بصورة زائدة على ذاته.

**Translation:** “The third of the kinds of apprehension is ‘the rational power's apprehension of things **by those things themselves**, and not through an additional conception superadded to their own reality.’ For every intellectual knower apprehends itself not by a representation added to itself.”

Narāqī argues that each person knows herself or himself as a uniquely determinate “I,” not as something capable of being predicated indifferently of many. A representation composed entirely of universal determinations would remain, at least in principle, applicable to another; the immediately known subject is **this very subject**. He concludes:

> فعلم النفس بذاتها إنما هو بحضور ذاتها لا بصورة زائدة عليها

**Translation:** “The soul's knowledge of itself, then, consists simply in the presence of its own reality, not in an additional representation standing over it.”

At the p.26–27 boundary he begins explaining why this is not an instance of abstracting an external object's *image* and then producing a second representation of that image. An insistence that every cognition must occur through an additional form would produce a regress: to know the first form, one would need another, and then a third, ad infinitum. The knowable first form must therefore already be known directly.

### D. [p.27] The regress of representations and the examples of pain and embodiment

> وإلا لتوقفت تلك الصورة الأخرى أيضا إلى صورة ثالثة ... وهكذا، فيلزم التسلسل في الصور

**Translation:** “Otherwise that other form too would depend upon a third form … and so on, producing an endless regress of forms.”

Narāqī also adduces the awareness of pain following the disruption of bodily continuity. The injured state is experienced, he argues, without an additional image of the disruption being produced in another organ merely in order for the pain to be known. Thus **some cognitions require only the presence of their object to the self, or to something having a special presential relation to the self**.

He extends this account beyond the soul's reflexive knowledge to its knowing its own acquired forms, psychological qualities such as courage or love, its own particular bodily organs and instruments, and the bodily powers by which it organises representations and performs inferential procedures. Their special relation to the self, not merely a likeness in a general image, underwrites the specificity of apprehension.

**End of our checked passage at p.27:** the paragraph continues into the next page concerning the apprehension of particular images and faculties. **Do not treat p.27 as the end of Narāqī's historical discussion or silently join it to the next preliminary premise**.

## 4. What changed after Ṣadrā, and why this belongs here instead of duplicating Tajrīd

- The Tajrīd baseline asks whether a mind can know a nonexistent or a quiddity without making the intellect literally bear all the external object's physical properties. **That problem is linked**, not reproduced.
- Narāqī's discussion is a distinct *later source*: whether presence is a more basic mode than acquired representation, how unique self-awareness works, and how a nonmaterial self knows its very own material faculties. These are developments that a bare Tajrīd mental-existence lemma does not settle.
- It would be premature to claim Narāqī's account here simply follows, refutes or innovates upon Ṣadrā. That historical claim needs the **precise al-Asfār discussion on ʿilm ḥuḍūrī and bodily instruments**, followed by a direct text comparison with Narāqī's own complete argument.
- This is **not** an independent translation of earlier authors merely invoked by Narāqī. The exact passage is in his own *Jāmiʿ al-afkār*, not a newly authenticated citation of every source he references.
