I. Division of causes — printed pp.257–258

§1 [257]. We have discussed substances and accidents, their priority and posteriority, and how definitions correspond to the universal and particular things defined. It is therefore appropriate to discuss cause and effect, since these, too, are concomitants of being insofar as it is being. As you have heard, the causes are form, matter, agent and end.

§2 [257]. By the formal cause we mean that cause which is part of the constitution of a thing, and by which the thing is what it is in actuality. By the material cause we mean that cause which is part of a thing's constitution, through which it is what it is in potentiality, and in which resides the capacity for its existence.

§3 [257]. By the agent we mean the cause which gives an existence distinct from its own essence. Its own essence, in the primary intention, is not the subject in which the recipient acquires the existence of the thing whose form is realised there, such that the agent would possess, except accidentally, the potentiality for the recipient's existence. Moreover that existence must not be for the sake of the agent insofar as it is an agent; if it is for its sake, that must be considered under another aspect. For the metaphysicians do not mean by “agent” merely a principle of motion, as natural philosophers do; they mean a principle and giver of existence, as the Creator is to the world. A natural efficient cause, by contrast, gives no existence other than motion in one of its modes: the cause that gives existence in natural philosophy is a principle of motion. By the end we mean that for the sake of which something distinct from the end itself comes into existence.

§4 [258]. It appears that no cause falls outside these kinds. For a cause of something either enters into its constitution and is part of its existence, or it does not. If it enters into its constitution, it is either a part whose existence by itself is not enough to make the thing actual but constitutes its being in potentiality—called prime matter—or a part whose existence is the thing's becoming actual, namely form. If it is not part of the thing's existence, then it is either that for the sake of which the thing exists, or it is not. If it is the former it is the end. If not, either the thing's existence derives from it without being in it save accidentally, and it is the agent, or the thing derives its existence from it by being in it, and it is also its matter or subject.

§5 [258]. Thus one can count five principles under one aspect and four under another. If one distinguishes the receptive substrate which is not a constituent of a thing from matter which is a constituent, the principles are five. If one considers the two together because both share the character of potentiality and preparedness, they are four. One must not take matter in the sense of a receptive constituent as a principle of form as though it were first a self-subsisting thing: it is a principle of the composite. The receiver, as such, is a principle only accidentally, since its actuality is constituted first by form and, taken in itself, it is only potential. What is potential, precisely insofar as potential, is not itself an actual principle.

§6 [258]. It is, rather, an accidental principle [of the relevant outcome], because an accident requires the subject to be actually established before the subject becomes a cause of the accident's subsistence, whether that accident is inseparable, so that the subject is prior by essence, or separable, so that it is prior both by essence and in time. These are the kinds of causes. Moreover, when a subject constitutes and is the cause of an accident, it does not do so in precisely the same way that a matter is the cause of the composite. That is another species of cause.

II. How form is cause of matter and of the composite — printed pp.259–260

§7 [259]. When form is a cause constituting matter, it does not act in the same respect in which form causes the composite, although both cases agree that the cause is not essentially separate from what it causes.

§8 [259]. They agree in this regard, but in one case the cause does not by itself give the other its existence; rather, something else gives the existence, but does so through it. In the other case the cause is the proximate principle through which the effect is given its actuality, yet not by itself: a partner and another cause bring that very cause—namely the form—into existence, and it constitutes the other [matter] through itself. It thereby acts as an intermediary with a partner in giving the other actuality. Form would be like an efficient principle of matter if matter's actuality proceeded from form alone; instead form resembles part of an efficient cause, like one of two people moving a ship, as will become clearer later.

§9 [259]. Form is the formal cause of the composite that consists of form and matter. It is the form of matter but it is not the formal cause of matter as such.

§10 [259]. The efficient agent gives another thing an existence that the other thing does not have from its own essence. That existence issues from the agent without the agent's essence being a receiver of the form of the effect or joined to it as an intrinsic constituent. Rather, each essence is external to the other; neither contains a potentiality to receive the other.

§11 [259]. This does not rule out the agent's bringing about its effect in the very place where the agent itself is, in contact with its own essence. For a nature in wood can be an efficient principle of motion; motion comes to be in the matter in which that nature is, and where the nature itself is. Yet their conjunction is not such that either is a constituent part or a matter of the other. The two realities are distinct, even though they share one subject. Some agents may at a given time not be acting, and their effects may not exist; later certain conditions arise through which the agents come to be actual agents.

III. What an agent actually gives: existence, not temporal “afterness” — printed pp.260–261

§12 [260]. We have discussed this earlier. At that point the agent becomes actual, and from it a thing exists after previously not existing. That thing thus has existence, and it also has the feature of not having existed. Its not having existed does not come from the agent, nor does its having come to exist after not existing. What it receives from the agent is its existence. If nonexistence belonged to it in itself, then when it acquires existence its existence follows upon nonexistence and it comes to be after not having been.

§13 [260]. Accordingly, what comes from the agent in itself is existence: the effect's existence comes from another thing being in such a complete condition that the other has existence from the agent's very existence. That the effect previously did not exist is not the doing of an actual causal agent. Nonexistence can sometimes be ascribed to a cause, namely the absence of the effect's cause. But that its existence follows nonexistence is not something that acquires its actuality from a cause; in the stipulated case its existence cannot occur except after nonexistence. What cannot be otherwise in that respect has no separate cause for that respect. Its very existence may or may not occur, and therefore has a cause; its nonexistence may or may not occur, and therefore may have a cause. But its existence's being after its prior nonexistence does not have a further efficient cause as such.

§14 [260]. Someone may object that existence after nonexistence can likewise occur or not occur. We reply: if by this you mean its existence insofar as it is existence, nonexistence does not enter into its account. The existence itself is not necessary—that is, it is possible. Its nonnecessity is not a consequence of being after nonexistence; the nonnecessary thing is this particular occurrence of existence, which as it happens had previously been nonexistent.

§15 [260–261]. If, however, one takes its existence specifically as existence after nonexistence, one has to attend to its being after nonexistence and not to the bare fact that it is now existing, which happens to be preceded by nonexistence. The temporal ordering as such does not have a separate cause. This is not to say that the existence that follows nonexistence has no efficient cause; it may indeed be caused. What is not caused as an additional item is the abstract property that the existence is “after” the stipulated prior nonexistence.

IV. Why the effect continues to need its cause — printed pp.261–263

§16 [261]. Some suppose that a thing needs an agent and a cause only so that it should come to exist after it had not existed. Once the thing exists, the cause could disappear and the thing would remain self-sufficient. They therefore suppose that a thing needs its cause only in its origination; after it has come to exist it no longer needs the cause. Causes, on this view, are causes of origination alone, earlier than their effects rather than contemporaneous. This is an erroneous supposition.

§17 [261]. Existence after origination must either be necessary or nonnecessary. If necessary, its necessity belongs either to the essence in itself—in which case the essence requires existence and could never have originated—or to a condition. That condition is either the prior origination itself, an attribute of the essence, or something distinct from it. Its necessity cannot come from origination itself: origination has no necessity through itself, so how could it necessitate the existence of another? Moreover, origination is already past; how could it remain the cause of the necessity of existence when it is no longer present? If one says that the cause is not origination but the thing's having undergone origination, this would be an attribute of the originated thing and falls under the second possibility.

§18 [261–262]. Such attributes either belong to the essence insofar as it is an essence, irrespective of whether it has come to exist—in which case the necessity they produce would belong to the essence itself, and it would be intrinsically necessary—or they arise together with existence. But then we must ask whether their own existence is necessary. The same alternatives recur. Either infinitely many such attributes, each possible in itself, are posited, or the series ends with an attribute whose existence is necessary through something external. In the first case, all the attributes are merely possible in themselves; it has already been established that what is possible in itself is made to exist through another. The series therefore does not remove external dependence. In the second, the conclusion is explicit: originated existence remains in being through an external cause.

§19 [262]. Besides, you already know that origination means nothing but existence after not having existed. There are two items for consideration: existence, and having come after not having existed. The originating agent neither produces nor explains the fact that the thing previously did not exist. Its efficacy lies in the thing's receiving existence from it. It merely happens that, at that time, this existence follows a former nonexistence. A feature which attaches incidentally does not enter into the constitution of the thing. The preceding nonexistence therefore has no part in explaining why the originated existence requires a cause. The existence proper to that kind of essence requires a cause even if it continues and persists.

§20 [262]. Thus one cannot properly say that something causes the thing's existence to have the property that it comes after not having existed. This is not something that can be produced. Some existents necessarily cannot be after nonexistence; others necessarily are after nonexistence. But existence considered as the existence of a given essence can proceed from a cause, whereas the circumstance that it is after prior nonexistence does not in that way proceed from a cause. The thing, insofar as its existence is originated—that is, insofar as its existence is described as “after nonexistence”—has no cause for that description itself, whereas it has a cause insofar as existence belongs to its essence. The situation is the reverse of what those thinkers imagine: the cause concerns existence as such. If prior nonexistence happens to precede it, it is originated; otherwise, it is not originated.

§21 [263]. What ordinary people call an “agent” is not, strictly speaking, a cause precisely in the respect in which they call it an agent. They regard someone as an agent insofar as it is assumed that previously the agent was not acting. It is therefore called “agent” on account of being a cause together with an accompanying circumstance. The consideration of what receives causal existence is conjoined with consideration of what the thing does not receive from the cause, and the composite consideration is called “agency.”

§22 [263]. For that reason, in everything they call an agent, they require it first to have been a nonagent, and afterward to will, undergo compulsion, or acquire some new condition that it previously lacked. When this new circumstance accompanies its essence, the essence-plus-circumstance becomes an actual cause, whereas previously it lacked the condition. They therefore speak of an agent as one who comes to be an actual cause after being only a potential cause, not simply as an actual cause.

§23 [263]. Everything they call an agent will in that sense also have to be what they call something acted upon, since they associate it with an adventitious state because of which what proceeds from it now exists after not having existed. But it has been shown that the existence of an essence depends on another insofar as it is the existence of that essence, not insofar as it is existence after nonexistence. This existence, therefore, is an effect for as long as it exists. As an effect, it remains dependent upon another. It is evident that an effect needs the giver of its existence essentially because of its existence itself; origination and other such features are accidents belonging to it. The effect therefore needs the giver of its existence always and perpetually, for as long as it exists.

Source-control notes and substantive issues

  1. Witness status. These are printed Arabic pages 257–263. The edition reports variant readings, but no manuscript images were inspected or collated independently. The translator's 23 numbered units do not purport to reproduce an Arabic author's 23 original numbered paragraphs.
  2. Metaphysical versus physical agent (§3). Ibn Sīnā sharply distinguishes mabdaʾ al-wujūd wa-mufīduhu from a merely moving principle in natural philosophy. This does not deny real physical efficacy; rather, it distinguishes the respects in which the agent makes something move or gives it complete being.
  3. Five versus four causes (§5). The apparently fifth cause is a receptive subject distinct from constituent matter; both can be grouped under material causality due to potentiality. This distinction is especially relevant to debates over whether a subject of an accident has the same ontological role as prime matter.
  4. Partial role of form (§8). Avicenna says form appears like part of the efficient cause in relation to matter because other principles bring the form into actuality; it is not an unmediated self-sufficient bestower. This anticipates IX.5's joint account of separate-bestowed form and natural preparation.
  5. “After nonexistence” (§§12–15). The claim that the temporal order of existence after nonexistence has no independent cause does not imply that a generated being's existence is causeless. Precisely the contrary: the existence that happens to follow nonexistence is an effect. The argument prevents confusing the accidental temporal predicate with the proper object of the efficient causal act.
  6. Continuing conservation (§§16–23). The rejection of “once made, it needs no cause” is directly attested in VI.1. This predates Narāqī and must not be presented as a late innovation. Compare Aquinas ST I.104 and De potentia 5, distinguishing natural generators from essential preserving causes.
  7. The “necessity through another” regress (§18). The argumentative branch is not a blanket rejection of any infinite temporal succession; VI.2 later explicitly permits the possibility of unbounded successive preparatory causes. The rejected explanatory option is an infinite set of merely contingent properties pretending to explain existential necessity without an external ground.
  8. Editorial distinction: a complete chapter translated into new English prose here is not a manuscript-critical Arabic edition, a translation of all of the Shifāʾ, or proof of historical identity with Aquinas. The detailed Arabic base can be checked from the cited edited witness.

Continuous translation

[263] I. What it means for a cause to accompany its effect

Chapter Two: Resolving the difficulties raised against the position of those who hold the truth—that every cause accompanies its effect—and determining the account of the efficient cause.

§1. The supposition that a son remains after his father, a building after its builder, and heat after fire arises from confusion caused by ignorance of the cause in the proper sense. The builder, father, and fire are not, strictly speaking, causes of the constitution and continued subsistence of these effects. The craftsman who constructs the building is neither the cause of the building's continuing constitution nor, in this proper sense, of its existence.

§2. In the case of construction, the builder's motion is the cause of another motion; subsequently, his coming to rest, leaving off movement, or no longer moving and transferring [the material] after that transfer is the cause of the cessation of the movement. That very transfer and the cessation of that movement cause a certain coming together. The coming together causes a certain configuration. In each case, whatever is a cause accompanies its effect.

§3. The father, likewise, is a cause of the movement of the semen. When that movement ends in the manner described, it is the cause of the semen's settling in its receptacle. Its settling there is then the cause of something further. As for its being formed into an animal and its continuing to be an animal, these have another cause. Once the matter is understood this way, every cause accompanies its effect.

§4. Fire likewise causes the heating of the element water. Heating causes the destruction of the water's actual preparedness to receive the watery form—or to retain it. Another thing then causes the production, in such circumstances, of complete preparedness to receive the opposite form, namely the fiery form. The cause of the fiery form is the causes that clothe the elements in their forms: these are separate [from matter].

[264] II. Essential and preparatory causes

§5. Thus the true causes exist together with their effects. The earlier causes are causes either accidentally or as assistants. It must therefore be held that the cause of a building's shape is the coming together [of its components]; the cause of that is the natures of the things brought together and their remaining as they have become arranged; and the cause of that is the separate cause which produces those natures. The cause of the child is the conjunction of its form with its matter through the cause that bestows forms. The cause of fire is the cause that bestows forms together with the disappearance of the complete preparedness for the opposite of those forms. We find, therefore, that causes are together with their effects.

§6. When we judge, in the matters relevant to our discussion, that causes are finite, we are referring to these causes. We do not deny that auxiliary and preparatory causes may proceed without end, one preceding another. Indeed this is necessarily so: every newly occurring thing becomes necessary after not having been necessary because its cause then became necessary, as we have explained; and the cause of that thing, too, became necessary. Consequently, among particular events, the preceding conditions through which actually existing causes become necessitating and become actual causes of their effects can form an unending succession. For this reason the question “Why?” never reaches a halt among them.

§7. But there is a difficulty here. Of these things without end, either each exists only for an instant, so that contiguous instants succeed one another with no time between them—which is impossible—or each lasts through a period of time. In that case it ought to necessitate its effect throughout that whole period, rather than at one of its boundaries; and whatever makes it necessitate its effect would also have to accompany it throughout that period. The question of what necessitates its necessitation would recur, yielding an infinity of causes simultaneously.

§8. It is precisely this last result that we deny. We say that, were it not for motion, this difficulty would follow. But motion keeps one and the same thing from remaining in one and the same condition. What is renewed, state after state, at each posited instant, does not succeed by discrete instants that abut one another; the renewal is continuous. Thus the cause's essence does not necessitate [the effect] simply through its existence—

[265] III. Motion resolves the apparent infinite simultaneous series

§9. —but through its being in a certain relation. Motion is the cause of that relation, or a partner in its cause; alternatively, motion is what makes the cause an actual cause. The cause is therefore neither fixed in existence in one unchanging state nor altogether deprived of existence and newly originated within a single instant. Necessarily, then, what preserves or shares in preserving the order of these causes, and thereby resolves the difficulties, is motion. We shall make this still clearer elsewhere.

§10. It is thus evident that a thing's essential causes, through which its very being exists in actuality, must exist along with it. They must not precede it in existence in such a way that they disappear when the effect comes to be. Such earlier and disappearing causes are permissible only among nonessential or nonproximate causes. There is no obstacle to the nonessential or nonproximate causes extending without end; rather, such a succession is required.

§11. Once this is settled, if something is by its own essence always a cause of another thing's existence, it will always cause that thing so long as its own essence exists. If it exists perpetually, its effect will exist perpetually. This is more deserving of the name “cause,” for it excludes sheer nonexistence from the effect and gives it complete existence. Philosophers call this ibdāʿ [origination without a pre-existing material subject]: making a thing be after absolute not-being. For the effect, considered in itself, has “not-being”; through its cause, it has “being.”

§12. What belongs to a thing in itself is prior in the intellect by essence—not temporally—to what belongs to it through another. Every effect is thus “being after not-being” in an order of essential, rather than temporal, posteriority.

§13. If the name “originated” (muḥdath) is applied to everything whose “being” is posterior to its “not-being,” even where the posteriority is not temporal, then every effect is originated. But if it is not so applied, because being originated requires a time or moment before which [the thing did not exist]—

[266] IV. Essential and temporal posteriority; ibdāʿ and takwīn

§14. —so that it did not exist and then came after that interval, its posteriority is a posteriority that does not coexist with the prior state but is distinct from it in existence, precisely because it is temporal. In this sense not every effect is originated. Rather, an originated effect is one whose existence is preceded by a time. That time's passage necessarily involves motion and change, as you already know. We have no wish to quarrel over names.

§15. Furthermore, what is originated in the sense that does not require time either has existence after absolute “not-being” or has existence after “not-being” that is not absolute, but is the negation of a particular opposed state in pre-existing matter, as already explained. If its existence follows absolute “not-being,” the proceeding from its cause is ibdāʿ. This is the most excellent way of giving existence: nonexistence is wholly excluded and existence established in dominion over it. Were nonexistence to be admitted so that it preceded existence [temporally], the thing could come to be only from matter. The power of bringing into being—by which I mean one thing's existing from another—would then be weaker, limited, and renewed.

§16. Some do not call everything with this characteristic “originated by ibdāʿ.” They say: if we suppose something to exist from a first cause through an intermediate efficient cause, even without matter and with no prior dominance of nonexistence, then because it receives its existence from the genuinely first cause only after the existence of another thing through which it proceeds, its being-made-to-be is not from absolute “not-being” but from “being,” even though it is not material. Others apply ibdāʿ to every instance of formal existence, whatever it may be, whereas in a material effect—even if the matter was not temporally prior—they reserve the name takwīn [formation/generation] for its relation to its cause.

§17. We certainly do not quarrel over these names, provided the meanings are distinguished. We find that some things always have existence from a cause without matter, and others with matter; some through an intermediary and others without one. It is appropriate to call anything that has not existed out of antecedent matter “not generated” but “originated by ibdāʿ,” and to reserve the most excellent sense of ibdāʿ for what has no intermediary between it and its first cause—whether that intermediary would have been material, efficient, or something else.

[267] V. The natural agent

§18. Let us return to our subject. The agent to which acting belongs adventitiously must have matter in which to act, for every newly occurring thing, as you have learned, requires matter. It may act at once or by producing motion, in which case it is a principle of motion. When natural philosophers call an agent a “principle of motion,” they mean the four kinds of motion; they speak loosely in this context by also counting generation and corruption as motion.

§19. An agent may act by its very essence, or it may act by a power. An agent acting by essence would be like heat if heat existed separately and acted: what proceeded from it would proceed because it was heat, and for no other reason. An agent acting by a power is like fire acting through its heat. We have enumerated the kinds of powers elsewhere.

Translation apparatus: textual and philosophical issues

  1. Printed-page boundaries. The chapter heading begins on printed p. 263 and the final two paragraphs stand on p. 267. The website's chapter URL begins at p. 264; its continuous text includes the preceding page. The edition's pagination is more precise than the website URL alone.
  2. The builder: qiwām is “constitution/continued subsistence,” not simply “coming to be.” The house can remain when the craftsman is absent because the craftsman is not a continually sustaining existential cause in the relevant sense.
  3. The father: the passage denies that the father's activity alone is the adequate sustaining cause of the living animal; it does not deny reproductive agency or physical causation.
  4. The Giver of Forms: the phrase “the causes that clothe the elements in their forms, which are separate” occurs inside §4. The causal role is ontological and natural-philosophical, not merely an allegory of conceptual abstraction. The phrase “cause which bestows forms” in §5 reinforces this.
  5. Manuscript/print variants (reported by the printed editors, not independently collated): p. 263 note 19 records الماء versus النار for the element acted upon; “water” is the main printed reading, while “fire” is assigned to witness m. Also at p. 263 n.20, أو حفظها / وحفظها differs. On p. 265 n.17, تأييس (making be) is reported as تليس in witness b. On p. 265 n.11 the printed لا متقدمة is لا تتقدمه in several witnesses. These bear on translation but do not justify claims about archetypal readings without the actual manuscripts.
  6. Motion and infinity: §§6–10 permit a temporal, causal chain of successive preparatory conditions while denying infinitely many simultaneously existing essential causes. The continuity of motion allows changing causal relations without introducing an infinity of coexisting discrete instants. This must not be flattened into “Avicenna rejects every actual infinity” or into an argument for a temporally first event.
  7. ibdāʿ: rendering it merely “creation” would obscure the distinction from temporal creation and from takwīn. The famous taʾyīs formula means “making-to-be,” not first supplying a positive entity called non-being.
  8. al-muḥdath: §§12–14 explicitly distinguish origin in essence from origin in time. This is relevant to Aquinas' account of the possibility of an eternal created world but does not make the two theologies identical on divine freedom.
  9. Significant doctrinal tension: §§4–5 assign substantial form-bestowal to separate causes; §§18–19 still recognise material agents, motions, powers, and physical effects. Thus “Avicenna denies natural efficacy” is false, while “Avicenna gives the natural generator the same substantial-form-producing role as Aquinas” remains unproved.
  10. Scholarly limits: This file gives a new full continuous translation of VI.2 as a bounded unit, but it does not independently re-edit the Arabic, reproduce every editorial variant, or translate all of al-Ilāhiyyāt. Comparison with printed, scanned and manuscript witnesses remains a separate task.

A. The sublunary elements and their causes — pp.409–410

§1 [409]. When the celestial spheres have reached their full number, the existence of the elements must follow. For elemental bodies undergo generation and corruption; accordingly, their proximate principles must be things receptive of some kind of alteration and motion. A purely separate intellect, taken by itself, cannot be the sole cause of their existence. This must be established from principles that we have repeated often enough and whose demonstration we have already completed.

§2 [409]. These elements have a matter that they share and forms through which they differ. The diversity of their forms must therefore be assisted by diversity among the states of the celestial spheres, and their agreement in matter assisted by what is common among the states of the spheres.

§3 [409–410]. The spheres agree in a nature requiring circular motion. What this common nature requires must therefore assist the existence of [elemental] matter, while their points of difference are principles of matter's preparedness for diverse forms. But multiple things sharing a species or genus do not, without the cooperation of some determinate one, constitute the cause of an entity which is itself one and uniform; the entity's constitution comes from something else. Accordingly, this single thing cannot arise from them except through their being joined to a unity that reduces them to one order.

§4 [409–410]. The separate intellects—or, rather, the last among them, the one nearest us—must therefore be that from which, in cooperation with the celestial motions, there flows something possessing an imprint of the forms of the lower world in the manner of receptivity. In that intellect, or those intellects, the forms have their imprint in the manner of agency. Then the forms flow from it into [receptive matter], each specifically determined, not through the separate intellect's essence acting in isolation. For, as you know, insofar as one acts upon one, only one proceeds from it. Rather, it acts in cooperation with celestial bodies. When this receptive thing is singled out by a celestial influence, either immediately, without an elemental body mediating, or through such a body's mediation, it acquires a special preparedness in addition to the general preparedness that already belonged to its substance. A particular form then emanates from the separate principle and is imprinted in that matter.

B. Disposition, specificity and the form of fire — p.410

§5 [410]. You know that one thing, considered precisely as one, cannot single out another thing, considered as one, for one determination rather than another. Distinct differentiating factors are needed. In the case of matter those differentiating factors are its preparations. A preparatory cause is one from which a certain state arises in the subject being prepared, such that the subject's suitability in view of that state is greater for one definite thing than for another. This preparation makes it more fitting that the thing for which it has become especially prepared should exist through the first principles that bestow forms.

§6 [410]. Were matter left in its initial preparedness, its relation to two contrary forms would be alike. What would determine one rather than the other? Perhaps a difference in the influences acting upon it. But that difference itself would stand in the same relation to all the portions of matter; one portion would not receive its influence rather than another unless there were something further in that portion of matter. That can only be the complete preparedness. Complete preparedness is simply the complete suitability of something for the particular determination for which it is prepared.

§7 [410]. An example is water heated beyond measure. The foreign heat and the watery form are now found together, even though [this newly introduced heat] has little suitability to the watery form and a strong suitability to the fiery form. Once the heating becomes excessive, that suitability increases and preparedness grows stronger. It then becomes fitting that the fiery form should emanate and that the preceding [watery] form should cease. Since matter cannot remain without a form, its subsistence does not derive from what is attributed to the first principles alone but from those principles together with form.

C. The ontological constitution of matter — p.411

§8 [411]. Since the form now constituting this matter is one without which the matter had previously existed [when constituted by a different form], matter's subsistence does not come from this form alone; it comes from it, and from the abiding principles, through its mediation or the mediation of another form like it. If [matter's constitution] derived from the first principles alone, it would have no need of form; if from this form alone, it would not have preceded that form. Just as, in the celestial realm, the common element in circular motion requires a nature established by the specific natures of each sphere, so here matter is constituted, together with the common nature, by what proceeds from the specific natures, namely the forms.

§9 [411]. Just as motion is the lowest of the states in the celestial realm, matter here is the lowest of substances. As motion there is attendant upon the nature of what is in potency, so matter here accompanies what is in potency. As the celestial natures, common and particular, are principles of or assistants to the natures, common and particular, here below, so the diverse and changing relations arising from those celestial natures through motion are principles of the changes and alterations of states here below. Likewise, the intermixture of their relations there is a cause of, or an assistant to, the intermixture of the relations of these elements.

§10 [411]. The heavenly bodies influence the bodies of this world by the properties peculiar to them, which extend down into this world. The celestial souls likewise influence the souls belonging to this world. It is through these considerations that one knows that the nature governing these bodies through perfection and form comes from, or is assisted by, the soul pervading the heavenly sphere.

D. Criticism of a purely mechanical theory of elemental origin — pp.412–413

§11 [412]. Some of those who claim affiliation with this science have said: because the heavenly sphere is circular, it must revolve around something fixed in its interior. Friction against that thing necessarily heats it until it becomes fire. What lies far from the sphere remains motionless, cools, condenses and becomes earth. What lies near the fire is hot, but less hot than fire; what lies near earth is dense, but less dense than earth.

§12 [412]. They go on: a lesser degree of heat and a lesser degree of condensation give rise to moisture, since dryness derives either from heat or from cold. But the moist element next to earth is colder, while the moist element next to fire is hotter. Such, on their view, is the explanation of the coming-to-be of the elements.

§13 [412]. This account cannot be demonstrated by syllogistic reasoning; on examination it is unsound. Things seem more likely to follow a different order. The matter resulting from the cooperation [of causes] may receive an influence from the celestial bodies, either from four bodies or from a limited number grouped into four classes. Each of them supplies what prepares the matter for the form of a simple body. Once the matter becomes prepared, it receives the form from the Giver of Forms. Alternatively, all this may flow from a single heavenly body, while a cause hidden from us produces its division.

§14 [412–413]. If you wish to see the weakness of their account, consider what they must suppose: first there is a body that has no constitutive form except corporeal form itself, and only afterward does it acquire the remaining forms through motion and rest. But we have already shown the impossibility of this. We have demonstrated that a body does not acquire complete existence through corporeal form alone without a further form accompanying it. The form which constitutes prime matter is not merely the dimensions, for dimensions themselves depend for their existence upon other forms prior to dimension.

§15 [412–413]. If you wish, consider rarefaction through heat and condensation through cold. Indeed, a body would not become a body at all, on the account they propose, until it were placed in a condition to follow another [body] in motion, so that its following the successive motions which we have shown to be natural rather than forced would heat it. But before any of that can occur its nature must already be complete. It is, however, possible that after its nature is complete, it is preserved in the location best suited to its preservation: the hot is preserved where motion occurs, and the cold where there is rest.

§16 [413]. Nor do these thinkers consider why one part of that matter should sink toward the centre and thereby become cold, while another should pass upward. We have already identified the reason for this: on the general level, lightness and heaviness; and in the case of a particular part of one element, the fact that the elements' parts undergo generation. If some part comes into being in a particular place, necessarily one of its surfaces is toward the upper region. If it moves upward, that surface has a greater claim to being uppermost than the other surface. At its first coming-to-be, however, one surface faces upward and another downward because the part has necessarily undergone some change involving motion, which has imposed a definite spatial orientation.

§17 [413]. What we ourselves have maintained therefore seems closer to the truth. I suspect that whoever originally advanced that explanation of the origin of the elements wished to render the matter intelligible to one of his ordinary correspondents, but those who came later treated the remark as a definitive doctrine. In any case, the writer of that account is greatly given to wavering and confusion.

Issue 23 — Denying preference intrinsic to a contingent being

Arabic heading: المسألة الثالثة والعشرون في نفى الأولوية الذاتيّة ليتبيّن تساوى طرفي الممكن بالنظر إلى ذاته

[90] The problem and the rival views

§23.1 The purpose is to show that the two alternatives for a contingent thing stand on an equal footing in relation to its own essence. That equality did not follow merely from the earlier division [into necessary, impossible and possible], for the possible thing identified in that division was something whose own essence requires neither existence nor nonexistence in such a way that one of them would be necessary for it. This does not by itself exclude the possibility of one alternative being preferable for it intrinsically with a preference falling short of necessity.

§23.2 The majority maintain that the existence and nonexistence of the contingent being are equal in relation to its essence, and that neither alternative has any preference over the other. Some have said that nonexistence is preferable for the contingent being unconditionally. They say so either because they imagine that the occurrence of nonexistence requires no cause, or because fewer conditions are required in the state of nonexistence: the nonexistence of one component of the complete cause of existence is sufficient, and so nonexistence is easier to bring about, whereas existence demands the conjunction of all of them.

§23.3 The weakness of both considerations is evident. Against the first: just as a contingent thing's existence depends on the existence of its cause, so its nonexistence depends on the absence of that cause. Against the second: the absence of a composite is nothing other than the non-obtaining of all of its components together, whether some components occur or none do. This does not entail an intrinsic preference for nonexistence, as though the contingent thing's essence had a tendency toward nonexistence. In sum, the greater ease of the thing's nonexistence relative to other factors does not entail a preference residing in its own essence.

§23.4 Others have said that when an efficient agent exists but a condition is missing, existence is preferable to nonexistence for the contingent thing; or that if its cause exists, existence is preferable, and otherwise nonexistence is preferable. The defect in these views is equally obvious: the preference they describe arises from something other than the contingent thing, not from its essence.

§23.5 Others say that nonexistence is preferable for flowing accidents, such as motion, time and sound, and their qualities, because they cannot persist. This too is unsound. Existence is distinct from persistence and does not entail it. The quiddities of such things, since they entail termination and renewal, are not capable of persistence. Their incapacity for persistence does not amount to an intrinsic preference for never existing.

[90–91] The commentator on the Maqāṣid distinguishes three meanings

§23.6 — Reported speech of Taftāzānī, explicitly attributed by Lāhījī. The commentator on the Maqāṣid says: Sound reflection requires us to distinguish meanings. If “the preference of existence or nonexistence” means that one alternative becomes preponderant in virtue of the contingent thing's essence in such a way that it actually occurs without an external cause, its impossibility is necessary: the thing would then be necessary or impossible [rather than contingent].

§23.7 If someone objects that this would follow only if the other alternative could not occur through an external determinant, the reply is that the occurrence of the allegedly preferred alternative would then depend on the absence of that external determinant. [Hence the intrinsic essence would not be sufficient by itself.]

§23.8 If “preference” means that an alternative is closer to occurring because it needs fewer conditions and impediments or because its causes are more frequently present, this is a preference arising through another, not intrinsic preference. That is clear.

§23.9 If it means that, when the intellect considers a contingent thing, it may discover in it a certain inclination toward existence or nonexistence, but not one strong enough to produce actual occurrence—for that would turn the contingent thing into a necessary or impossible thing—then its impossibility is not apparent. [End of the threefold distinction.]

§23.10 He [Taftāzānī] then says that the majority offered various proofs to exclude this third meaning; he sets them out and responds that they depend on building actual occurrence into the meaning. They therefore do not touch the point in dispute.

[91] Lāhījī determines the point genuinely in dispute

§23.11 — Lāhījī's own response. I say: the commentator has treated the impossibility of a preference sufficient for occurrence as self-evident and not open to dispute, while treating as disputable the impossibility of a preference too weak to bring anything about. But the apparent subject of this discussion is the first, not the second. The theologians made this thesis a premise of the contingent thing's need for an efficient cause and accorded it great importance lest the way to proving the Creator be blocked. Indeed, the fact that the proofs intended to disprove intrinsic preference presuppose occurrence is the clearest indication that what is at issue is a preference sufficient for occurrence.

§23.12 Admittedly, those who call nonexistence preferable to the contingent thing absolutely, or to flowing accidents in particular, appear to intend the third meaning of preference, the one not sufficient for actual occurrence. As for the alleged self-evidence that a preference sufficient for occurrence is impossible, even if we grant that this is necessary, it is not so manifestly necessary that dispute is inconceivable. In fact the proposition that occurrence without an external cause entails that what occurs is necessary or impossible is not itself immediately evident. A necessary being is not simply something needing no other for its existence; it is what needs no other for the necessity of its existence. The issue is therefore open to argument, though the true position is close to self-evident.

§23.13 The author [Ṭūsī] points toward this when he says that it is not conceivable for either alternative of the contingent thing to possess intrinsic preference. By saying “it cannot be conceived,” without offering an explicit demonstration, he indicates that such preference is unintelligible. We can explain this as follows. Suppose a contingent thing has intrinsic preference, meaning that its essence is sufficient for that preference, and suppose that the preference is itself sufficient for one alternative actually occurring. Then either the preference is not mere preference but necessity—in which case the contingent thing is transformed into something necessary or impossible—or else the preference is not intrinsic. Thus a preference that is at once intrinsic and merely preferential is inconceivable.

§23.14 Here is the entailment. Suppose one alternative enjoys an intrinsic preference. If the arrival of the opposite alternative is impossible, that opposite is impossible and the preferred alternative necessary: the first consequence follows. If the opposite can occur, either it occurs without a cause, entailing that the less-preferred alternative prevails without a cause—worse than one of two equal alternatives prevailing causelessly—or it occurs through a cause. If that cause does not make the opposite preferable, it is not a cause of its occurrence. If it does make the opposite preferable, what was intrinsically preferred has become dispreferred. Its preference, then, was not intrinsic, for what belongs to a thing intrinsically cannot be removed. The second consequence follows.

[91–92] Objections to the proof and Lāhījī's replies

§23.15 — Objection. If the possibility of the opposite alternative's occurring, or its impossibility, is considered relative to the essence of the contingent thing, the claim that something intrinsic cannot disappear follows only if the essence requires its preference necessarily. But if the essence requires that preference only preferentially, no such conclusion follows. The opponent does not concede that what is intrinsically preferred cannot change. Indeed, whether a contingent thing can intrinsically prefer one of its alternatives without excluding the other is precisely the disputed question.

§23.16 — Objection continued. If, however, the possibility or impossibility of the alternative is considered as a matter of fact, not merely in relation to the essence, we reject the inference from the factual impossibility of the opposite alternative to the necessity of the preferred one. Necessary existence, after all, means that a thing's opposite is impossible with respect to its essence, not absolutely. Here it is possible that the opposite is possible relative to the contingent thing's own essence but impossible relative to the preference the essence has conferred. The essence, through that preference as intermediary, would thus give rise to necessity, while necessity in the earlier division meant what was necessary without reference to anything else. What is necessary relative to another thing—the preference—is not necessary in itself.

§23.17 — Further objection. Furthermore, a cause only makes its effect preferable when the cause actually occurs. If the mere essence of the cause, irrespective of its occurrence, sufficed to make the effect preferable, both alternatives of a contingent thing would be preferable at the same time, since each requires a cause, and that is impossible. We may therefore suppose that the cause of the less-preferred alternative never occurs. In that case the lesser alternative never becomes preferred, and the preference intrinsic to the first is not removed. Nor will it suffice to appeal to the possibility of the other cause's occurring, for that would make it possible to remove an intrinsic feature, which is also impossible. One could moreover deny that the opposite's cause is itself possible: just as a contingent thing's cause may be necessary in itself, so it may be impossible in itself—for instance the nonexistence of the Necessary Existent in relation to the nonexistence of the First Intellect. [The objection reaches the page-92 boundary.]

§23.18 — Lāhījī's first answer, [92]. We first choose the reading in which the alternative's possible occurrence is considered relative to the essence of the contingent thing. We say that the essence requires the preference necessarily, given our assumption that the essence is sufficient for producing it. The preference cannot fail to belong to that essence. If it could, either its disappearance would be causeless—making the less-preferred alternative preponderant without a cause—or it would have a cause, in which case the absence of that cause would have played a part in producing the preference and the essence would not have been sufficient for it. That contradicts the supposition.

§23.19 — Second answer. We next choose the reading in which the alternative's possibility or impossibility is considered as a matter of fact. To the first objection we reply: when the essence together with a preference grounded in it makes existence necessary, this essence is a principle from which it is impossible for existence to be separated. That is precisely what we mean by necessity; there is no damage to the conclusion from considering an intermediary that derives from the essence.

§23.20 To the second objection we reply: if the less-preferred alternative is possible, it certainly has a cause, whether that cause is itself possible or impossible. Thus the preference of the supposedly preferred alternative depends upon the absence of the opposite's cause. The essence is not sufficient by itself to produce that preference.

[92] The last attempted escape

§23.21 — Objection. But the preference denied in this argument is one for which the essence is sufficient and which is sufficient to bring the contingent thing into actuality. Someone might therefore propose either a preference for which the essence is not sufficient, since it requires the absence of the less-preferred alternative's cause, or a preference for which the essence is sufficient but which is not sufficient for actual occurrence, since occurrence additionally requires that absence. Suppose in both cases that the favored alternative is existence and that no cause of nonexistence is present. A contingent thing would then exist without requiring any existing efficient cause, blocking the proof of the Creator just as in the case of a sufficient preference.

§23.22 — Lāhījī's reply. I answer: where the essence is not sufficient to account for the preference, or where the preference itself is not sufficient for actual occurrence, the source of the preferred alternative's actuality cannot be the essence alone. Consequently, when the preferred alternative is existence, its actual occurrence plainly requires an existing efficient agent, just as it would if there were no preference at all.

§23.23 It thus follows that, once a preference sufficient for occurrence has been excluded, the alternatives of a contingent thing are equal in their need for a cause. Positing a preference that falls short of sufficiency makes no difference to their equality in this respect. The majority's position is established: existence and nonexistence are equal in relation to the contingent thing's essence—equal, that is, with respect to occurrence and the requirement for a cause of occurrence—whether a non-sufficient intrinsic preference is postulated or not.

§23.24 — A further interpretation attributed to “the Master of the Critical Investigators.” That scholar construes intrinsic preference in another way: just as the necessary and impossible are things that have necessity of existence or nonexistence in relation to themselves, rather than being things whose essence causes the necessity of either alternative, an opponent might say that a contingent thing exists because existence is, as a matter of fact, more appropriate or fitting for it intrinsically than nonexistence, without the essence exerting any causal influence at all. Proofs framed in terms of the essence's producing or causing a preference cannot rule out this possibility without qualification.

§23.25 One possible refinement is to say that the essence must be sufficient for the preference, and the preference sufficient for the occurrence of the preferred alternative, whether that sufficiency is causal or merely a matter of concomitance. If the opposite is impossible relative to the essence, the original supposition is contradicted. If it remains possible relative to the essence, then something for whose occurrence the essence is sufficient could fail to accompany the essence. That is impossible in just the way that the effect cannot fail to accompany its complete cause: the impossibility of the latter is grounded in the sufficiency of the complete cause.

§23.26 A more satisfactory rebuttal, however, is that occurrence without any basis in efficacy or determination has no intelligible meaning. The analogy with a necessary being is unsound: in the necessary existent, existence is identical with the essence, which is why one cannot posit a separate causal determination between them; in the contingent thing [existence is not thus identical]—

[93] End of Issue 23

§23.27 —and so the preferred existence cannot be identical with the essence of the contingent thing; otherwise separation from existence would be impossible, and existence would not merely be preferred but necessary. [End of Issue 23.]

Issue 24 — A possible thing must become necessary through its cause before it occurs

Arabic heading: المسألة الرابعة والعشرون في ان الممكن ما لم يجب وجوده من العلة لم يوجد

[93] External preference is conceivable but insufficient

§24.1 [The heading means:] a contingent thing does not exist until its existence becomes necessary through its cause; similarly it does not fail to exist until its nonexistence becomes necessary through its cause. The author indicates this by saying: “Extrinsic preference is not sufficient.” That is, preference arising from a cause external to the essence of the contingent thing does not suffice to bring about either existence or nonexistence; one alternative must become necessary through the cause before it occurs. Extrinsic preference is thus conceivable, but insufficient for occurrence, whereas intrinsic preference sufficient for occurrence was not conceivable.

§24.2 Extrinsic preference is conceivable because, if we suppose it and suppose that the contingent thing occurs by means of it alone, no transformation of an intrinsically contingent being into an intrinsically necessary one follows, nor does an intrinsically belonging feature disappear. If the opposite alternative cannot occur, the preference need not have become an intrinsic necessity, so the reversal of intrinsic modality does not follow: necessity through another is consistent with intrinsic possibility. If the opposite can occur, this involves the disappearance of a feature received through another through the action of another, which is not impossible.

§24.3 But extrinsic preference is not sufficient. This is what the author indicates in his reason: “For supposing it does not make the opposite impossible.” Supposing extrinsic preference for one alternative does not make the opposite alternative impossible. Otherwise it would be extrinsic necessity, not extrinsic preference. With that preference, then, the opposite can occur as well as the supposedly preferred alternative. The occurrence and non-occurrence of the favored alternative remain equally possible under the conditions specified; the preference has been included in the complete specification of either outcome, yet has not determined one.

§24.4 For example, a contingent thing whose existence is favored can still fail to exist, just as it can exist. Neither outcome has been settled by the preference alone. If its existence is supposed to occur solely through that preference, one of two equal alternatives prevails without a determinant. If it occurs through some further determinant, we repeat the question about that determinant. We must finally reach necessity—a determinant through which the contingent thing's existence, for example, becomes necessary—otherwise an infinite regress ensues. More decisively, even allowing the entire infinite sequence of proposed determinants, either their total operation necessitates the contingent thing's existence, which is what we seek, or it does not, in which case we still have a prevailing alternative without a sufficient determinant. Hence one alternative of the possible thing cannot occur until it becomes necessary.

[93] Jurjānī, Dawwānī and Lāhījī

§24.5 — Objection of al-Sayyid al-Sharīf al-Jurjānī as attributed in this passage. The need for a further determinant can be disputed. Why should not the preponderance arising from an external cause suffice for the favored alternative's occurrence? That is not self-evidently impossible; what is self-evidently impossible is the occurrence of one of two equal alternatives or the less favored alternative. [End reported objection.]

§24.6 — Lāhījī. From the explanation already given, it is apparent that this objection has no force.

§24.7 — Jurjānī's alternative argument. It would be better to say that the cause through which existence occurs must determine existence. Otherwise, with it, existence and nonexistence would both be possible. Suppose existence accompanies it at one time and nonexistence at another. The fact that one alternative occurs at the one time but not the other, if not owing to a determinant absent at the other time, entails the uncaused preponderance of one of two equals.

§24.8 — Dawwānī's objection to that formulation. What is possible is a thing whose existence and nonexistence are admissible when considered in relation to its essence, not necessarily something capable of existing at one time and not at another. Such temporal alternation may be impossible even though the thing is contingent, as with time itself. Even if we concede the supposition, one could say that the alternation is impossible in relation to the preference furnished by the cause: if that preference persists throughout the relevant period, existence cannot be restricted to one part and nonexistence to another without an additional differentiating consideration.

§24.9 — Lāhījī's repair. I say: this objection does reach the argument in that form. But if we put it instead as follows, it would succeed. If existence is not necessitated by the cause, both existence and nonexistence remain possible in its presence. Thus nonexistence could occur instead of existence; the specification of one alternative rather than the other requires a further determinant. The objection based on two different times would then no longer apply.

§24.10 Note also that this proof against merely extrinsic preference would exclude intrinsic preference too, if intrinsic preference were conceivable, as is clear from what has preceded. [Here the issue's argument ends; the next words in the source begin a further lemma on antecedent and consequent necessity and are not translated in this fascicle.]

Critical apparatus and argumentative reconstruction

  1. Doctrinal scope. Awlawiyya means a preference or preponderance weaker than wujūb (necessity). The question is not whether some physical conditions make an event statistically likelier. It concerns whether preference, taken as metaphysically insufficient for necessity, can fully explain actual existence.
  2. The decisive distinction in Issue 23: “not necessary” leaves open (a) a merely conceptual preference, (b) a preference aided by extrinsic conditions, and (c) an intrinsic preference itself sufficient for occurrence. The objection to the argument for a First Cause principally targets (c). Lāhījī recognizes that (a) may be noncontradictory yet irrelevant to causal sufficiency.
  3. Speech layers: Lāhījī quotes or paraphrases Taftāzānī's Sharḥ al-Maqāṣid, Ṭūsī's Tajrīd, Jurjānī and Dawwānī. Their speeches above are translated as reported by Lāhījī, not as independently verified versions of those works. This prevents false witness-counting.
  4. “The Master of the Critical Investigators” is retained as an unattributed title in §23.24. Its named identity must be established by collating the wider witness and scholastic usage; it is unsafe to assign it to a particular individual here.
  5. Printed/transcription difficulty, p.92: the sentence transcribed as أو الأولوية كافية الوقوع sits inside a reply that logically requires “or the preference is insufficient for occurrence”; the preceding objection and the following conclusion make this apparent. The English represents the argumentative reading, but no silent Arabic emendation is claimed: verify the printed scan and manuscript before choosing between غير كافية and other possibilities.
  6. Further p.92 difficulty: أو الأولوية كافية الوقوع and several unpointed forms in this searchable text underscore the need to distinguish translation of a plausible authorial reading from a settled critical text. Treat this as a working edition.
  7. Lāhījī's result is not a proof of determinism in ordinary physics. The conditional maxim is: given the complete cause, one alternative must be necessitated. Whether “complete cause” includes divine volition, conditions, and impediments remains a distinct argument.
  8. The Ibn Sīnā connection: compare Ilāhiyyāt I.6, IV.1, VI.1–2 and the later Ishārāt proof. His necessity-through-another does not simply mean that the quiddity itself becomes intrinsically necessary.
  9. The Aquinas connection: distinguish necessitas consequentiae (conditional necessity given the complete antecedent) and necessitas consequentis (necessity of the resulting thing). The nearest Thomist analogy is a conditional necessity compatible with contingency and, in appropriate contexts, divine free willing. Analogy is not identity.
  10. Article 1. Whether creatures need to be conserved in being by God

Objections

1. [32757] It seems that creatures do not need to be kept in existence by God. For what cannot fail to exist needs no conservation in being, just as something that cannot depart needs no one to prevent it from departing. Yet some creatures are such that, according to their natures, they cannot cease to be. Therefore not all creatures need divine conservation. The minor premise is shown as follows: what belongs to a thing of itself must belong to it, and its opposite cannot belong to it; a multiple of two, for example, is necessarily even and cannot be odd. Existence follows form of itself, for everything is an actual being insofar as it has form. Some creatures are subsistent forms, as has been said of angels, so that existence belongs to them of itself. Likewise things whose matter has potency only for one form, such as celestial bodies, according to the older cosmology, exist by necessity of their own natures and cannot cease to be: the capacity not to exist could be grounded neither in form, which existence follows of itself, nor in matter under a form that it cannot lose, since it has potency for no alternative form.

2. [32758] Further, God is more powerful than any created agent. But a created agent can impart to its effect the ability to continue in being after the agent's operation ceases: a house remains after the builder's action ceases, and water heated by fire retains heat for a time after the fire is removed. A fortiori, God can grant a creature continued being after divine action has ceased.

3. [32759] Further, no violent event occurs without an acting cause. But tending toward nonexistence is unnatural and violent to any creature, since every creature naturally seeks being. No creature, then, can tend toward nonexistence except through something acting to corrupt it. Yet there are things which nothing can corrupt, such as spiritual substances and celestial bodies. Such creatures cannot tend toward nonexistence even if divine action ceases.

4. [32760] Further, if God conserves things in being, this will be through some action. Every efficacious action of an agent produces something in the effect. Therefore the conserving action would produce something in the creature. But it apparently does not. It cannot produce the creature's very existence, since what already exists is not made; nor can it add something else, since God would then either not conserve continuously or continually add something to the creature, which is unacceptable. Thus creatures are not conserved by God.

On the contrary — [32761]

Scripture says (Hebrews 1) that the Son sustains all things by the word of his power.

I respond — [32762]

We must say, both according to faith and according to reason, that creatures are conserved in being by God. To make this clear we must consider that one thing conserves another in two ways.

One way is indirectly and accidentally, as someone is said to preserve a thing by removing a corrupting cause: a person who prevents a child from falling into fire is said to preserve the child. In this way God may be said to preserve some things, but not all: some things have no corrupting causes that have to be removed in order to conserve them.

A second way is directly and of itself, insofar as the conserved thing depends on what preserves it so that it cannot exist without it. In this way every creature needs divine conservation. The being of every creature depends upon God, so that none could subsist even for a moment, but would fall into nothingness, unless conserved by the operation of divine power, as Gregory says.

This can be seen as follows. Every effect depends on its cause according to the respect in which it is its cause. But some agents are causes of their effects only in their coming-to-be (secundum fieri), not directly in their being (secundum esse). This happens among both artefacts and natural things.

A builder is the cause of the house's coming-to-be, but not directly of its continuing being. Clearly the house's being follows its form; that form is a composition and ordering which follows the natural powers of certain things. As a cook cooks food by employing a natural active power—fire—so a builder makes a house by employing mortar, stones and timber, whose natures make them capable of receiving and retaining such composition and order. The house's being therefore depends on the natures of these things, while its coming-to-be depends on the builder's operation.

The same reasoning applies to natural things. If an agent is not cause of form precisely as this kind of form, it will not be an essential cause of the existence following that form, but only a cause of the effect's coming-to-be. Clearly, when two things belong to the same species, one cannot be an essential cause of the other's form insofar as it is such a form, since on that account it would be a cause of its own form—the intelligible account of the form is the same in both. Yet it can be a cause of that form as received in this matter, that is, of this matter's acquiring the form. Such an agent causes coming-to-be: one human being generates another and one fire generates another.

Whenever a natural effect is able to receive the agent's influence in the same respect as it exists in the agent, the effect's generation depends on the agent, but not its being as such. Sometimes, however, an effect cannot receive the agent's influence in the same respect in which it exists in the agent. This is evident when an agent does not produce something like itself in species, as the heavenly bodies were held to cause the generation of inferior things differing from them in species. Such an agent can be a cause of form under the intelligible account of the form itself, and not merely as acquired in this matter. It is therefore a cause not only of the thing's coming-to-be but also of its being.

Just as a thing's coming-to-be cannot continue if the agent causing it as coming-to-be ceases to act, so the thing's being cannot continue if the action of an agent causing its effect not only in becoming but in being ceases. This explains why water retains heat for a time after fire ceases to act upon it, whereas illuminated air does not remain illuminated even for an instant once the sun ceases to shine. Water's matter can receive fire's heat in the same manner in which it exists in fire; if water were brought perfectly into the form of fire it would always retain the heat. If it participates imperfectly in something of the form of fire, by a mere inception, the heat continues for a time but not forever, because the participation in the principle of heat is weak.

Air cannot receive light in the same manner as it exists in the sun, so as to receive the sun's very form, which is the principle of light. Since the light has no intrinsic root of that sort in air, it ceases as soon as the sun's action ceases. Every creature stands toward God as air toward the illuminating sun. Just as the sun is luminous by its own nature while the air becomes luminous through sharing in the sun's light without sharing in the sun's nature, so God alone is being through his own essence, since his essence is his existence. Every creature, by contrast, is a being through participation, not through an identity between its essence and its being. Augustine therefore says that if God's power ever ceased to govern created things, their species and nature would immediately collapse; and elsewhere that as air shines in the presence of light, so a person is enlightened by God's presence and immediately darkened in his absence.

Replies

To objection 1 — [32763]. Existence follows the form of a creature of itself, on the supposition of God's influx, just as light follows the translucence of air on the supposition of the sun's influx. Hence the possibility of nonexistence in spiritual creatures and heavenly bodies lies rather in God, who can withhold his influx, than in the form or matter of such creatures.

To objection 2 — [32764]. God cannot communicate to a creature the power to continue in being when God's operation ceases, any more than he could communicate to it the property of God's not being the cause of its being. The creature requires conservation precisely insofar as the effect's existence depends on the cause of being. An agent who is not the cause of being but only of coming-to-be is a different case.

To objection 3 — [32765]. The argument concerns conservation by the removal of a corrupting agent. Not all creatures need conservation in that sense, as has been explained.

To objection 4 — [32766]. God's conservation of things is not through a new action but through the continuation of the action by which he gives being, an action without movement or time. Likewise the continued influx of the sun conserves light in the air.

Article 2. Whether God immediately conserves every creature

Objections

1. [32767] It seems that God conserves every creature immediately. God conserves things through the same action by which he creates them, as was said in Article 1, reply 4. But he immediately creates all things. Therefore he also immediately conserves all things.

2. [32768] Further, each thing is closer to itself than to another. But no creature can be given the power to conserve itself. All the less, then, can a creature be given the power to conserve another. Therefore God conserves everything without any intermediate conserving cause.

3. [32769] Further, an effect is conserved in being by what causes it not only in its coming-to-be but also in its being. Yet created causes, it appears, cause their effects only in their coming-to-be, since they act only by moving, as established above. They are therefore not conserving causes of their effects' being.

On the contrary — [32770]

A thing is conserved through that by which it has being. But God gives being to things through certain intermediate causes. Therefore he also conserves things in being through intermediate causes.

I respond — [32771]

As explained, something conserves a thing in existence in two ways: first indirectly and accidentally, by removing or preventing a corrupting agent's action; second directly and of itself, because the existence of the other depends on it, as an effect's being depends on its cause. In both ways one finds a created thing conserving another.

It is plain that even among bodies many things prevent corrupting actions and are consequently called preservers: salt prevents meat from rotting, and many other examples could be given.

It is also found that the being of some effect depends upon a creature. Where there are many ordered causes, the effect must depend first and principally on the First Cause, and secondarily on all the intermediate causes. The First Cause, then, is principally responsible for the effect's conservation, and all intermediate causes secondarily so, the higher and nearer to the First Cause a cause is, the more its conservation extends.

This is why conservation and permanence are attributed to higher causes even among bodily things. Aristotle says in Metaphysics XII that the first motion—the daily motion—is the cause of the continuity of generation, whereas the second motion, along the zodiac, causes variation in generation and corruption. Astrologers likewise assign fixed and stable things to Saturn, the highest of the planets according to that scheme.

We must therefore say that God conserves some things in being through intermediate causes.

Replies

To objection 1 — [32772]. God immediately created all things, but in their creation he established an order whereby some things would depend on others, through which they would be conserved secondarily in being, always presupposing the principal conservation which belongs to God himself.

To objection 2 — [32773]. A proper cause conserves the effect depending upon it. Just as no effect can be given the ability to be the cause of itself, though it can be given the ability to cause another, so no effect can be given the ability to conserve itself, yet it can be given the power to conserve another.

To objection 3 — [32774]. No creature can be a cause of another's acquiring a new form or disposition except through some kind of change, since a creature always acts upon a presupposed subject. But once it has introduced that form or disposition into the effect, it can conserve it without any further change in the effect. When air first becomes illuminated, there is a change; but the conservation of that light involves no further change in the air, only the continued presence of the illuminating source.

End of Article 2. Article 3 turns to the distinct problem of whether God can reduce something to nothingness, a point vital for divine freedom and the Avicennian necessity dispute, but not translated in this fascicle.

Historical and philosophical apparatus

  1. The decisive correction: Aquinas expressly grants intermediate conserving causes in the sed contra, response and replies of Article 2. The proposition “Aquinas denies all vertical intermediary causes while Avicenna affirms them” is demonstrably false. The real question is whether they assign the same causal function to the same kind of intermediate causes.
  2. Similarity and difference in the builder and heat examples: Avicenna Ilāhiyyāt VI.1–2 and Aquinas q.104 a.1 both deny the builder's continued action is necessary for a house's being and both make the causation of fieri different from esse. Aquinas, however, explicitly distinguishes the natural producer's causing the acquisition of a form in this matter, whereas Ibn Sīnā IX.5 assigns the newly actual substantial form's emanation to the separate Giver of Forms.
  3. The “higher agent” in Aquinas a.1: Aquinas uses the obsolete Aristotelian causal role of celestial bodies as an example of a cause giving a form according to its intelligible account; this is a piece of thirteenth-century natural philosophy, not a modern astronomical claim. Importantly, its logical role is to show a derivative being-giving cause under the First, not an autonomous creator.
  4. Genuine difference from emanation: q.104 a.1–2 does not alone establish Aquinas' doctrine of free creation. That belongs in the adjacent theological texts, especially q.104 a.3 on the divine ability to cease giving being and I q.19 on divine willing.
  5. Identity versus participation: “God alone is a being through his essence, because his essence is his existence” is the Thomist ipsum esse subsistens claim. Avicennian necessity-through-self is close, yet should not be identified with Aquinas' doctrine without separate analysis of real essence–esse composition and divine simplicity.
  6. Do not conflate “immediate creation” and “intermediate conservation.” Aquinas asserts both: God creates immediately, yet establishes a causal order whose intermediate causes conserve some effects subordinately. Any later claim that angels or celestial bodies can create ex nihilo would need a separate argument.

Article 3 — Whether God can reduce something to nothingness

Objections

1. [32775] It seems that God cannot reduce anything to nothingness. For Augustine says in the Eighty-Three Questions that God is not the cause of a thing's tending toward nonexistence. But that would be the case if God brought a creature back to nothing. Therefore God cannot bring anything to nothingness.

2. [32776] Further, God is the cause of the existence of things through his goodness. As Augustine says in On Christian Teaching, we exist insofar as God is good. But God cannot fail to be good. Therefore he cannot bring it about that things fail to exist—which he would do if he reduced them to nothingness.

3. [32777] Further, if God were to reduce a thing to nothingness, this would have to occur through some action. But this is impossible, since every action terminates in some being. Even a corrupting agent's action terminates in something generated, since the generation of one thing is the corruption of another. Thus God cannot reduce anything to nothingness.

On the contrary — [32778]

Scripture says in Jeremiah 10: “Correct me, Lord, yet with judgment and not in your anger, lest you bring me to nothing.”

I respond — [32779]

Some have maintained that God produced things in being by acting from necessity of nature. If that were correct, God would be unable to reduce anything to nothingness, just as he cannot be changed from his own nature. But, as already established, this position is false and altogether contrary to the Catholic faith, which confesses that God produced things in being through his free will. Thus the Psalm says that the Lord did whatever he willed.

That God communicates existence to a creature therefore depends upon his will. Nor does God conserve things in existence otherwise than by continuously bestowing existence upon them, as previously explained. Consequently, just as before things existed he was able not to communicate existence to them and thus not to make them, so after things have been made he can withhold this influx of being from them, and thus they would cease to be. That is what it means for God to reduce them to nothingness.

Replies

To objection 1 — [32780]. Nonbeing has no essential cause, since nothing can be a cause except insofar as it is a being, and being, speaking essentially, is the cause of being. God therefore cannot be the cause of a thing's tending toward nonbeing. Rather, the creature has that from itself, insofar as it is from nothing. But God can be the cause of things' being reduced to nothingness accidentally, by withdrawing his action from them.

To objection 2 — [32781]. God's goodness is the cause of things, but not by necessity of nature, since divine goodness does not depend on created things; it causes them through free will. Thus, just as God was able not to produce things without any diminution of his goodness, so he can fail to conserve things in being without any injury to that goodness.

To objection 3 — [32782]. If God reduced a thing to nothingness, this would not be through an action, but through his ceasing to act.

Article 4 — Whether anything is actually reduced to nothingness

Objections

1. [32783] It seems that something is reduced to nothingness. An end corresponds to a beginning. But at the beginning there was nothing except God. Therefore things will be led back to that end, so that nothing will remain except God; creatures will thus be annihilated.

2. [32784] Further, every creature has a finite power. But no finite power extends to infinity: accordingly Aristotle argues in Physics VIII that a finite power cannot move for infinite time. No creature, then, can endure forever. Consequently, at some time it will be reduced to nothingness.

3. [32785] Further, forms and accidents have no matter as one of their parts. But they sometimes cease to exist. It seems, then, that they are reduced to nothingness.

On the contrary — [32786]

Ecclesiastes 3 says: “I have learned that all the works which God has made endure forever.”

I respond — [32787]

Among the things God does with regard to creatures, some occur in accordance with the natural course of things; others he brings about miraculously, outside the order instilled in creatures, as will be explained below. What God will do according to the natural order placed in things can be considered from their own natures. Miraculous actions, meanwhile, are ordered to the manifestation of grace, as the Apostle says in 1 Corinthians 12, “To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for some benefit,” before proceeding to speak of the working of miracles.

The natures of creatures, however, show that none is reduced to nothingness. For creatures are either immaterial, so that in them there is no potentiality for nonbeing, or material, so that at least matter always remains, since matter is incorruptible as the underlying subject of generation and corruption.

Nor does reducing something to nothingness belong to the manifestation of grace, since divine power and goodness are shown more clearly through the conservation of things in being. We must therefore say simply that nothing at all is reduced to nothingness.

Replies

To objection 1 — [32788]. That things were brought into existence after not having existed manifests the power of the producer. But their being reduced to nothingness would impede this manifestation, since God's power is chiefly shown by conserving things in being. Thus the Apostle says in Hebrews 1 that God sustains all things by the word of his power.

To objection 2 — [32789]. The creature's power for existing is only receptive, whereas the active power belongs to God, from whom the influx of being proceeds. Therefore the infinite endurance of things follows the infinity of divine power. Nevertheless some things have an appointed ability to persist only for a definite time, insofar as an opposing agent can prevent their reception of the influx of being; their finite power cannot resist that contrary agent indefinitely but only for a limited period. Therefore creatures without contraries, despite possessing finite power, endure forever.

To objection 3 — [32790]. Forms and accidents are not complete beings, because they do not subsist; rather, each is a constituent aspect of a being, being called a being insofar as something is through it. Even though they cease, they are not altogether reduced to nothingness in the mode of existence proper to them. This is not because a part of them remains, but because they remain in the potency of matter or of the subject.

Interpretive adjudication: Aquinas and Avicenna

  1. Continuing dependence and free will are logically distinct. ST I q.104 aa.1–2 gives the demonstration that being depends continuously upon a cause and permits the activity of intermediate conserving causes. Article 3 adds the explicitly further thesis that God could have refrained from creation or conservation because his effect depends on free will, not necessitation by nature. Avicenna's VI.1–2 argues for continual dependence, but it cannot therefore be identified without further premises with Aquinas' free act of creation.
  2. Possibility versus actuality of annihilation. Aquinas affirms the possibility that God could withhold being (a.3), while denying that any creature actually is or will be reduced to nothingness (a.4). Mixing these two would create a contradiction absent from his text.
  3. No positive causal process produces nonbeing. Both Avicenna VI.1's distinction between making existence and the incidental feature “after nonexistence,” and Aquinas a.3's account of annihilation by withdrawal, recognize a meaningful distinction between an act of bestowing being and descriptions of nonbeing.
  4. Scope of historical natural philosophy. The natural incorruptibility of celestial bodies in q.104 a.4 is a historical Aristotelian model, not an astronomical claim we adopt. The philosophical contrast between receptive finite capacity and unlimited primary power must be evaluated separately.
  5. Forms and accidents. Aquinas distinguishes the loss of a form from the complete annihilation of a subsistent being. This complicates readings of prime matter, substantial change, and the unity of a generated composite in the corresponding Avicennian and Lāhījī passages.
  6. Primary documents. Earlier Aquinas articles 1–2, Avicenna VI.1, Avicenna VI.2, Avicenna IX.5, Lāhījī I:90–93. Each retains a bounded coverage stamp.
