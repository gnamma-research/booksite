## I. Why this chapter belongs between *jaʿl* and divine agency

In the previous chapters, Lāhījī asks **what the cause makes**: not the abstract concept of existence, nor an essence imagined to be already actual, but this particular existent, understood in different respects. The natural-philosophy chapters then distinguish natural generation, the intrinsic form of a composite, and the Active Intellect's bestowal of forms. These discussions raise a third problem: **what sustains a substance once generated?**

The question cannot be answered by confusing three different meanings of “cause”:

1. **A cause of becoming**: an agent that produces a transition or arrangement, such as a builder arranging materials.
2. **A cause of form**: an agent or principle responsible for the presence of a constitutive substantial form.
3. **A cause of continuing being**: a cause upon which the actuality of an effect depends *as long as that effect exists*.

A single cause may occupy more than one role. It does **not** follow that every natural generator performs all three, nor that there is exactly one cause occupying each role.

## II. Ibn Sīnā: the cause explains existence, not merely “after nonexistence”

### II.1. What is given by the efficient agent?

**Arabic lemma — *Ilāhiyyāt*, printed p.260**

> إنما له من الفاعل وجوده.

**Working translation:** What the effect receives from its efficient cause is **its existence**.

**Contextual argument — editorial exposition:** Ibn Sīnā distinguishes (a) *the effect's existence*, (b) *its previously not existing*, and (c) *its being now existent after a period of nonexistence*. Only the first is, precisely as such, the positive actuality received from the efficient source. The fact of prior nonexistence is not a positive effect manufactured by that source. The causal relation is therefore not adequately defined as the bringing about of the *temporal relation “after”*.

**Interpretive consequence:** Even an eternal contingent effect could, in this technical sense, need a cause for its existence. Whether such an eternal created world is possible is a *further* thesis; the modal analysis does not itself prove a created cosmos has no first temporal moment.

### II.2. The preservation objection is already Avicennian

**Arabic lemma — p.261, an objection Ibn Sīnā reports**

> فظن من ظن أن الشيء إنما يحتاج إلى العلة في حدوثه، فإذا حدث ووجد فقد استغنى عن العلة.

**Working translation:** Some supposed that a thing needs its cause only in its origination, and that once it has originated and exists, it has become independent of the cause.

Ibn Sīnā immediately rejects that inference. Consider the effect *now existing*:

- Either it is necessary through its own essence, in which case it was not contingent simply in virtue of having originated.
- Or its continued existence is necessary under some condition, which must be analysed to discover whether that condition is the past event of origination, an intrinsic attribute, or an independently existing determinant.
- **Temporal origination cannot supply the needed necessity**. Origination is itself contingent and, as a past description, cannot by itself explain an effect's actuality now.
- If the supposed intrinsic characteristic makes the essence necessary in itself, the original classification of that effect as contingent has been abandoned rather than explained.
- A merely further dependent characteristic sends us back to the causal question; it does not eliminate it.

### II.3. The closing premise from p.262

**Arabic lemma**

> فلا دخول للعدم المتقدم في أن يكون للوجود الحادث علة.

**Working translation:** The preceding nonexistence plays no role in making the originated existent require a cause.

The primary source continues by affirming that the kind of being in question requires a cause **even if it continues and persists**. This is a stronger textual foundation for the modern comparison than simply assuming that every medieval philosopher believed that temporal creation and conservation were two independent events.

### II.4. From VI.1 to VI.2: builders, fathers and heat

The next chapter, **VI.2**, begins at printed p.264, with an objection: builders die and their houses remain; fathers die and their children remain; fire can depart while heat persists.

**Arabic lemma — VI.2, p.264**

> فإن البناء والأب والنار ليست عللا بالحقيقة لقوام هذه المعلولات.

**Working translation:** For the builder, the father and the fire are not, strictly speaking, causes of the continuing constitution of these effects.

**The argument:** These agents are causes of specific **motions and preparatory transformations**. A builder moves materials and produces an arrangement; a father is a causal condition of reproductive processes; a fire produces physical alterations in a patient. The essential and existential dependence of the resulting composite may have a different set of causes from the causes of those earlier transformations.

**Crucial qualification:** This does **not** imply that fire cannot be a genuine secondary cause of heating or that builders do not really construct buildings. Ibn Sīnā's precise claim is about **whether their previous actions are the continuing ontological cause of the already established result**.

**Difference between philosophical and everyday causal speech:** “The father caused the son” is appropriate in ordinary speech. In the causal analysis, “father” names a participant in an earlier process, not automatically the simultaneous cause upon which the son's present existing depends.

## III. Narāqī: the late defence by the essence's enduring contingency

Narāqī's fourth preliminary in *Jāmiʿ al-afkār*, I:38–42, explicitly states the same principle. His contribution is **not discovering conservation for the first time**. He gives a connected modal and epistemological demonstration, alongside objections from the theological tradition.

### III.1. Ground of need: contingency rather than origination

**Arabic lemma — printed p.38**

> الحقّ أن علة احتياج الممكن إلى المؤثّر هو الإمكان.

**Working translation:** The correct view is that **contingency** is the ground of a possible being's need for an efficient cause.

The argument depends on the principle that, if existence and nonexistence are equally compatible with the essence of a contingent being, their actual determination requires an external determinant (*murajjiḥ*). One must distinguish **not requiring either alternative** from the stronger (and contradictory) suggestion that an essence somehow positively requires both alternatives.

### III.2. The priority argument

Narāqī argues that making temporal origination the ground of causal need leads to a cycle in explanatory priority:

1. Origination presupposes an existent with a prior lack of existence.
2. That existent's actuality presupposes production.
3. Production presupposes the effect's dependence upon an efficient source.
4. The ground of that dependence must be prior **in the order of explanation**.
5. If temporal origination were the ground, the result of this order would have to be prior to its own explanatory prerequisites.

These are **ontological and epistemic ranks**, not elapsed moments measured on a clock.

### III.3. Why the distinction matters for persistence

**Arabic lemma — printed p.39**

> الإمكان لازم لماهية الممكن ذاتي لها لا ينفكّ عنها حال البقاء أيضاً.

**Working translation:** Contingency is intrinsic to a possible quiddity and does not depart from it even in the state of continuance.

Therefore the reason for causal dependence is still present while the effect exists. Narāqī also meets the argument that continued conservation would be *taḥṣīl al-ḥāṣil*, a useless second conferment of existence already obtained: **being at an earlier time does not by itself account for the continuance of being at a later time**.

**Interpretive caution:** This need not mean that a new numerically different substance is continuously created at each instant. One must not smuggle occasionalism into an Avicennian conservation argument.

## IV. Aquinas: created causes can genuinely conserve

This is an **important corrective** to simplified modern comparisons.

### IV.1. Conservation is a distinct explanatory dependence, not an additional temporal creation

In *Summa theologiae* I q.104 a.1, Aquinas distinguishes protection **against corruption** from direct conservation through the cause upon which a being depends. Every creature requires the latter from God, even if a particular creature has no ordinary natural cause of corruption.

**Latin lemma — q.104 a.1 ad 4 [32766]**

> conservatio rerum a Deo non est per aliquam novam actionem; sed per continuationem actionis qua dat esse.

**Working translation:** God's preservation of things is not a fresh action superadded to the first; it is the continuance of the action by which he gives them being.

This rebuts the assumption that conservation must mean repeated, separate temporal creations. It also explains why the first cause can continually support an effect whose intrinsic natural powers persist and operate.

### IV.2. The builder analogy: exact convergence and precise difference

**Latin lemma — q.104 a.1 corpus [32762]**

> Aedificator enim est causa domus quantum ad eius fieri, non autem directe quantum ad esse eius.

**Working translation:** A builder causes a house **in its coming-to-be**, but is not directly the cause of the house **with respect to its being**.

This is strikingly close to Ibn Sīnā's example in *Ilāhiyyāt* VI.2. But do not conflate their surrounding metaphysics. Aquinas explains the continuing house by the arrangement's dependence upon the natural qualities of the building materials; Ibn Sīnā embeds the example in a cosmology in which the ultimate bestowers of elemental substantial forms are separate principles. The *analogy* is shared, but the causal allocation behind it differs.

### IV.3. The underappreciated article: **secondary conservation** (q.104 a.2)

Aquinas explicitly says that creatures may be conserving causes of other creatures. He distinguishes mere indirect conservation (e.g. shielding something against a corrupting agent) from **direct conservation by an ordered cause** whose influence helps sustain an effect.

**Latin lemma — q.104 a.2 corpus [32771]**

> necesse est quod effectus dependeat primo quidem et principaliter a causa prima; secundario vero ab omnibus causis mediis.

**Working translation:** The effect must depend first and principally on the First Cause, and secondarily upon all the intermediary causes.

He even says that in some cases God preserves effects *through* intermediary causes, while his own principal conservation remains presupposed. Therefore:

**“Avicenna allows mediation, Aquinas allows only immediate causality” is false as a blanket historical claim.**

Aquinas rejects an account in which **separate intellects are autonomous creators** or the First's free act is constrained by cosmic necessitation. He does **not** deny that created causes can exercise a conserving influence in their proper order.

### IV.4. Created generation and conservation are still constrained

Aquinas q.104 a.2 ad 3 distinguishes introducing a new form or disposition by change from preserving the resulting determination without a further change, as in the continued illumination of air by a present light source. The same created cause can therefore be causally relevant **after** the initial transition, but not because it has become a self-sufficient source of being.

This is closer to the layered Avicennian picture than an exclusively immediate-causation model would be.

### IV.5. The decisive outstanding disagreement: willing versus natural necessity

In q.104 a.3, Aquinas says God preserves the creature because he **wills** to continue imparting existence, and that the First's natural simplicity does not force the production of every conceivable effect. The Avicennian thesis about the completeness of a cause and consequent necessity cannot simply be equated with this theological conclusion.

Thus, agreement on:
- causal dependence,
- simultaneous ordering of preserving causes,
- conservation without repeated temporal creation,

does **not** prove agreement concerning:
- free specification of a cosmic order,
- how many intermediary intellects exist or what functions they perform,
- whether the First could refrain from creating this world,
- the necessity and individuation of substantial forms.

## VI. The strongest constructive thesis consistent with the texts

**Proposed synthesis (not an attributed medieval doctrine):**

An actual natural substance has intrinsic constitutive principles (matter, form and nature), real efficient and final powers, and an identity that persists across many activities and changes. Its causal dependence may include many ordered secondary causes, while none of these causes—singly or collectively—is the unconditioned source of its existing. Its continuous dependence on a First Cause is distinct from both its initial natural generation and the temporal succession of its accidental activities.

This proposal **does not automatically dissolve** the difference concerning the separate Giver of Forms, or the difference between necessary cosmic emanation and freely willed creation.

### Four diagnostic tests for a genuinely coherent AT–Avicennian reconstruction

1. **Ontology test:** Is the quiddity/existence analysis applied to one *actual* existent without introducing a pre-existing essence?
2. **Natural causation test:** Which agent is the efficient source of the *substantial composite*, not merely of preparatory motions?
3. **Conservation test:** Are the First Cause's universal action and intermediary sustaining causes distinguished without treating them as rival agencies?
4. **Freedom test:** Is the cause/effect necessity *conditional upon* a fully specified cause, or does it entail that this entire contingent world proceeds from the First without free choice?

A theory may pass tests 1 and 3 and fail 2 or 4. It cannot be declared “equivalent to AT” on the strength of conservation alone.
