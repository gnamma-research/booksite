## I. The original hylomorphic demonstration from division and continuity

### Matn, gloss, and explanation must be kept distinct

The passage in *Mawāqif* VII:34 argues that corporeal extension is not by itself the whole of the body's reality. When one connected body is divided, the continuous unity is destroyed, yet something remains receptive of separation and connection.

**Arabic lemma, p.34 — embedded matn**

> فان ثمة أمرا قابلا للاتصال تارة والانفصال أخرى ... وهذا الأمر الذي هو قابل للانفصال ومغاير للاتصال هو الذي نسميه بالهيولى.

**English translation**

There must be something capable at one time of **connection** and at another of **separation**. That which receives separation and is distinct from the connected condition is what we call **prime matter** (*hayūlā*).

**Arabic lemma, p.34 — embedded explanation**

> فالقابل للاتصال والانفصال يغاير كلا منهما.

**English translation**

The underlying subject receptive of connection and separation is distinct from either of those determinations.

The argument has three steps:

1. A connected body can become separated.
2. The **same receptivity** must be distinguished from the opposed determinations of connection and separation, since what is destroyed cannot simply be the very subject that remains receptive.
3. Therefore, beyond the corporeal form of extension considered as an actuality, a **material principle** is posited.

This is an intelligible proof for hylomorphism, not a verbal stipulation that matter exists. But the argument requires a substantive premise about continuity and persistence.

### The first objection: why not corporeal form itself?

The later discussion (p.45) points out that the corporeal form of a body, while it entails some extension, need not entail **one determinate magnitude**. Perhaps the same corporeal actuality can receive varied magnitudes. If so, the argument has not demonstrated a **second** entity called prime matter.

**Arabic — p.45**

> وجوابه أن الصورة الجسمية وإن كانت مستلزمة في الوجود والتعقل للمقدار إلا أنها لا تستلزم مقداراً مخصوصاً، فجاز أن تكون هي قابلة لتلك المقادير المختلفة فلا يثبت وجود أمر آخر.

**English translation**

The reply is that, although corporeal form involves magnitude both in being and in intelligibility, it does not entail any **specific** magnitude. It could therefore itself receive the differing magnitudes, and there would be no proof that some further principle exists.

**Critical distinction:** The exchange exposes a problem common to Avicennism and Thomism: **what exactly persists** when a body changes its dimensions, continuity, or substantial form? Establishing that some receptive subject persists does not by itself establish that the subject is **pure potency** rather than corporeal substance with some actuality.

## II. The objection from substantial generation is not an automatic victory

A natural objection is that, in generation and corruption, one substantial form is taken off and another put on; a persisting matter must underlie those forms.

**Arabic — p.45, layered text**

> والكون والفساد ... إذ لا بد فيهما من أمر يخلع صورة ويلبس أخرى وهو الهيولى.

**English translation**

Generation and corruption seem to require something that removes one form and receives another: that would be prime matter.

The counterargument questions this inference by suggesting that what changes are the **specific substantial forms**, and perhaps corporeal form could itself be the receptive subject. It adds that if the phenomenon of generation is already explained by assuming prime matter, using it to **prove** prime matter would be circular.

**Arabic — p.45**

> على أنا نقول وجود هذه الأمور التي استعين بها مبنى على وجود الهيولى فيلزم الدور.

**English translation**

Moreover, we say that the existence of the very things appealed to in the proof rests on [the assumption of] prime matter; circular reasoning would follow.

### A genuine late-kalām challenge

The kalām commentators are not merely saying “Aristotle asserts matter, therefore matter exists.” They are exposing a possible **begging of the question** in one traditional hylomorphic demonstration. A Thomist must answer with a non-circular argument from potency, substantial continuity, or change, rather than invoke inherited terminology.

## III. The theologians' trilemma against matter — p.45

The display introduces what it identifies as a principal argument among theologians against prime matter. Matter either has **location** or it does not. If it has location independently, it appears already to be a body. If located through dependence on corporeal form, it looks like an attribute of form rather than the substrate receiving form. If it lacks location altogether, the relation of bodily form to it becomes obscure.

**Arabic — three core clauses**

> أما أن يكون لها حصول في الحيز أو لا ... فإن كان ... على سبيل الاستقلال فجسم ... [وإن] على سبيل التبعية للصورة الجسمية فالهيولى حينئذ صفة حالة في الجسمية ... وإن لم يكن لها حصول في الحيز ... فلا تختص الجسمية بها.

**English translation**

Either [prime matter] exists in a spatial region or it does not. If it has spatial emplacement independently, it would already be a **body**. If it is spatially located only in dependence upon corporeal form, matter would instead appear to be an **attribute inhering in corporeal form**. If it has no spatial emplacement at all, it seems impossible for corporeal form to pertain specifically to it.

**Voice warning:** The page includes subordinate objections and corrective glosses (including questioning the inference from similarity of the two elements to their strict identity). The trilemma is therefore a **reported theologians' argument within a layered edition**, not demonstrated as al-Jurjānī's final position simply by its presence in his commentary.

### Reply to be tested

The strongest Peripatetic reply would say that prime matter is **not itself an actually spatial body**, but is the **potential material principle of a body**, and that its emplacement belongs to it **through the actual composite** without making it an accident of corporeal form. The trilemma could therefore conflate “is in a place as a constituent of an actual body” with “has its own independently actual spatial extension.”

That reply is **editorial reconstruction**; it is **not** being attributed to a particular commentator until the response in a controlled primary edition is translated.

## IV. *Maqāṣid*: why the concepts of nonexistent things require careful epistemology

The *Sharḥ al-Maqāṣid* pp.78–79 treats **mental existence** (*wujūd dhihnī*) in direct relation to universal affirmative judgements and things without extramental instances.

### The argument from universals and affirmative judgements — p.78

**Arabic**

> الثاني أن الكلي مفهوم وكل مفهوم ثابت ضرورة تميزه عند العقل ... وليس في الخارج لأن كل ما هو في الخارج مشخص فيكون في الذهن.

**English translation**

Second, the universal is understood, and everything understood has some established status, because it is distinguished by intellect. Yet it cannot be established as a universal outside the mind, since everything actual outside the mind is individuated. It must therefore be established **mentally**.

**Arabic**

> الثالث أن من القضايا موجبة حقيقية وهي تستدعي وجود الموضوع ضرورة ... كقولنا كل عنقاء حيوان.

**English translation**

Third, some propositions are genuinely affirmative, and they seem to require the existence of a subject; yet that subject may have no actual extramental instance, as in “Every phoenix is an animal.”

A further point follows: sciences use universal judgements extending over all possible members, not just the presently actual specimens.

### The opposing reply: truth need not mean an independent internal image

**Arabic**

> وأعترض بأنا لا نسلم أن الإيجاب يقتضي وجود الموضوع ... بل اللازم هو تميز الموضوع والمحمول عند العقل بمعنى تصورهما.

**English translation**

An objection is raised: we do not concede that affirmative predication requires the actual being of the subject. Rather, it requires only that the subject and predicate be distinguished in the intellect, through their conception.

The objection then charges the advocate of mental existence with **assuming what is disputed**: if one argues that understanding a thing proves it is present as a form in the mind, one is already assuming the very theory under dispute.

**Arabic — p.78**

> وهذا نفس المتنازع لأن القول بكون التعقل بالحصول في العقل إنما هو رأي القائلين بالوجود الذهني.

**English translation**

That is precisely what is in dispute: saying that intellection consists in something's obtaining within the intellect is already the view of those who affirm mental existence.

### The response and its limit

The main response insists that intellectual apprehension requires a relation between knower and known and that pure nonbeing cannot serve as a term of such a relation. Since the known may lack extramental being, its suitable mode of establishment must be **mental**.

But p.79 then raises competing hypotheses: perhaps forms exist in **Platonic separated Ideas**, or are present in a separate **Active Intellect**, instead of being internally actual in the human subject. These possibilities must be addressed rather than silently excluded.

## V. Three objections against mental existence — *Maqāṣid* p.79

The later part of p.79 presents **three very precise objections**.

### 1. If heat is in intellect, does the intellect become hot?

**Arabic**

> لو كان تصور الشيء مستلزماً لحصوله في العقل لزم من تصور الحرارة والبرودة أن يكون الذهن حاراً وبارداً.

**English translation**

If conceiving a thing meant its obtaining in the intellect, conceiving heat and cold would make the intellect **hot and cold**.

### 2. If heaven is understood, must the whole immense cosmos be physically inside the mind?

**Arabic**

> أنه يلزم أن تحصل السماوات بعظمها في العقل عند تعقلها الكل وفي الخيال عند تخيلها.

**English translation**

It would follow that the vast heavens themselves obtain inside the intellect when the intellect knows them, or inside the imagination when they are imagined.

### 3. Does a nonexistent thing become extramentally real simply because the mind is physically real?

**Arabic**

> أنه يلزم من تعقل المعدومات وجودها في الخارج لكونها موجودة في العقل الموجود في الخارج.

**English translation**

It would follow that nonexistent things become extramentally existent when we understand them, because they would be inside an intellect that itself exists outside the mind.

### The primary reply: differentiate a thing's real *identity* from its intellectual *form*

**Arabic — p.79**

> والجواب أن مبنى الكل على عدم التفرقة بين الوجود المتأصل الذي به الهوية العينية وغير المتأصل الذي به الصورة العقلية.

**English translation**

The answer is that all three objections fail to distinguish **rooted or proper existence**, through which a thing has its real extramental identity, from the non-primary mode of existence proper to its **intelligible form**.

**Arabic**

> وبالجملة فماهية الشيء أعني صورته العقلية مخالفة لهويته العينية في كثير من اللوازم.

**English translation**

In short, the quiddity of a thing understood as its intellectual form differs from its **real external individual identity** in many of their concomitants.

This is not the thesis that knowing a horse forces a physical horse into one's brain. It is a differentiation of **what is signified**, **the intellectual form**, and **the body's external actuality**.

### A genuine historical route to Lāhījī

Lāhījī *Shawāriq* I:50–52 does not suddenly invent the distinction between an intellectual form and external reality. The *Maqāṣid* had already stated the core objection and reply in a scholastic form. Lāhījī develops additional distinctions concerning the **particular psychic actuality** of the knowing act, the represented object's categorical nature, and its mode of intentional existence.

The question for our historical narrative is thus not “who first invented mental existence?” but **how the commentators refined the semantic and ontological relation between a mental representation, its content, and the actual known thing**.

## VI. Connections to the four causes and the AT comparison

**Natural philosophy:** The *Mawāqif* passage tests Avicennian matter/form theory at the level of its most fundamental **proof**, not just its terminology. An AT philosopher defending prime matter must respond to the trilemma without treating matter as a tiny hidden spatial body.

**Epistemology:** The *Maqāṣid* passage tests the **intelligible representation** without turning it into an extramental body or a mere arbitrary mental name. Aquinas's distinction between the *species intelligibilis* as that **by which** one knows and the actual object **known** provides a useful contrast.

**Method:** Both text families apply a common scholastic discipline: distinguish senses before deciding whether an objection defeats a doctrine. A modern synthesis should retain this **method of determination**, including its technical distinctions, rather than treat medieval kalām as a set of anti-Peripatetic slogans.
