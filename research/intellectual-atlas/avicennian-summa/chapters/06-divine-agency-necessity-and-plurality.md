# Book IV, Chapter 1 — The First's agency: necessity, will, mediation and cosmic plurality
**Avicenna's divine simplicity and emanation confronted with Aquinas's divine agency, freedom and multiple effects**

**Edition status:** Source-located direct Arabic/Latin comparative translation of (a) *Ilāhiyyāt al-Shifāʾ* IX.1 p.373 and IX.4 p.402, and (b) Aquinas, *De potentia* III.16. Printed edition variants from the *Shifāʾ* are identified where relevant; no manuscript witness was inspected independently. Not a continuous complete translation of either IX.1 or IX.4, nor of Aquinas's 16th article. Lāhījī and Narāqī are included only where their already-translated argument informs the synthesis, not falsely as direct participants in this particular dispute.

**Primary witnesses:** [Ibn Sīnā, *Ilāhiyyāt* IX.1 p.373](https://books.rafed.net/view/1128/page/373), [IX.4 p.402](https://books.rafed.net/view/1128/page/402); [Thomas Aquinas, *De potentia* III.16, corpus](https://www.corpusthomisticum.org/qdp3.html), especially textual markers [59134] and [59135].

## I. Two arguments that must not be conflated

**A. Modal thesis:** If a complete cause is actually in place and lacks no condition, its effect follows; a possible effect is necessary **through another** while remaining possible in itself. Lāhījī's *Shawāriq* I:88 and Narāqī's *Jāmiʿ* I:38–42 help refine this distinction.

**B. Divine-agency thesis:** Whether the First **necessarily** determines this very cosmos and its intermediary intellects, or produces one of several possible cosmic orders through knowing and willing, is **not decided** by (A). Otherwise the causal modal proposition is being made to carry an additional cosmological and theological claim.

Aquinas's *De potentia* III.16 criticises Avicenna principally at the second level, not by denying that effects of actually complete causes have intelligible explanations.

## II. Avicenna: divine simplicity and the permanent effect

**Arabic — *Ilāhiyyāt* IX.1, printed p.373, selected connected text**

> فقد ظهر لنا أن للكل مبدأ واجب الوجود، غير داخل في جنس أو واقع تحت حد أو برهان، بريء عن الكم والكيف والماهية والأين والمتى والحركة، لا ند له ولا شريك له ولا ضد له، وأنه واحد من جميع الوجوه ... وقد بان لك بعد ذلك أن واجب الوجود بذاته واجب الوجود من جميع جهاته، وأنه لا يجوز أن تستأنف له حالة لم تكن، مع أنه قد بان لك أن العلة لذاتها تكون موجبة المعلول، فإن دامت أوجبت المعلول دائماً.

**Translation**

It has become clear that the whole has a principle that is **necessary in existence**, which falls under no genus, is subject to no definition or demonstration [of its essence], and is free of quantity, quality, quiddity, place, time and motion. It has neither equal nor partner nor opposite; it is one in every respect ...

It has further become clear that the Necessary Existent through itself is necessary in every respect and cannot begin to have a state that it previously lacked; and it has been established that a cause through its very essence **necessitates its effect**—if the cause endures, it necessitates its effect continually.

**Critical note.** This connects divine immutability with the thesis of the complete cause's effect. It should not be dismissed as the assertion “God is a natural mechanism”; Ibn Sīnā also insists on divine knowing. But it is a genuine modal commitment whose implications for cosmic origination differ from those of the Thomistic freedom thesis.

### Printed variants on p.373

- In the lemma «في صفة فاعلية المبدأ الأول», the phrase «فاعلية» is reported as «ما عليه» in ب، ح، د. This changes the transmitted title or description and is editorially significant, though not conclusive about doctrine.
- In «فإن دامت أوجبت المعلول دائما», the clause is found in the printed base reading; no substantive variant is reported there in the displayed apparatus.

**Editorial boundary:** The ellipsis marks intervening claims about the First's unity and the first mover. The whole of IX.1 has not been reproduced.

## III. Avicenna: neither purposive choice like ours nor blind nature

**Arabic — IX.4, p.402**

> قد صح لنا فيما قدمناه من القول أن الواجب الوجود بذاته واحد، وأنه ليس بجسم ولا في جسم ولا منقسم بوجه من الوجوه، فإذن الموجودات كلها وجودها عنه ... فلهذا لا يجوز أن يكون كون الكل عنه على سبيل قصد منه كقصدنا لتكوين الكل ولوجود الكل فيكون قاصدا لأجل شيء غيره ... وليس كون الكل عنه على سبيل الطبع بأن يكون وجود الكل عنه لا بمعرفة ولا لرضى منه، وكيف يصح هذا وهو عقل محض يعقل ذاته؟

**Translation**

It has been established that the Necessary Existent through itself is one, neither a body nor located in a body, and in no respect divisible. It follows that the being of all existents is from it. It cannot be the cause of their existence in the manner of **deliberation like our own**, intending the production of the cosmos for the sake of something other than itself. Yet the whole does **not** proceed from it as a **blind natural process**, without knowledge or satisfaction: how could that be, when the First is pure intellect understanding itself?

### Why this is a more difficult position than the textbook formula suggests

Ibn Sīnā excludes two **different** inadequate accounts:

1. **Human purposive deliberation** that seeks an additional benefit outside the perfect First; such benefit would imply something the First lacked.
2. **Unknowing natural production** that would contradict the First's pure intellectual self-knowledge.

The First's agency proceeds through intellectual understanding without a temporal transition into intending or realising a new purpose. One cannot responsibly say “Avicenna's First has no knowledge of effects,” because the quoted text expressly rejects unknowing operation. Equally, one must not assume that “the First knows what proceeds” establishes *libertarian alternative possibilities* in the Thomistic sense.

**Printed variant warning:** The same passage reports “لا بمعرفة” versus a variant “لمعرفة” in د. Such a change alters the local positive/negative wording, and cannot be ignored when discussing knowledgeable production. The running text contrasts action “without knowledge” with the First's *actual* intellect; the surrounding argumentative grammar governs the reading. A further variant «ولا لرضى» / «رضى» is recorded. The text should be checked against the edition's witness key before privileging one interpretation of divine approval.

## IV. Aquinas's reconstruction and criticism: the First's simplicity does not impose a single first effect

**Latin — *De potentia* III.16**

> Alii pluralitatem rerum et modos diversos earum ex necessitate causae efficientis ponebant, sicut Avicenna, et eius sequaces. Posuit enim quod primum ens, in quantum intelligit se ipsum, producit unum tantum causatum, quod est intelligentia prima.

**Translation**

Others, such as Avicenna and his followers, placed the plurality of things and their diverse modes of being under the **necessity of the efficient cause**. He held that the First Being, insofar as it understands itself, produces only **one immediate effect**, namely the first intelligence.

This is Aquinas's report of Avicenna's cosmological inference, not a verbatim quotation from Ibn Sīnā. The correct comparison pairs it with Ibn Sīnā's IX.4 and the explicit elaboration of separate intellects, not just with the general doctrine of causes.

### Aquinas's first objection: God is not limited to one effect

**Latin — *De potentia* III.16**

> Sed haec etiam positio stare non potest. Primo, quia ponit potentiam divinam terminari ad unum effectum, qui est intelligentia prima.

**Translation**

This position cannot stand: first, because it makes the divine power terminate in **only one effect**, the first intelligence.

### The second objection: intermediary substances are not creators in their own right

**Latin**

> Secundo, quia ponit alias substantias praeter Deum esse aliarum creatrices, quod esse impossibile in alia quaestione, art. 4, ostensum est.

**Translation**

Second, because it holds that substances other than God are **creators** of other substances; but we showed in article 4 that this is impossible.

This is an important distinction between **secondary causal agency** and **creation of existence absolutely**. Aquinas is not arguing that no angel or intelligence can affect another thing; he denies that a created intellect can be an independent source of creative power.

### The third objection: rational order rather than restriction by natural necessity

**Latin**

> Sequitur etiam ad hanc positionem ... quod decor ordinis universi sit casualis, ex quo rerum diversitatem non adscribit intentioni finis, sed terminationi potentiarum activarum ad suos effectus.

**Translation**

It also follows from this position, Aquinas says, that the beauty of the universe's order becomes **accidental**, since the diversity of things is not attributed to an intention ordered to an end, but merely to the restriction of productive powers to their effects.

**A possible challenge to Aquinas:** Avicenna certainly recognises final causality, knowledge, cosmic hierarchy, and providence. Aquinas's criticism therefore cannot be taken as a historical demonstration that Avicenna simply **denied all finality**. Its precise force is whether the **diversity of this very cosmos** is intended as an end through unconstrained divine wisdom or instead follows necessarily from the degrees and limitations of emanated causes. The strongest formulation must engage Avicenna's account of *ʿināya* rather than an artificially stripped-down Avicennian cosmology.

## V. Aquinas's positive account: divine wisdom, many effects, no natural necessity

**Latin — *De potentia* III.16**

> Quod enim Deus tale universum constituere voluerit, non est necessarium neque debitum, neque ex fine neque ex potentia efficientis, neque materiae ... Sed supposito quod tale universum producere voluerit, necessarium fuit quod tales et tales creaturas produxerit ...

**Translation**

That God should will to establish **this particular cosmos** is neither necessary nor due to an end, to the power of the efficient cause, or to matter. **But given that God wills** to produce a cosmos of this kind, it becomes necessary for him to produce the particular creatures from which its intended constitution follows.

Here Aquinas himself draws the essential modal distinction:

- **Absolute necessity:** God had to produce this world.
- **Conditional necessity:** if God has freely willed *this* world, then its required constituents and ordering follow.

The latter does not imply the former, and therefore the Thomistic account can affirm **necessary connections within a freely chosen causal order**.

### Multiple things can proceed from one simple First

**Latin**

> Sic igitur dicendum est quod ab uno primo multitudo et diversitas creaturarum processit ... ex ordine sapientiae, ut in diversitate creaturarum perfectio consisteret universi.

**Translation**

Therefore we must say that from the one First Principle arose the plurality and diversity of creatures—not through material necessity or the limitation of productive power, but through the **ordering of wisdom**, so that the perfection of the cosmos should consist in the diversity of creatures.

**Unification and division:** The First is absolutely simple; the many dependent effects do not constitute parts inside the First. The First can know diverse creatures and will their contingent existence without acquiring a new internal accident.

## VI. The precise AT–Avicennian differences

| Issue | Avicennian text | Thomistic text | What can already be established |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simplicity of the First | Absolutely simple and immutable | Absolutely simple and immutable | Real common commitment |
| Knowledge of dependent order | First knows itself as principle; unknowing production rejected | Effects ordered by divine wisdom and will | Agreement that agency is not blind; differences over willing |
| Necessary causal dependence | Complete enduring cause necessitates effect | Within freely willed cosmic order, necessary conditions follow | Important shared modal tools, distinct global inference |
| Immediate plurality | Cosmology proceeds by mediation from a first separate intelligence | The First can immediately cause many without division | Real disagreement over necessary mediation |
| Creative agency | Intelligence hierarchy has genuinely causal roles in cosmic production | Only God can create absolutely; intermediaries may act subordinately | Important difference needing qualified comparison |
| Cosmic teleology | Order of being/providence through intellects | Diversity intended through wisdom and goodness | Both have teleology, differently grounded |
| Free alternative worlds | IX.4's rejection of deliberation does not establish Thomistic alternative-willing | This cosmos was not absolutely necessary | Real disagreement or at least unresolved reconciliation |

## VII. What would make a synthesis defensible?

A mature comparative system must distinguish **four meanings of necessity**:

1. Necessary in itself (*wājib bi-l-dhāt*).
2. Necessary through an actually complete cause (*wājib bi-l-ghayr*).
3. Necessary given the First's free willing of a particular order (*ex suppositione*).
4. Necessary by an unfree production of one effect from divine nature.

Confusing (2) with (4) makes Avicenna appear to deny freedom by a merely formal inference. Confusing (3) with (4) makes Aquinas appear equally necessitarian. The real disagreement concerns what additional **principle of cosmic specification**—knowledge and will, or intrinsic causal emanation—explains why the **particular** cosmic order obtains.

A possible reconciliation can preserve Avicenna's layered causes and account of necessary *dependencies* while replacing the claim that the **First can only immediately produce one intelligence** with a Thomistic account of freely intended plurality. But this is a **new proposed synthesis**, not the doctrine already found in one historical thinker. The precise ramifications for the Giver of Forms, intellective cognition and divine science must then be reworked.

## VIII. Remaining primary-text obligations

- *Ilāhiyyāt* IX.4 beyond p.402 and IX.3 on the proceeding intellects; IX.6 on providence and evil.
- Rāzī and Ṭūsī on divine willing, “from one only one proceeds,” and whether causal necessity implies determinism.
- Taftāzānī, Jurjānī and Lāhījī's *Shawāriq* on free choice, divine knowledge and contingent events.
- Narāqī's *Jāmiʿ al-afkār* on particular cognition, will and eternity, beyond the already-translated contingency passage.
- Collation of physical manuscript witnesses and divergent prints for IX.4 variants (*maʿrifa*, *riḍā*).
- Aquinas, *De potentia* III.15 (the divine will) and his broader *SCG* and *ST* discussions of creaturely agency.
