# Book IV, Chapter 2 — God, being, unity and plurality in Mahdī al-Narāqī
**A critical translation of a decisive post-Sadrian argument from *Jāmiʿ al-afkār wa-nāqid al-anẓār* II:165–167**

**Textual status:** A complete translation of the **bounded two-interpretation and response unit** from the latter part of II:166 to II:167, with its introductory quoted mystical argument from II:165–166 as selected excerpts. The Arabic base is printed below, separated by authorial voice. This is a translation and printed-text comparison, **not a fresh collation of manuscript witnesses**. The modern digital transcription includes artefacts marking page/copy breaks (e.g. DB, MA); they are removed from the normalized quotation without silently treating them as words of Narāqī.

**Source:** [*Jāmiʿ al-afkār* vol. II, p.165](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/9228/171?lang=ar), [p.166](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/9228/172?lang=ar), [p.167](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/9228/173?lang=ar).

## 1. The preceding claim is *reported*—not yet Narāqī's conclusion

Narāqī surveys the philosophical literature on the First's knowledge of existing things. He reports a statement from later interpreters that all the perfections of existents are included within the First's absolutely simple perfection and that the absolutely simple reality can therefore be called “all things.”

**Arabic, II:165 — quoted/reported argument**

> وبيانه ـ على ما ذكره بعض العرفاء المحققين ـ هو: أن كل ما هو بسيط الحقيقة فهو بوحدته كل الأشياء، ولا يخرج عنه شيء منها إلا ما هو من باب النقائص والأعدام والإمكانيات.

**Translation**

Its explanation—as stated by some accomplished mystical philosophers—is that whatever is absolutely simple in reality is, in its very unity, **all things**. Nothing is excluded from it except what belongs to deficiencies, privations and possibilities.

The argument contends that if the subject of a true negation were absolutely simple, it could not be excluded from any positive perfection without thereby possessing a privative determination or a division in itself. It concludes that the absolutely simple First encompasses all positive existential perfections and therefore knows them supremely.

**Arabic — source, II:165**

> فيثبت أن البسيط كل الموجودات من حيث الوجود والتمام لا من حيث النقائص والأعدام ولهذا ثبت علمه بالموجودات علماً بسيطاً وحضورها عنده على وجه أعلى وأتم.

**Translation**

It follows that the absolutely simple reality is all existents **in respect of being and perfection**, not in respect of deficiency and privation. For this reason its knowledge of existents is a simple knowledge, and they are present to it in a higher and more perfect manner.

**Voice warning:** The author here reports an argument **from other metaphysicians and mystics**. The next step is his analysis of whether their formulation entails numerical identity of God and creatures. A reader who quotes only the bold statement “the simple is all things” and labels it Narāqī's unqualified position will reverse the conclusion of the passage.

## 2. The stronger statement of mystical unity — II:166

Narāqī quotes or paraphrases the reported account of the First as pure existence, from which contingent things' existence and perfections emanate, and notes that privations and limitations belong to finite beings.

**Arabic — reported position**

> وتوضيح ذلك: أن واجب الوجود لما كان عين حقيقة الوجود بحيث لا يشوبه نقص ولا عدم ولا قصور ولا ماهية ولا تركيب بوجه من الوجوه، بل هو صرف الوجود ومحض الخيرية وعين الكمال، وكل وجود أو كمال وجود إنما هو يفيض عنه ويرشح منه ...

**Translation**

The explanation is that the Necessary Existent is the very reality of existence, unmixed with deficiency, privation, limitation, quiddity or composition in any respect; rather, it is pure existence, pure goodness and perfection itself; and every existence and every existential perfection flows and emanates from it ...

**Arabic — last words of reported account**

> وذلك هو التوحيد الوجودي فاعرفه إن كنت أهلاً لذلك!؛ انتهى.

**Translation**

“That is existential unity [*al-tawḥīd al-wujūdī*]; understand it if you are qualified to do so.” **[End of the reported account.]**

The terminal «انتهى» explicitly closes the reported statement. The **subsequent** position is Narāqī's critical response.

## 3. Narāqī's two interpretations — complete translated argument

### Arabic base — II:166–167

> وغير خفي أن ما ذكره بعض العرفاء يحتمل وجهين: أحدهما أن الواجب لما كان صرف الوجود وتأكده ووجودات الأشياء وجودات مشوبة بالقوة والعدم فائضة منه، وهذه الوجودات المشوبة بالعدم إذا فرض تحليلها إلى وجودات صرفة وعدماتها يكون وجود الواجب مشتملاً على تلك الوجودات الصرفة.
>
> يعني أن وجوده لما كان فوق التمام فيشتمل على كل مرتبة دونه مع الزيادة من غير أن يكون عين سائر المراتب، بمعنى أنه إذا حلل الأشياء الموجودة إلى وجوداتها الصرفة وأعدامها كان وجودها عين الواجب. وذلك كما أن السواد الأشد يشتمل على جميع السوادات التي تحته مع الزيادة من غير أن يكون الأشد عين سائر المراتب؛ فإذا قلنا أن السواد الأشد كل السوادات، يكون المراد أن كل مرتبة دون الأشد يوجد في الأشد مع الزيادة، لا أن يكون الأشد عين باقي المراتب.
>
> وثانيهما أن يكون المراد أن وجود الواجب هو عين وجودات الممكنات إذا خلصت عن أعدامها. وأنت خبير بأن مبنى الوجه الأول على كون الوجود مقولاً بالتشكيك، وكون وجود الواجب فرداً أقوى وأشد منه؛ ومبنى الثاني على وحدة الوجود بل الموجود؛ والقول بكل منهما يشتمل على مفاسد، وقد بينا ذلك بما لا مزيد عليه في رسالتنا المسماة «قرة العيون».
>
> فالحق أن مراد المعلمين من أن الواجب كل الوجود مع وحدته البسيطة الصرفة: أن جميع الوجودات لوازم ذاته ورشحات وجوده وهو علة تامة لها، وهي ناشئة صادرة عنها، وهو كل الأشياء في الشهود العلمي بمعنى أن حضور ذاته عند ذاته هو حضور الأشياء، بل هو في انكشاف الأشياء أقوى من حضورها بأنفسها.
>
> فمن قال أن الأشياء عين الواجب أراد بالعينية العينية باعتبار الشهود العلمي وباعتبار ملزوميته وعليته لها وانطوائها فيه إجمالاً، وهذا المعنى هو مرادهم من قولهم إنه مجمل الأشياء أو كلها، لا أن تكون تلك الوجودات عين وجود الواجب بعد تخليصها عن أعدامها الإمكانية أو يكون وجود الواجب مشتملاً عليها مع الزيادة؛ لأنه إن تحقق حينئذ بين وجود الواجب وسائر الوجودات ما به الاشتراك وما به الامتياز لزم التركيب؛ وإن لم يكن بينهما ما به الامتياز أصلاً لزم وحدة الوجود؛ وإن لم يكن بينهما ما به الاشتراك بل كانا متخالفين بأنفسهما لم يكن لاشتمال وجود الواجب على سائر الوجودات معنى.

### English — connected translation

It is clear that the statement reported from certain mystical philosophers admits **two interpretations**.

The first is that, because the Necessary Existent is **pure and intensified existence**, whereas the existences of things are mixed with potentiality and privation and flow from it, if those existences were analytically separated into **pure existence** and their privations, the existence of the Necessary Existent would encompass their pure existences.

That is to say: because its existence transcends all ordinary completeness, it encompasses every lower degree **with a surplus of perfection**, without being numerically identical to those lower degrees. On this reading, were actual things analytically divided into their pure existences and their privations, their existential perfection would be identical [in the relevant respect] with that of the Necessary Existent. This resembles the way the most intense black contains all lesser degrees of blackness with an additional intensity, without being numerically identical to each weaker shade. Thus, if one says that the most intense black is “all blackness,” the meaning is that every lower degree is included within the more intense shade **plus something further**, not that the most intense shade is literally each of the others.

The second interpretation is that the existence of the Necessary Existent is **numerically identical with the existences of contingent things**, once their privations are removed.

You should recognise that the first interpretation rests upon the **gradation of existence** (*tashkīk al-wujūd*) and upon the Necessary Existent being its most intense and strongest instance. The second rests upon the **unity of existence**, indeed the unity of the **existent**. Each of these two positions, Narāqī says, entails serious difficulties, which he has explained at length in his treatise entitled ***Qurrat al-ʿUyūn***.

The correct interpretation of the teachers' claim that the Necessary Existent is “all existence” while remaining absolutely simple and one is this: **all existences are necessary concomitants of its essence and emanations of its existence**. It is their complete cause, and they proceed from it. It is “all things” in the order of **intellectual witnessing or divine knowledge**, meaning that its own self-presence to itself is also the presence of things; indeed, its disclosure of things is more complete than their presence to themselves.

Thus, anyone who says that “things are identical with the Necessary Existent” must mean **identity with respect to the intelligible witnessing in which they are known**, and with respect to the First's being their necessary ground and complete cause, in whose perfection they are contained **in an undifferentiated way**. That is what is meant by saying that the First **comprehends** all things or **is all things**.

It does **not** mean that those finite existences become numerically identical to the Necessary Existent's existence when stripped of their contingent privations, nor that the existence of the Necessary Existent contains them simply as degrees plus a greater degree.

For if one posited between the Necessary Existent and the other existences **something shared** alongside **something that differentiates them**, this would entail **composition** in the First. If there were **nothing whatsoever** differentiating them, one would collapse into numerical **unity of existence**. But if there were no common respect at all and the two were utterly unrelated in themselves, then the assertion that the Necessary Existent **encompasses** other existences would lose its meaning.

**Editorial note:** The English qualification “[in the relevant respect]” in the first interpretation flags the tension in Narāqī's original compressed exposition: he reports “identity” in the reconstructed argument yet uses a graded-black analogy to deny numerical identity of levels. It is not a doctrinal correction silently put into his mouth.

## 4. Three competing ontologies, with their real costs

| Interpretation | Strength | Narāqī's objection | Further philosophical task |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Graded existential participation**: God is strongest instance of one existential reality | Explains participation, the recurrence of perfections and analogical unity | Shared principle plus differentiating degree may introduce complexity into the absolutely simple First | Can analogy avoid treating divine existence as a species within a genus or scale? |
| **Numerical monism**: purified finite existence is God's own existence | Radical explanation of dependent being and divine immanence | Distinction between the Necessary and contingent existents collapses | Can real plurality and independent substantial natures survive? |
| **Causal and epistemic containment**: all finite existences proceed from and are known through the First | Preserves derivative reality and First's uncompounded perfection | Must show how the First is present to all without a multiplicity of intrinsic representations | Requires theory of divine knowledge, causality and analogy |

The remarkable point is Narāqī's refusal simply to accept the first choice because it seems less monistic. He worries that **gradation itself** may make God's existence an instance within a larger shared ontological class, risking composition; this is a different pressure from a straightforward objection to mystical monism.

## 5. Thomistic parallels and differences

Compare Aquinas, [*De potentia* III.16, ad 3–4 and ad 6](https://www.corpusthomisticum.org/qdp3.html), on the one and the many, the distinct kinds of unity, and the ability of the one First to cause differentiated beings.

### A. Causal containment is not numerical identity

Aquinas says the First causes all beings and their diverse modes because **the source of being as being** cannot leave the determinations of being wholly unexplained. This resembles Narāqī's refusal to dissolve derivative existents into the existence of their source.

### B. Participation is neither a genus nor simple univocity

The standard AT approach is that finite beings **participate in being** and God is not a generic maximal case within the same genus. It therefore attempts to avoid the argument that graded existence forces God into a common externally differentiated species.

This is promising but not identical to Narāqī's account. His positive reading stresses **scientific/intellectual witnessing** (*shuhūd ʿilmī*) and the necessary emanation of all creatures as concomitants of God's self-presence. Aquinas stresses divine knowledge and willing without the claim that the First is cosmologically necessitated to produce this actual order.

### C. Divine knowledge without real multiplicity

Narāqī interprets “all things are present in the First” as the First's **knowing itself**, whose perfection contains the ground of all things. Aquinas likewise holds that God's knowledge of things is not a collection of accidental representations subsequently acquired. But the mode of creaturely dependence and the necessity or freedom of cosmic production remain contested.

### D. Why “one existence” is an ambiguous phrase

A mature summa should state exactly which of these is being asserted:

- **one intelligible meaning of existence** (conceptual unity);
- **graded participation in being** (analogical/metaphysical structure);
- **one source of all existence** (causal unity);
- **one existential act numerically identical in all things** (strong monism);
- **all existents known at once in God's simple self-knowledge** (epistemic unity).

Only the fourth is directly a claim of **numerical identity** of all contingent existence with divine existence. Narāqī endorses the third and fifth here while rejecting the simplistic formulations of the second and fourth.

## 6. A historical correction to the previous GNAMMA research

The earlier statement “Narāqī rejects *waḥdat al-wujūd*” is **too blunt**. He reports and interprets an **Akbarian/perfectionist** formula, distinguishes two metaphysical readings and rejects the corresponding strong claims, but then retains a powerful **causal and epistemic unity**. This is not merely an anti-mystical position.

Likewise, the fact that a contemporary modern historian calls his position “transformed Avicennism” does not imply wholesale fidelity to the earlier Ibn Sīnā. At the level of divine knowledge, his *Jāmiʿ* exhibits a sophisticated post-Sadrian, Akbarian-aware philosophical problem even when rejecting some forms of ontological unity.

2. **Independent treatise:** locate and collate *Qurrat al-ʿUyūn*, which Narāqī explicitly identifies as having supplied the stronger objections. The title itself does not establish edition or manuscript witness.

5. **Ṣadrā:** distinguish gradation of existence from the “personal unity of being” in his mature synthesis and the role of intelligible emanation.
