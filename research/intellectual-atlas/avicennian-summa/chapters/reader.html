CRITICAL TEXT 01

Book I, Chapter 1 — Mental existence, quiddity, and the meaning of wujūd

A post-classical Avicennian argument in full, with a critical comparison to Thomistic intentionality

Editorial state: Translation of one complete bounded argument unit in Lāhījī's Shawāriq al-ilhām, vol. I, printed p. 52 (fifth question, through but not including the heading of the sixth). The associated mental-existence discussion from pp. 50–52 is represented by selected lemmata, not a complete translation. One digital print transcription has been checked; no handwritten witnesses or alternative editions have been collated.

Primary sources: Lāhījī I:50, I:51, I:52; Aquinas, Summa theologiae I q. 85 a. 2.

I. The problem

When the intellect understands a horse, is the horse's quiddity present in intellect, or only a distinct representation? How can a mental content representing a substance be an accidental quality in the knower? And is existence a separate property added to an already constituted quiddity?

These are connected but not identical questions. Lāhījī's solution turns on an exact differentiation between what is represented, the representation's mode of being in a knower, and the representation as an actual psychological event. His subsequent criticism of existence as a real adjunct to a prior quiddity prevents an overly literal use of the essence–existence distinction.

A. A substance known and the cognition as a quality

Arabic — Lāhījī I:50 (selected contiguous passage)

فهو من حيث هو معلوم بالذات وجوهر أي ماهية جوهر وكلي وموجود في الذهن، ومن حيث خصوص الوجود الذهني علم، ومن حيث القيام بالذهن الشخصي والمحفوفية بعوارض ذلك الذهن جزئي وعرض من الكيفيات النفسانية وموجود خارجي بالمعنى المقابل للوجود الظلي.

Translation

Considered in itself, it is something known primarily, a substance—that is, it has the quiddity of a substance—a universal, and something existing in the mind. Considered with regard to the specific mode of its mental existence, it is knowledge. Considered insofar as it inheres in a particular mind and is surrounded by that mind's individual conditions, it is a particular, an accident belonging to the psychological qualities, and an externally existing thing in the sense opposed to derivative or shadowy existence.

Critical note 1. Mawlūm bi-l-dhāt must not be collapsed into the accidentally cognised extramental individual, maʿlūm bi-l-ʿaraḍ. That the mental act is an accidental quality does not imply that the object signified thereby is a quality.

Critical note 2. The word "external" in the last clause means the act of cognition as an actual state of the really existing knower, not that the horse represented exists extramentally inside the mind. Lāhījī explicitly differentiates these descriptions.

B. The apparent contradiction is equivocation between respects

Arabic — I:50 (selected)

ولا منافاة بين كون الشيء جوهرا ذهنيا وعرضا خارجيا ... وإنما المنافاة بين كون الشيء جوهرا ذهنيا وعرضا ذهنيا أو بين كونه جوهرا خارجيا وعرضا خارجيا.

Translation

There is no contradiction in something being a mental substance and an extramental accident. What would be contradictory is its being both mental substance and mental accident, or both extramental substance and extramental accident, in the same consideration.

Here "mental substance" means that the content represents something whose extramental mode of being is not inherence in a subject; "extramental accident" means that the representing psychological state actually inheres in a knower.

C. The knowing image itself is actual in the knower

Arabic — I:51 (selected)

ومما يجب أن يعلم أن وجود الصورة العلمية في الذهن وقيامها به إنما هو وجود ذهني للمعلوم الذي هو ذو الصورة وليس هو وجودا ذهنيا لنفس هذه الصورة ... فإن الذهن أعني النفس الناطقة موجودة في الخارج وقد قامت بها تلك الصورة وترتبت على تلك الصورة آثارها ولوازمها.

Translation

One must understand that the existence of the cognitive form in the mind, and its inherence there, constitutes the mental existence of what is known—the thing represented by that form—not the mental existence of the form considered as an object in its own right. For the mind, that is, the rational soul, really exists outside the mind as an actual being; the cognitive form inheres in it, and the form's own effects and concomitants belong to it.

Source caveat. The sentence includes material omitted by the ellipsis; it has been broken out as an explanatory excerpt, not a continuous translation of every word on p. 51.

D. Why a likeness is not necessarily a substitute object

Lāhījī reports a customary distinction between ancient advocates of mental "images" resembling objects and later writers who speak of the quiddities themselves obtaining mentally. He argues that the word "shadow" can designate a quiddity's different mode of being, without implying a fundamentally different object merely matching some of its accidents.

Arabic — I:52 (selected)

فإن الحكم على شيء إنما يستدعي وجود ذلك الشيء وثبوته لا ثبوت أمر مغاير له وإن وافقه في بعض الأعراض.

Translation

For judging something requires the being or establishment of that very thing, not the establishment of something numerically or essentially different from it merely because the latter resembles it in certain accidents.

Limitation: This does not yet answer all problems of intentionality, nor settle whether the known nature is numerically identical across mental and external instantiations. The relevant Shifāʾ and logical discussions must be collated.

II. COMPLETE BOUNDED TRANSLATION: Shawāriq I:52, the fifth question

Boundary: from «المسألة الخامسة [في أن الوجود ليس أمرا ينضم إلى الماهية]» through the final sentence immediately before «المسألة السادسة». Arabic orthography and punctuation below are lightly normalised; the underlying online print transcription has not been collated.

Arabic base passage

المسألة الخامسة: في أن الوجود ليس أمراً ينضم إلى الماهية فيتحقق به الماهية، بل هو نفس تحقق الماهية وحصولها وكونها كما أشرنا إليه فيما سبق، وإليه أشار المصنف بقوله: «وليس الوجود معنى به يحصل الماهية في العين بل الحصول». وهذا ردّ لما ذهب إليه جماعة من أتباع المشّائين من كون الوجود صفة موجودة في الخارج منضمة إلى الماهية وكون الماهية موجودة بها؛ فعندهم المجعول بالذات والصادر من الجاعل بالحقيقة إنما هو الوجود، والماهية من حيث هي غير مجعولة ومن حيث الاتصاف بالوجود مجعولة بالعرض. وهذا المذهب بظاهره سخيف جداً، وقد أبطله شيخ الإشراق في كتاب الإشراق وغيره بما لا مزيد عليه. وحاصله أن ثبوت الصفة العينية للموصوف يتوقف على ثبوت الموصوف في العين، فيلزم كون الماهية موجودة في الخارج قبل وجودها في الخارج. وأيضاً لو كان الوجود موجوداً في الخارج فإما بوجود غيره فيلزم التسلسل في الوجودات، وإما بوجود هو نفسه فيلزم أن لا يكون حمل الموجود على الوجود وغيره بمعنى واحد، إذ مفهومه فيه أنه نفس الوجود وفي غيره أنه شيء له الوجود، والحال أنه لا يطلق على الجميع إلا بمعنى واحد. ومستندهم هو أن الماهية إن لم ينضم إليها أمر من الجاعل فهي على العدم. وجوابه أنكم فرضتم ماهية في الخارج ثم يضم الجاعل الوجود إليها، وهذا باطل؛ لأن الماهية إذا كانت أولاً فهي موجودة، إذ لا يفهم من الوجود إلا كون الماهية، فأي حاجة إلى ضم الوجود إليها؟ بل نفس الماهية إنما صدرت من الجاعل فكانت في الخارج، فكان كونها في الخارج بعد الصدور هو وجودها. والمصنف أشار بهذا الكلام إلى أنه لا حاجة في إبطال ذلك المذهب إلى هذه المؤونة، بل يكفي أن يقال: إنا لا نعني ولا نفهم من الوجود إلا كون الماهية وحصولها بالضرورة، لا أمراً ينضم إلى الماهية فتصير موجودة؛ فلو انضم إليها أمر من الجاعل لا يكون ذلك الأمر وجوداً بالمعنى المفهوم للكل. ولا ينبغي أن يتوهم المنافاة بين ما ذكر هاهنا وبين ما أشرنا إليه سابقاً وسيأتي تحقيقه من كون الوجود عند الحكماء ذا أفراد حقيقية، إذ ليست تلك الأفراد عندهم ما ينضم إلى الماهية فتصير موجودة به، بل إن هي إلا أكوان خاصة للماهيات المتخالفة، كل منها نفس كون ماهية مخصوصة بحسب الخارج، كما ستعرفه إن شاء الله تعالى.

English — complete translation of the argument unit

The fifth question: Existence is not something that attaches to a quiddity such that the quiddity comes to be established through it; rather, it is the very establishment, obtaining, and being of that quiddity.

We have already indicated this, and it is what the author of the base text signifies when he says: “Existence is not a meaning through which a quiddity obtains in extramental reality; rather, it is the obtaining itself.”

This replies to the position of a group among the followers of the Peripatetics: according to them, existence is a really existing attribute that is joined to a quiddity outside the mind, such that the quiddity becomes existent through it. On their account, what is made in itself, what genuinely proceeds from the productive cause, is existence; the quiddity, considered in itself, is not made, while considered as qualified by existence it is made incidentally. In its apparent sense this position is extremely unsatisfactory. The Master of Illumination has refuted it in the Ḥikmat al-ishrāq and elsewhere in a way that leaves little to add.

The substance of the refutation is as follows. For a real attribute to belong to a subject, that subject must already be established in reality. It would therefore follow that a quiddity is existent outside the mind before it exists there. Moreover, if existence itself were something existing outside the mind, either it would exist through another existence, in which case an infinite regress of existences would result; or it would exist through an existence identical with itself, in which case predicating “existent” of existence and of something else would not have the same meaning. For in the former case it would mean “existence itself,” and in the latter “something possessing existence,” whereas the expression is employed with one common meaning.

The ground invoked by advocates of that position is that a quiddity remains non-existent unless something coming from the productive cause is added to it. The reply is that you have first posited a quiddity outside the mind and then had the producer add existence to it. That is false: if the quiddity is already there in the first place, it is already existent. For by existence we understand nothing other than the quiddity's being; what need, then, would there be to adjoin existence to it? Rather, the quiddity itself proceeds from the productive cause and is thereby outside the mind; its being outside the mind upon proceeding from that cause is its existence.

The author of the base text indicates that this more elaborate effort is unnecessary for refuting the disputed position. It suffices to say that by existence we necessarily mean and understand nothing but the quiddity's being and obtaining, not something that is joined to it and thereby makes it existent. Even if something were adjoined to it by the producer, that addition would not be existence in the meaning commonly understood by everyone.

Nor should anyone imagine a contradiction between the present account and the view already mentioned, which we shall examine further, that for the philosophers existence has real instances. For those instances are not, on their account, things that join a quiddity and thereby make it exist. They are nothing other than particular modes of being of different quiddities, each being precisely the extramental actuality of a determinate quiddity, as you shall learn, God willing.

Critical notes on this translated unit

[E1] Attribution. The terse sentence introduced by «قال المصنف» is the Tajrīd lemma being expounded. The criticisms involving Suhrawardī, the explanatory regress, and the final reconciliation of particular existences are Lāhījī's commentary. They must not be merged into a single authorial voice.

[E2] Scope of criticism. The target is an extrinsically joined, independently actual property called existence. The argument does not simply deny that there can be a genuine metaphysical distinction between essence and esse, and it does not establish the Scotistic or Thomistic status of that distinction.

[E3] The apparent contradiction resolved. He denies “existence is a second actual item joined to a previously actual essence” while affirming “there are different particular existences.” That is consistent if the latter are the ways in which different things actually are, not a stock of detachable existence-properties.

[E4] What follows concerning Ṣadrā? Neither the primacy of existence (aṣālat al-wujūd), nor a single all-inclusive reality of existence, follows merely from this critique of the extrinsic-property account. A separate ontology is needed.

[E5] Editorial uncertainty. The online copy's transcription has occasional spelling and typographical irregularities. No manuscript variants are being reported or invented; the Arabic here is a normalised reading of that one printing.

III. The Thomistic comparison: species qua, not species quod

Aquinas distinguishes the intelligible form as that by which the intellect understands from the extramental nature as what the intellect primarily understands. He rejects the thesis that knowledge of nature is restricted to cognition of one's own mental representations. See Summa theologiae I q. 85 a. 2, Corpus Thomisticum, response and replies.

Latin lemma:

Species intelligibilis non est quod intelligitur actu, sed id quo intelligit intellectus.

Translation:

The intelligible species is not what is actually understood; rather, it is that by which the intellect understands.

This is not automatically equivalent to Lāhījī's distinction. The latter employs an elaborate system of “mental existence of what is known” and “external existence of the mental act,” whereas Aquinas's formal/objective distinction involves a theory of intentional assimilation, abstraction, and the act's term. The comparison must preserve these technical differences.

Argument graph

  1. The intellect can consider the quiddity of an extramental substance.
  2. The mental act is itself a particular quality belonging to a particular intellect.
  3. It is not a contradiction that the quiddity represented belongs to the category substance while the act representing it belongs to the category quality.
  4. Therefore the existence of a representation must not be mistaken for an extramental horse or other substance somehow lodged in the intellect.
  5. In the linked ontology, “existence” means the actual being/obtaining of a thing and need not be pictured as an additional actual item glued to it.
  6. Neither (1)–(5) proves the numerical identity of nature across instantiations, nor settles whether existence is really or only conceptually distinct from essence.
  7. Both the Avicennian and Thomistic accounts therefore require a further analysis of intentionality and metaphysical composition.

New result for the mature-summa project

Lāhījī is not simply a conservative repeater of a naive essence-plus-existence-as-accident theory. His text already integrates a theory of intentionality, the critique of object-substitution, differentiated existence, and a refined interpretation of making. The next complete argument unit, On what a cause makes, shows why that last topic cannot be detached from the foregoing distinctions.

Book I, Chapter 2 — Jaʿl: what does the cause actually make?

*Lāhījī, Shawāriq al-ilhām I:158–161; al-Taftāzānī, al-Ījī and al-Jurjānī; comparison with Ibn Sīnā and Aquinas*

Scholarly status: A connected critical translation of the central objection–reply sequence on printed pp. 158–161. All specifically translated Arabic extracts are identified below; the page contains additional technical discussion of the classification of simple and complex essences, which is not represented as exhaustively translated. Arabic checked against the named searchable print transcription only. No source-manuscript variants established.

Primary sources: Shawāriq I:158, I:159, I:160, I:161. The cited Sharḥ al-Maqāṣid and Sharḥ al-Mawāqif are embedded quotations reported by Lāhījī; they must be checked against independent editions before being represented as collated readings.

Thesis and the prior question

The dispute over whether quiddities are “made” often mixes up at least four questions:

  1. Does an existent thing owe its existence to a producer?
  2. Does a thing's quiddity count as the kind of quiddity it is only through an act of production?
  3. Does a complex quiddity have parts and thus a kind of internal dependence not shared by something simple?
  4. What is really one outside the mind but analysed into distinct concepts—particular existent, its quiddity, and its existence?

The disputants can agree on (1) while disagreeing about (2)–(4), or appear to disagree only because they use “made” differently.

I. Three reported positions — Shawāriq I:158

Arabic (the reported schools)

وكما يتحقق الحاجة إلى جاعل في المركب فكذا في البسيط. اعلم أن العقلاء بعد الاتفاق على أن وجود الممكن بالفاعل اختلفوا في ماهيته. قال شارح المقاصد: فذهب المتكلمون إلى أنها بجعل الجاعل مطلقاً، أي بسيطة كانت ومركبة، وذهب جمهور الفلاسفة والمعتزلة إلى أنها ليست بجعل الجاعل، بمعنى أن شيئاً منها ليس بمجعول، وذهب بعضهم إلى أن المركبات مجعولة دون البسائط.

Translation

Just as there is a need for a maker in the case of something composite, so there is in the case of something simple. Understand that, although those who employ reason agree that the existence of a contingent thing comes from an agent, they disagree about its quiddity. The commentator on the Maqāṣid [al-Taftāzānī] states: the theologians maintained that it comes about through the producer's making, whether simple or composite; the majority of the philosophers and the Muʿtazilites maintained that it does not come about through the producer's making, in the sense that none of it is made; and some maintained that composite quiddities are made but simple quiddities are not.

Editorial qualification: These are historical position ascriptions in this dispute, not a blanket agreement among all theologians or all Muʿtazila. The strong Muʿtazilī view about the nonexistent as a thing must be checked school by school.

A. The missing distinction: simple and composite making

Arabic

وأما وجه ذهاب الفلاسفة إليه فهو أنهم نفوا الجعل المركب، أعني جعل الماهية ماهية، على ما يدل عليه دليلهم كما يأتي. وأما الجعل البسيط، أعني كون الصادر عن الفاعل نفس الماهية ...

Translation

The reason for attributing that view to the philosophers is that they deny composite making: making a quiddity be that very quiddity, as their proof will indicate. As for simple making, meaning that the very quiddity is what proceeds from the agent ...

The passage continues on p. 159. The ellipsis above indicates omitted text, not a completed paragraph. The real distinction is between “making something exist” and “making humanity be humanity” or “making blackness be blackness.”

II. The first reported position: its three arguments and Lāhījī's replies — I:159

Objection 1: possibility is universal

Arabic

ثم إن مستند المذهب الأول أمور: الأول أن علة الحاجة هي الإمكان وهي عامة.

Translation

The first position rests on several considerations. First, the ground of causal dependence is possibility, which is common [to the simple and the composite].

Answer

والجواب عن الأول بأن معنى حاجة الممكن إلى الجاعل أن وجوده منه لا ماهيته.

The answer to the first is that the need of the contingent being for a producer means that its existence comes from that producer, not that its quiddity as such comes from him in the sense under dispute.

Objection 2: if the quiddity is unmade, making disappears altogether

Arabic

الثاني أنه لو لم يكن الماهية مجعولة ارتفع المجعولية؛ لأن ما فرض كونه مجعولاً من الوجود أو موصوفية الماهية به فهو أيضاً ماهيته في نفسه، والمفروض أن ما هو ماهية ليس بمجعول.

Translation

Second, if quiddity were not made, making as such would disappear. For whatever is assumed to be made—existence or the quiddity's being qualified by existence—also has a quiddity of its own, and the assumption is that whatever is a quiddity is not made.

Answer

وعن الثاني أن المجعول هو الوجود الخاص وما هو من أفراد الوجود، لا ماهية الوجود ومفهومه.

In reply to the second: what is made is particular existence, something that belongs to the instances of existence—not the quiddity or concept of existence.

Critical distinction: Lāhījī's reply permits particular actuality to be produced while rejecting the proposition that the meaning of existence has to be made a meaning. Compare Chapter 1 on why existence is not an independently actual property adjoined to an already actual quiddity.

Objection 3: without making, a quiddity would possess independent external establishment

Arabic

الثالث أنه لا تقرر للماهية في الخارج بذاتها، لما مر في نفي ثبوت المعدومات، فيكون تقررها بالجاعل.

Translation

Third, the quiddity has no independent establishment outside the mind, as already explained when denying the established reality of non-existent things. Its actual establishment must therefore come from a producer.

Answer

وعن الثالث أنه ليس تقرر الماهية إلا وجودها.

In reply to the third: the quiddity's external establishment is nothing other than its existence.

Result: His thesis does not posit a quiddity established outside the mind awaiting a separate act of existential attachment. It is precisely this possible misconception that must be removed in comparing him with Aquinas.

III. The opposing proof from essential identity — I:159

Arabic (reported proof and counter-reply)

ومستند المذهب الثاني أن كون الإنسان إنساناً مثلاً لو كان بجعل الجاعل لارتفع بارتفاعه فيلزم سلب الشيء عن نفسه على تقدير عدم الفاعل وهو محال. وأجابوا عنه بمنع استحالته على طريق السلب دون العدول. وردّ بأن السلب الصادق على الإنسان حال ارتفاعه هو أن المعدوم ليس بإنسان لا أن الإنسان ليس بإنسان، والحق أن استحالة سلب الشيء عن نفسه ضرورية.

Translation

The argument supporting the second position is that, if a human being's being human, for example, resulted from an agent's making it so, then it would cease to be human if that productive activity were removed. This would entail denying of a thing what it itself is, on the hypothesis of the agent's absence—which is impossible.

The reply offered to this says that there is no impossibility here when the denial is taken as negative predication rather than as the affirmation of an opposite property [salb as opposed to ʿudūl]. But this is answered as follows: the true negation when the human is absent is “the non-existent is not a human,” not “the human is not human.” The impossibility of denying of something what it itself is must be regarded as necessary.

Apparatus: The distinction between salb and ʿudūl is a technical logical distinction, not evidence that one can coherently predicate “not human” of an actually existing human. The objection guards the invariance of an essence's defining content under hypothetical non-instantiation.

IV. A third position: composites but not simple quiddities — I:159

Arabic

ومستند المذهب الثالث أن شرط المجعولية الإمكان وهو يستدعي نسبة وهي تستدعي اثنينية وهي غير متصورة في البسيط دون المركب. والجواب أن الاثنينية التي تستدعيها النسبة اللازمة للإمكان إنما هي باعتبار الماهية والوجود وهي شاملة للبسيط والمركب.

Translation

The third position rests on the claim that the condition of being made is possibility; possibility requires a relation, which in turn requires a duality, and such duality cannot be conceived in the simple, though it can be conceived in the composite.

The reply is that the duality required by the relation involved in possibility is the distinction in consideration between quiddity and existence. It applies to both the simple and the composite.

Key inference: Dependence is not restricted to internally composite essences; an externally dependent simple being can still be contingent. This matters when drawing analogies to separated intellects or incorporeal created substances.

V. Al-Taftāzānī: clarify the real subject of disagreement — I:159

Lāhījī quotes the commentator on the Maqāṣid. We reproduce the argument as reported in Lāhījī; independent collation against Sharḥ al-Maqāṣid is outstanding.

Arabic

فإنه معلوم أن ليس للفاعل تأثير وجعل بالنسبة إلى ماهية الممكن وآخر بالنسبة إلى الوجود حتى تكون الماهية مجعولة كالوجود، وأن ليس للماهية تقرر في الخارج بدون الفاعل حتى يكون المجعول هو الوجود فقط، بل أثر الفاعل مجعولية الماهية بمعنى صيرورتها موجودة.

Translation

For it is known that the agent does not exercise one act of influence and making with respect to the contingent being's quiddity and a second act with respect to its existence, such that the quiddity is made just as existence is made. Nor does the quiddity enjoy extramental establishment without the agent, such that only existence is made. Rather, the agent's effect is that the quiddity becomes actual—that is, it is made in the sense of coming to exist.

Critical insight: Taftāzānī does not present essence and existence as two separately manufactured external items. This is an important point of contact with an AT analysis of the composite's dependence on a cause.

VI. Al-Ījī and Jurjānī: two senses of dependence — I:159–160

Lāhījī next relays the author of the Mawāqif and the commentator on it. Do not attribute all the following distinctions directly to al-Ījī.

Arabic — reported in I:159

المجعولية قد يراد بها الاحتياج إلى الفاعل وقد يراد بها الاحتياج إلى الغير على ما يعم الجزء.

Translation

“Being made” may mean needing an efficient agent, or it may mean needing another in a broader sense that includes needing a constitutive part.

A simple contingent existent can need an efficient agent without being composed of internal parts; a composite quiddity additionally depends upon its constituents. Treating these as the same need would make the disagreement appear larger than it is.

*Arabic — I:160, reported Sharḥ al-Mawāqif***

والصواب أن يقال معنى قولهم الماهية ليست مجعولة أنها في حد ذاتها لا يتعلق بها جعل جاعل وتأثير مؤثر ... فلا مغايرة بين الماهية ونفسها حتى يتصور توسط جعل بينهما ...

Translation

The correct way to state the meaning of “the quiddity is not made” is that, taken in itself, it is not the terminus of a producer's making or a cause's influence. There is no difference between a quiddity and itself such that one could conceive an act of making that intervened between the two.

This does not mean the existent is independent of its creator. It means a causal act is not required to make what humanity means be humanity.

The dyer analogy — I:160

*Arabic — reported Sharḥ al-Mawāqif***

فإن الصباغ مثلاً إذا صبغ ثوباً فإنه لا يجعل الثوب ثوباً ولا الصبغ صبغاً بل يجعل الثوب متصفاً بالصبغ في الخارج ...

Translation

When a dyer dyes a garment, he does not make the garment be a garment, nor dye be dye; he makes the garment qualified by the dye outside the mind.

Lāhījī's criticism

أقول وهذا كلام لا غبار عليه إلا أنه ينبغي تحقيق معنى جعل الماهية متصفة بالوجود لئلا يتوهم أن الماهية والوجود كليهما حاصلان بلا جعل وأثر الجاعل أنه يجعل أحدهما متصفاً بالآخر.

I say: this is a sound account, except that we must clarify what is meant by making a quiddity qualified by existence, lest someone imagine that quiddity and existence are both already actual without being made and that the producer's effect is merely to join one to the other.

The dyer analogy is pedagogical, not an ontological model of two already established substances being combined. That further clarification is Lāhījī's central corrective.

VII. Lāhījī's own adjudication — I:161

Arabic

بل المراد أن الصادر من الجاعل إنما هو أمر واحد يحلله العقل إلى أمرين ويجعل أحدهما متصفاً بالآخر، فهذا هو معنى جعل الماهية متصفة بالوجود كما مر غير مرة.

Translation

Rather, the intention is that what proceeds from the producer is one thing, which the intellect analyses into two considerations and regards one as qualified by the other. That is the meaning of making a quiddity qualified by existence, as has already been explained more than once.

Arabic

Translation

I add that this account, too, removes the disagreement: no meaning remains that would properly support a dispute. The correct course, as we indicated, is to locate the disagreement in composite making of the quiddity while abstracting from existence. For the need of a producer in respect of existence is admitted on all sides, as has been shown, while the simple making of a quiddity is a subtle matter not understood by most.

He goes on to explain why some theologians, unfamiliar with simple making, treated making as composite; others, notably those who accepted pre-existential quiddities, denied the need to make those quiddities; philosophers denied the same compound making as unintelligible; and advocates of a simple/composite distinction reasoned from the imagined internal division of a composite. These are Lāhījī's explanatory diagnoses of the schools, not independent verification of what every author held.

Arabic — concluding interpretation

فالماهيات الممكنة سواء كانت مركبة أو بسيطة محتاجة إليه على السواء ومجعولة بالجعل البسيط بالمعنى الذي عرفت.

Translation

Contingent quiddities, whether composite or simple, need the producer equally and are made through simple making in the sense already explained.

The major repair to the prior argument

The productive act does not connect two already actual parts called "essence" and "existence." It makes one particular existent, which the intellect legitimately considers under both the terms quiddity and existence. This is why Lāhījī's earlier claim that particular existence is what is made is consistent with rejecting a separately joined existential attribute.

This is a real post-classical argumentative refinement: it integrates the ontological problem with the disputed meanings of jaʿl, the logic of essential identity, and the three theological schools' senses of dependence.

VIII. Where this places the AT comparison

Aquinas, in De potentia III.8, says that what is produced in natural generation per se is the composite, while the form is that through whose acquisition something becomes actual; he denies that a natural substantial form must be individually created as an independent item.

This is not an identity of doctrines with Lāhījī. Yet both insist on a useful principle:

Do not multiply independently existing objects simply because an adequate explanation distinguishes several metaphysical principles.

The points requiring independent investigation are:

  • Does the maker confer an act of existence really distinct from essence in Aquinas's sense, or is the essence–existence contrast in Lāhījī primarily a legitimate conceptual analysis of one actual existent?
  • Is a distinct natural generator the proximate cause of the composite's substantial form, or are proximate material agents merely dispositive with respect to the form's reception from a separate intellect?
  • How can the distinction between existence and quiddity be real without reifying both as prior existing items?
  • Does a simple created intellect have a composition of essence and existence without the material composition of parts?
  • How does the objection to real, separately added existence interact with Ṣadrā's aṣālat al-wujūd and tashkīk?

Dependency summary

Knowledge and quiddity → distinguishing mental and extramental modes → meaning of existence → particular existence as made → denial of an extrinsically attached existence-thing → simple making of one actual existent → real causal dependence without dependence on internal parts → generation and conservation.

This is one continuous philosophical route, not a list of unrelated school positions.

I. The older, not merely reactive, Peripatetic problem

Before Ṣadrā, the general Aristotelian–Avicennian position distinguished:

  • Accidental motion, such as the same body becoming hotter or changing place;
  • Substantial generation/corruption, in which one substantial form ceases and another begins, ordinarily not analysed as continuous motion through an infinity of actual intermediate substantial species.

This distinction is explicitly argued in al-Ḥillī's Nihāyat al-marām, IV, discussion of the impossibility of motion in substance.

Arabic — al-Ḥillī IV:399

اعلم أن الذي يقع الاشتباه فيه من الجواهر وأنه متحرك في الجوهر إنما هو الصور الجوهرية ... والمدّعى هنا بيان أن الصور الجوهرية إنما يمكن حدوثها على الوجه الأول دون الثاني.

Translation

Know that the question susceptible of confusion among substances, whether something moves in substance, concerns substantial forms. The thesis to be established is that substantial forms can come into existence in the first of two ways—at once—and not in the second, namely gradually.

Arabic — IV:410

أن هذا التبدل والتغير في الصور الطبيعية لا يمكن أن يحدث على التدريج، بل إنما يحدث دفعة واحدة.

Translation

Such replacement or alteration of natural forms cannot occur gradually; it occurs all at once.

Important source distinction: The web edition of IV:399 interleaves a modern editor's note about Ṣadrā and Ṭabāṭabāʾī with al-Ḥillī's primary prose. That note cannot be translated as if al-Ḥillī, who predated Ṣadrā, were discussing Ṣadrā's later theory. Its provenance belongs to the apparatus, not the matn.

The later disagreement is therefore more precise than “philosophy was static until Ṣadrā.” Many accidental movements, natural developments, and even substantial coming-to-be were already recognised; the disagreement concerns the ontological category in which gradual change occurs and whether the substance itself is an intrinsically flowing existence.

II. Lāhījī — sixth question on movement in existence (Shawāriq I:52–53)

Boundary: from the heading «المسألة السادسة في أن الماهية لا تتحرك في الوجود» on p. 52 through the immediately preceding argument before «المسألة السابعة» on p. 53.

L1. Motion presupposes a subject stable relative to its changing determination

Arabic — p. 53

والدليل على ذلك هو أن الحركة في كل شيء معناها أن يكون المتحرك متقوماً وموجوداً من دون ذلك الشيء وباقياً بعينه ويتوارد عليه في كل آن من الآنات المفروضة في زمان الحركة فرد من أفراد ذلك الشيء.

Translation

The argument is that motion in respect of something means that the thing moving is constituted and exists without that respect in which it moves, remaining the same individual while a different instance of the relevant determination comes upon it at each posited instant during the motion.

In change of place, the body already exists without possessing this specific place and acquires successive positions. In heating, the same water persists while particular intensities of heat succeed one another.

L2. Essence cannot remain actual while one existence is exchanged for another

Arabic

وظاهر أن مثل ذلك التبدل والتوارد لا يمكن في الماهية بالقياس إلى الوجود، فإن الماهية لا يتصور كونها متقومة وموجودة وباقية من دون الوجود ليتصور توارد الوجود عليها.

Translation

Plainly, such exchange and succession cannot occur to a quiddity with respect to existence, since a quiddity cannot be conceived as constituted, existent and persisting without existence, so that successive existences could come upon it.

In the same way, prime matter cannot undergo a motion in substantial form if it cannot be established without some form; the allegedly stable subject is not available in the way required by the definition of motion.

The inference extends to substance, essence and what is essential, and more generally to anything without which the subject cannot exist as a constituted actual thing.

L3. Gradation of different existents does not itself prove movement of one existent

Lāhījī grants the possibility of gradational comparison (tashkīk) among existing beings while resisting the inference from such comparison to the progressive intensification of one quiddity's existence. Different instances can be stronger and weaker without it following that the same actual individual changes continuously from one instance of existence into another.

Arabic

وأيضاً إن الوجود ليس له فرد قائم بالماهية بمعنى كونه صفة موجودة ممتازة عن الماهية، بل هو مجرد كون الماهية.

Translation

Moreover, existence does not have an instance subsisting in a quiddity as though it were an actual property distinct from the quiddity; rather, it is simply the quiddity's being.

This links the substantial-motion problem directly to the critique of an attached existence-property.

L4. The first objection: perhaps existences can replace one another instantaneously

Arabic

فإن قيل ... فيجوز أن يتوارد على الماهية وجودات متعاقبة بحيث إذا انتفى عن الماهية وجود في آن حصل لها في ذلك الآن بعينه وجود آخر أشد أو أزيد أو أضعف أو أنقص، وكذلك في الهيولى.

Translation

Someone might object that the quiddity needs only some instance of existence, just as matter needs some instance of form. Successive existences might therefore come upon it: at the very instant one instance ceases, another stronger, greater, weaker or smaller instance obtains. Likewise successive forms might come upon matter.

Answer

قلت وجوب بقاء الموضوع في الحركة بعينه يستدعي بقاء وجود بعينه مع الماهية بالضرورة، وإلا لكانت الماهية مع كل وجود فرداً آخر منها فلا تكون باقية بعينها.

I reply: the requirement that the subject of motion remain numerically the same necessarily requires the persistence of the same existence belonging to the quiddity. Otherwise, with each instance of existence the quiddity would be a different instance of itself and would not persist numerically unchanged. The same applies to matter in relation to its form.

Core pressure: identity is not guaranteed by the formal claim that “the same essence” is being predicated at each stage. Lāhījī thinks the identity of the actual subject is required for a continuous physical motion.

L5. The second argument: do infinitely many intermediate states exist actually or potentially?

Lāhījī presents a further response. In continuous motion the indefinitely many individual positions or qualitative stages traversed between two limits are not all individually actual as discrete, instantaneously occupied states. If a moving thing really occupied an actual determinate state at every supposed instant, either it would remain at one state long enough to interrupt motion, or a succession of actually existing indivisible instants would follow, which cannot form a continuous time.

Applied to movement in existence itself, this analysis threatens to leave the quiddity actually nonexistent between posited stages, since its instances of existence would be merely potential.

Arabic (selected)

فلو تحركت الماهية في الوجود لزم أن يكون جميع أفراد وجودها في زمان الحركة بالقوة فيلزم كون الماهية حال الحركة في الوجود موجودة بالقوة لا بالفعل.

Translation

If a quiddity moved with respect to existence, all its instances of existence during the time of motion would have to be potential, and the quiddity during that motion would exist potentially rather than actually.

L6. Objection from motion in place, and answer

An interlocutor objects that the body cannot be deprived of an actual location. Lāhījī answers that what is needed is not its occupation of each discrete terminal location at each instant; the actuality of being between the extremes can account for motion while the individual points are potential divisions.

A final objection proposes that an actual specific state exists at each hypothetically chosen instant, without successive instants: between any two posited instants lies time. Lāhījī replies that the quiddity would then lack actual existence during that intervening time if the relevant actual instances were restricted to each posited instant.

The unit closes with an instruction to reflect (fa-tadabbar), not a full rejection of all concepts of continuous individual existence.

Critical evaluation of Lāhījī's premises

His case rests on a subject-and-determination model of motion:

  1. The moving subject exists actually.
  2. The determination in which it moves must be distinct from what makes that subject actual.
  3. A numerically identical subject persists during the reception of successive determinations.
  4. Existence and constitutive form cannot be treated as determinations that a subject already actual without them receives.
  5. Therefore movement in existence and in constitutive substance cannot occur in the same manner as movement in place or heat.

The vulnerable premise is (2): must all continuity be analysed as an already actual subject bearing different ontologically derivative features? Ṣadrā answers no.

S1. One flowing individual can possess potentially infinite determinations

The difference: Ṣadrā does not require innumerable separate actual substantial kinds at every instant. The differentiated substantial meanings are merely potential boundaries or analyses of a numerically continuous existence.

Ṣadrā reports this as the objection of Ibn Sīnā and earlier philosophers, not as his own conclusion.

فلا محالة يتبدل عليه صفات ذاتية جوهرية ولم يلزم منه وجود أنواع بلا نهاية بالفعل، بل هناك وجود واحد شخصي متصل له حدود غير متناهية بالقوة.

S4. Does movement need an enduring substrate?

Arabic — IV:87

الأصل في كل شيء هو وجوده والماهية تبع له ... وموضوع كل حركة وإن وجب أن يكون باقياً بوجوده وتشخصه إلا أنه يكفي في تشخص الموضوع الجسماني أن يكون هناك مادة تتشخص بوجود صورة ما ...

Translation

The fundamental reality of each thing is its existence, while its quiddity follows upon it. Although the subject of every movement must indeed persist in its existence and individuation, it suffices for a bodily subject's individuation that there be matter individualised through the presence of some form ...

This is a radical reformulation of what persists. Ṣadrā can preserve continuity while treating the continuous actual existence as the principle of identity, rather than taking an unchanged specific quiddity as a subject to which substantial states are attached.

What this rebuttal does and does not establish

  • Established in Ṣadrā's own text: a continuous individual existence can support substantial intensification without an actual infinity of simultaneously or successively separate species.
  • Not yet established: that every natural substance in ordinary physical science must be understood this way, or that the proposed continuity is demonstrated independently of Ṣadrā's account of existence.
  • Key philosophical objection still live: can individual unity of existence itself be specified non-circularly without presupposing the very continuity whose possibility is under dispute?

IV. AT comparison: not all “substantial change” is the same

Aristotelian–Thomistic philosophy permits substantial generation and corruption and genuine powers of development within living substances. It ordinarily denies that one numerically identical substantial form is simply a continuous series of different substantial species in the same sense Ṣadrā proposes.

But the following are distinct:

  1. Accidental alteration of a persistent substance (a body becomes hotter).
  2. Growth and maturation of one organism (same natural kind, developing powers and accidents).
  3. Substantial generation/corruption (loss or acquisition of constitutive substantial form).
  4. Ṣadrian substantial motion (the existence of a material substance intrinsically flows toward different degrees of actuality).

A comparison that calls (2) “substantial motion” because it involves real development would equivocate. A stronger comparison would ask whether the unity of a changing organism's life admits degrees of actuality that can be understood without converting a substance into a succession of numerically different substances.

The new philosophical synthesis to test

Proposition: An individual may have real developmental continuity, with varying determinations of actuality, while retaining substantial identity.

  • Lāhījī supplies the identity constraint: changes presuppose a numerically identifiable subject.
  • Ṣadrā supplies the continuity possibility: numerical unity may belong to one flowing existence, not merely to an unchanging kind.
  • AT supplies an account of act, potency, form and intrinsic powers, including the distinction between what it is to retain the same substantial nature and what it is to undergo substantial generation.

There may be a useful new synthesis around analogical grades of actuality within enduring substances, but this is a proposed philosophical development, not a position to be attributed to Ibn Sīnā, Lāhījī or Aquinas without further texts.

V. What actually changed in the tradition?

A. From Ibn Sīnā and al-Ḥillī to Lāhījī: the traditional claim that substantial form is not acquired through continuous motion is preserved, but Lāhījī develops an explicit argument from persistence of the existence of the subject and meets a specific objection about replacing instances of existence at the same instant. That is more than slogan-level conservatism.

B. Ṣadrā's real innovation: the very individuation of a material thing can be its continuous existential flow, with different substantive determinations only potentially rather than actually distinct. The debate is therefore about individuation, existence and how the continuum is analysed, not merely about whether material beings change.

C. Later Peripatetic response: a historian cannot infer that every late Avicennian rejects substantial motion. In particular, the nineteenth-century Jilwa is reported as the author of a printed Ithbāt al-ḥaraka al-jawhariyya, Tehran 1313 AH print noted in al-Ṭihrānī's al-Dharīʿa. Its title and physical printing are documented, but its thesis, precise argument and relationship to Sadrian substantial motion must be inspected directly. The previous reflexive classification “Jilwa = opponent of substantial motion” is retracted.

  • Collate the full Lāhījī sixth question with additional editions; particularly his distinction between tashkīk among instances and motion of one individual.
  • Retrieve the relevant parts of Avicenna's Physics and Metaphysics, and Rāzī's objections, rather than relying on Ṣadrā's report of their position.
  • Keep al-Ḥillī's matn and modern editor's footnotes separate.
  • Collate Ṣadrā's running text IV:85–87 with its glosses (e.g. Sabzawārī), separately attributed.
  • Locate and inspect the 1313 AH Jilwa print: Ithbāt al-ḥaraka al-jawhariyya, and distinguish it from other writings titled Taḥqīq al-ḥaraka fī l-jawhar.
  • Compare the exact Latin De principiis naturae and Aristotle's Physics V before declaring an AT consensus about the indivisibility of substantial generation.

فإن الأفكار والتأملات حركات معدة للنفس نحو قبول الفيض، كما أن الحدود الوسطى معدة بنحو أشد تأكيداً لقبول النتيجة.

Translation

Our acts of thought and reflection are preparatory movements disposing the soul to receive the emanation, just as middle terms [in demonstration] dispose it still more decisively to receive the conclusion.

Important distinction: Sensible experience is not pointless in this account. Its role is genuinely preparatory, and disciplined demonstration determines which content can be received. The division of epistemic labour, however, differs significantly from a theory in which the human agent intellect itself abstracts the universal intelligible form from phantasms.

A cross-domain result

The epistemic role of the Active Intellect has to be distinguished from its cosmological role as Giver of Forms. Ibn Sīnā employs related language of preparation and receiving forms in both, but neither role can be automatically inferred from the other when reconstructing later philosophers. A nineteenth-century thinker could revise the one without clearly revising the other.

III. Why learning is not a permanent storehouse of fully actual thoughts

Ibn Sīnā's Nafs V.5 considers whether a learned intelligible that is not currently being thought remains present in a continuously actual, fully cognised condition, or is recovered through stored bodily representations, or depends on renewed intellectual actualisation.

Arabic — reported printed p. 336

فبقي أن يكون القسم الصحيح هو القسم الأخير، ويكون التعلم طلب الاستعداد التام للاتصال به ... فتفيض منه الصور مفصلة في النفس بتوسط الفكرة.

Translation

The remaining correct alternative is the last: learning is the acquisition of a complete preparedness for connection with [the Active Intellect] ... so that detailed intelligible forms are bestowed from it upon the soul through the mediation of thinking.

Arabic — reported printed p. 337

وإذا قيل إن فلاناً عالم بالمعقولات فمعناه أنه بحيث كلما شاء أحضر صورته في ذهن نفسه ... ليس أن ذلك المعقول حاضر في ذهنه ومتصور في عقله بالفعل دائماً.

Translation

When someone is called knowledgeable about intelligibles, this means that the person is so disposed that, whenever he wills, he can bring the intelligible form before his mind; it does not mean that the intelligible remains present in his mind, actually understood at all times.

Critical implication: An acquired habit or stable disposition (malaka) is distinct from the current act of considering an intelligible. Ibn Sīnā's explanation introduces an external intellectual source into this transition. A Thomistic explanation can preserve the habit/act distinction without adopting the same separate mediator.

This is relevant to a wider theory of acquired intellectual dispositions, teaching, and educational formation: a student's possessing a science is not equivalent to simultaneously contemplating every proposition in it.

IV. The real disagreement over the union of knower and known

In Ibn Sīnā's psychology, becoming an actual knower of a thing does not, for our individual human soul, automatically make the knower and known numerically one substance.

*Arabic — Nafs V.5, reported printed p. 329 (selective)*

فلا يكون العقل والعاقل والمعقول شيئاً واحداً في أنفسنا، نعم هذا في شيء آخر يمكن أن يكون على ما سنلمحه في موضعه.

Translation

Therefore intellect, the one who understands, and what is understood are not one and the same thing in our case. This could, however, be true of something else, as we shall indicate in the appropriate place.

Editor's separately quoted Rāzī argument, NOT Ibn Sīnā's own prose: In the modern edition, a note quotes Rāzī's al-Mabāḥith al-mashriqiyya: if the knower literally becomes the first intelligible and subsequently a different intelligible, it is unclear how the original knowledge can persist without one individual becoming two different essences. The original Mabāḥith passage needs its own collation.

Sadrian contrast: Ṣadrā develops a much stronger role for unity of the intellect, knower, and intelligible. It is essential to specify which level of intellectual knowing is claimed to involve existential unity, rather than parody his doctrine as though it were ordinary physical identity between two objects.

V. Ṣadrā on the identity of a human being through bodily change — Asfār V:190

Arabic

الأصل السابع أن هوية البدن وتشخصه إنما يكونان بنفسه لا بجرمه، فزيد مثلاً زيد بنفسه لا بجسده، ولأجل ذلك يستمر وجوده وتشخصه ما دامت النفس باقية فيه وإن تبدلت أجزاؤه.

Translation

Seventh principle: the identity and individuation of the body belong to it through its soul, not through its bulk of matter. Thus Zayd is Zayd through his soul, not through his body; and therefore his being and individuation continue so long as his soul remains within it, even if the body's material components are replaced.

Arabic

فإن الهوية الإنسانية في جميع هذه التحولات والتقلبات واحدة هي هي بعينها لأنها واقعة على سبيل الاتصال الوحداني التدريجي.

Translation

For human identity through all these transformations and changes is one and numerically the same, since they occur through a gradual continuity that possesses unity.

Important qualification: This is not an attribution of unchanging substantial form throughout every phase of the person's development. Ṣadrā's framework makes personal identity involve continuous development in existence, including the claim that the human soul is corporeal in origination and spiritual in persistence. The exact argument for that further doctrine requires additional passages from the Asfār.

An apparent puzzle with the subject of substantial motion

  • A material body changes in parts, states, faculties and (according to Ṣadrā) intrinsic existence.
  • The soul constitutes the human's central principle of identity.
  • This gives a stronger account of personal unity through transformation, but it also raises a question: does the soul itself possess a continuous identity independently of an already unified temporal life, or is that unity constituted by the existential course?

The same identity question arose in Lāhījī's objection to substantial motion. Thus the psychology and natural philosophy must be analysed together.

VI. Aquinas on what the intellect knows

*Latin — ST I q. 85 a. 2*

Species intelligibilis non est quod intelligitur actu, sed id quo intelligit intellectus.

Translation

The intelligible species is not what is actually understood; it is what enables the intellect to understand.

In the body of the article Aquinas argues that if all sciences concerned merely the species internal to the knower, natural sciences would not be about external things. Intellect is thereby capable of knowing the nature in the thing, while its act of understanding belongs to the knower.

Aquinas also explains abstraction from phantasms and the agent intellect's activity in ST I qq. 84–85, rejecting the claim that all intelligibles necessarily come into us from separately existing Forms.

VII. Two especially useful implications for the AT synthesis

A. Don't mistake agency of the human intellect for the cosmological Giver of Forms

Natural formation of a new substance and abstraction of an intelligible nature are two distinct causal operations. In Ibn Sīnā's scheme the Active Intellect figures in both, but their separation matters:

  1. Could the separate bestowal of natural forms be rejected while preserving a form of intellectual illumination?
  2. Could the human agent intellect perform abstraction without thereby being the sufficient efficient cause of intellectual subsistence?
  3. Could the causation of substantial forms be both natural and dependent upon God without adding the separate tenth intelligence as an ontologically necessary mediator?

A coherent synthesis has to decide these separately, not conflate them under the vague phrase “vertical causality.”

B. A shared doctrine of substantial personal unity need not imply an identical theory of change

Aquinas and Ibn Sīnā can agree that a person is not merely a heap of material parts. Ṣadrā radicalises the continuity question by applying it to the substance's very becoming. The philosophical issue is whether the same-person relation is best understood through persistent form, continuous existence, or a carefully differentiated combination.

English — connected translation of the complete definitions unit

We have discussed substances and accidents, how priority and posteriority are considered in them, and how definitions correspond to the universal and particular things defined. It is therefore appropriate now to speak about cause and effect, since these too are properties that belong to the existent insofar as it is existent.

The causes, as you have heard, are form, element [matter], agent and end.

We mean by the formal cause that cause which is a constitutive part of a thing and through which the thing is actually what it is. By the material or elemental cause we mean the constitutive part of a thing through which the thing is potentially what it is, and in which the potentiality for its existence resides.

By the agent we mean a cause which bestows an existence distinct from its own essence. That is, the agent's own essence is not, primarily and as such, the subject in which the existence of what receives existence from it is realised; it is not in itself the potency of that existence except incidentally. Furthermore, the existence conferred must not be something for the sake of which the agent acts qua agent; if it is an end at all, it is so under another description.

This is because metaphysical philosophers do not mean by an agent merely a principle that causes motion, as natural philosophers do. They mean a principle and bestower of existence, as in the case of the Creator's relation to the cosmos. A natural efficient cause, on the other hand, does not bestow an existence other than movement of one of its kinds. Thus the source of existence in natural philosophy is a principle of movement.

Finally, by the end we mean the cause for whose sake the existence of something other than that end is brought about.

2. Intrinsic form and material potentiality: no reified substrate

In Ilāhiyyāt II.4 Ibn Sīnā explains that corporeal matter becomes actual only in the presence of form. Matter's receptivity and the form's actualisation are not two complete substances that are independently actual and then combined. The argument, from II.4 p.120, begins:

Arabic

فقد صح أن المادة الجسمانية إنما تقوم بالفعل عند وجود الصورة وأيضا فإن الصورة المادية ليست توجد مفارقة للمادة.

Translation

It has therefore been established that corporeal matter is actual only upon the presence of form; likewise, material form does not exist separately from matter.

The following discussion distinguishes a genuine causal order of ontological dependence from two things that merely correlate in existence: the negation of one may accompany the negation of the other without causing that negation. This anticipates the later Lāhījī concern: a changing subject cannot simply be defined as matter already actual apart from its substantial form.

3. From cosmic disposition to the wāhib al-ṣuwar

The cosmological context of Ilāhiyyāt IX.5 matters. Ibn Sīnā first considers a proposed derivation of elemental qualities from celestial rotation—heating near the region of fire, cooling near earth, differing degrees of condensation and rarefaction. He criticises the proposal as insufficiently demonstrative and suggests the actual order may be more complex.

Arabic — IX.5, p.413, selective connected passage

وأن تكون هذه المادة التي تحدث بالشركة يفيض إليها من الأجرام السماوية، إما عن أربعة أجرام، وإما عن عدة منحصرة في أربع جمل، عن كل واحد منها ما يتهيأ لصورة جسم بسيط. فإذا استعد نال الصورة من واهب الصور، أو يكون ذلك كله يفيض عن جرم واحد، وأن يكون هناك سبب يوجب انقساما من الأسباب الخفية علينا.

This is a direct primary-source statement, not merely the modern historian's reconstruction, of the difference between becoming disposed (istaʿadda) and receiving the form from the wāhib al-ṣuwar.

4. Aquinas' explicit reconstruction of Avicenna and objection to it

*Latin — De potentia III.8, corpus, [58809] (selected contiguous argument)*

Et quia operatio naturae non potest esse ex nihilo, et per consequens oportet quod sit ex praesuppositione, non operabatur, secundum eos, natura, nisi ex parte materiae disponendo ipsam ad formam. Formam vero, quam oportet fieri et non praesupponi, oportet esse ex agente qui non praesupponit aliquid, sed potest ex nihilo facere ... Et hoc Avicenna dixit esse intelligentiam ultimam inter substantias separatas.

Translation

Because nature cannot act from nothing, and consequently must act from something already presupposed, some held that nature works only upon the side of matter, disposing it for form.

But form itself, which had to be brought about rather than presupposed, they thought must come from an agent that presupposes nothing and can act from nothing. Avicenna identified that agent as the last intelligence among the separate substances.

Critical note: This is Aquinas' report and criticism of the view, not a direct quotation from Ibn Sīnā. But Ibn Sīnā's own IX.5 passage above independently confirms the distinct role of the Giver of Forms in his account of the elements.

Aquinas' central objection: why would the natural generator have precisely that form?

*Latin — De potentia III.8*

Quia cum unumquodque natum sit simile sibi agere ... non requireretur similitudo secundum formam substantialem in agente naturali, nisi forma substantialis geniti esset per actionem agentis ... inconveniens videtur, hoc generante praetermisso, aliud exterius inquirere.

Translation

Since it is natural for each thing to produce something similar to itself, a natural agent would not need to resemble its effect in substantial form unless the generated thing's substantial form actually resulted from the natural agent's activity. Since what must be acquired in the generated thing is already actual in the natural generator, it seems inappropriate to set that generator aside and seek some other external agent.

The force of the argument: Natural agents are not mere preparatory triggers with no specific causal relation to the essential character of the generated thing. The match between the generator's substantial form and that of what it generates constitutes evidence of true proximate efficient causality.

Aquinas' positive resolution: it is the composite, not an isolated form, that comes to be

Latin — corpus

Unumquodque autem factum, hoc modo dicitur fieri quo dicitur esse ... unde illud quod proprie fit per se, compositum est. Forma autem non proprie fit ... Nihil ergo obstat ... quin formas substantiales ex operatione naturae esse dicamus. Nam id quod fit, non est forma, sed compositum; quod ex materia fit, et non ex nihilo. ... Et sic non proprie dicitur quod forma fiat in materia, sed magis quod de materiae potentia educatur.

Translation

Something is said to be made in the manner in which it is said to exist, since being is the terminus of production. Therefore what is properly and primarily produced is the composite. Form is not, properly speaking, itself made in isolation. There is thus no obstacle, on the ground that nature produces nothing from nothing, to affirming that substantial forms arise through the operation of nature: what comes to be is not form considered alone, but the composite, which comes from matter and not from nothing.

Accordingly, we should not strictly say that form is independently produced inside matter; rather, it is drawn forth from matter's potency (de materiae potentia educatur).

This is the decisive explanatory reallocation: the form is intrinsic in both thinkers, but Aquinas makes the natural generator causally sufficient in its own dependent order to generate a composite, without treating the form as a separately created entity.

Exception: the human rational soul

*Latin — De potentia III.8 ad 7*

licet anima rationalis habeat materiam in qua sit, non tamen educitur de potentia materiae, cum eius natura supra omnem materialem ordinem elevetur ...

Translation

Although the rational soul has matter in which it is present, it is not educed from matter's potency, for its nature is raised above every material order.

The rational soul is an explicit exception. Any synthesis saying “Aquinas thinks all forms simply come from natural material generators” is false.

Strongest compatible reformulation — explicitly proposed, not attributed

Could the Active Intellect be regarded as a secondary but higher-order completing cause of natural generation while natural generators are genuinely productive in their own right? Such an account would avoid two extremes—eliminating natural powers or making a separate intelligence causally unnecessary—but it would remain subject to Aquinas' objection: if the natural generator is sufficient in its own order for the composite, what irreducible causal work remains for that particular separate intellect rather than simply for divine universal concurrence?

The answer cannot be supplied by the observation that God creates and sustains all things. That would change the question from the role of the created Active Intellect to the role of the uncreated First Cause.

6. The connections to later Avicennian revisions

  • *Lāhījī, Shawāriq I:159–161: what an efficient cause makes is particular existence, and one actual concrete reality may be distinguished conceptually into quiddity and existence. This helps avoid reifying the form and actuality as two independent made items. Chapter on jaʿl*.
  • *Ṣadrā, Asfār IV:85–87*: the very identity of a natural thing is a continuously developing individual existence. This potentially transforms how “receiving form” and “persists through change” are analysed, even without a demonstrated rejection of a hierarchy of intelligences. Chapter on substantial motion.
  • *Ibn Sīnā, al-Nafs V.5: the separate Active Intellect also actualises intellectual apprehension*. This epistemological role must not be silently treated as evidence for a later thinker's acceptance of its cosmological role. Chapter on cognition.
  • *Narāqī, Jāmiʿ al-afkār I:38–42: continuing existential dependence upon a cause is not* the same as the efficient generation of new form; the builder objection operates at precisely this explanatory interface. Working Narāqī dossier.
  • Jilwa and Khayrābādī: the collected bibliographies do not yet provide sufficient collated natural-philosophical Arabic to state conclusively whether either abolished, retained or transformed the Giver of Forms. Do not infer that from a school label.

7. What this comparison actually establishes

Established from primary texts: Avicenna explicitly distinguishes metaphysical and natural efficient causation, recognises matter and form as constitutive causes, and describes elemental matter as disposed for form received from the Giver. Aquinas explicitly identifies Avicenna's last separated intellect as the giver, rejects it as necessary to ordinary natural generation, and argues that the generated composite is properly the product of a natural generator.

Still contested: whether a suitably refined account of different orders of causal explanation can reconcile their systems without losing Aquinas' real criticism. That requires examination of both Avicennian natural agency and the explanatory work of the last intelligence, plus subsequent developments.

I. Two arguments that must not be conflated

A. Modal thesis: If a complete cause is actually in place and lacks no condition, its effect follows; a possible effect is necessary through another while remaining possible in itself. Lāhījī's Shawāriq I:88 and Narāqī's Jāmiʿ I:38–42 help refine this distinction.

B. Divine-agency thesis: Whether the First necessarily determines this very cosmos and its intermediary intellects, or produces one of several possible cosmic orders through knowing and willing, is not decided by (A). Otherwise the causal modal proposition is being made to carry an additional cosmological and theological claim.

Aquinas's De potentia III.16 criticises Avicenna principally at the second level, not by denying that effects of actually complete causes have intelligible explanations.

II. Avicenna: divine simplicity and the permanent effect

*Arabic — Ilāhiyyāt IX.1, printed p.373, selected connected text*

فقد ظهر لنا أن للكل مبدأ واجب الوجود، غير داخل في جنس أو واقع تحت حد أو برهان، بريء عن الكم والكيف والماهية والأين والمتى والحركة، لا ند له ولا شريك له ولا ضد له، وأنه واحد من جميع الوجوه ... وقد بان لك بعد ذلك أن واجب الوجود بذاته واجب الوجود من جميع جهاته، وأنه لا يجوز أن تستأنف له حالة لم تكن، مع أنه قد بان لك أن العلة لذاتها تكون موجبة المعلول، فإن دامت أوجبت المعلول دائماً.

Translation

It has become clear that the whole has a principle that is necessary in existence, which falls under no genus, is subject to no definition or demonstration [of its essence], and is free of quantity, quality, quiddity, place, time and motion. It has neither equal nor partner nor opposite; it is one in every respect ...

It has further become clear that the Necessary Existent through itself is necessary in every respect and cannot begin to have a state that it previously lacked; and it has been established that a cause through its very essence necessitates its effect—if the cause endures, it necessitates its effect continually.

Critical note. This connects divine immutability with the thesis of the complete cause's effect. It should not be dismissed as the assertion “God is a natural mechanism”; Ibn Sīnā also insists on divine knowing. But it is a genuine modal commitment whose implications for cosmic origination differ from those of the Thomistic freedom thesis.

Printed variants on p.373

  • In the lemma «في صفة فاعلية المبدأ الأول», the phrase «فاعلية» is reported as «ما عليه» in ب، ح، د. This changes the transmitted title or description and is editorially significant, though not conclusive about doctrine.
  • In «فإن دامت أوجبت المعلول دائما», the clause is found in the printed base reading; no substantive variant is reported there in the displayed apparatus.

Editorial boundary: The ellipsis marks intervening claims about the First's unity and the first mover. The whole of IX.1 has not been reproduced.

III. Avicenna: neither purposive choice like ours nor blind nature

Arabic — IX.4, p.402

قد صح لنا فيما قدمناه من القول أن الواجب الوجود بذاته واحد، وأنه ليس بجسم ولا في جسم ولا منقسم بوجه من الوجوه، فإذن الموجودات كلها وجودها عنه ... فلهذا لا يجوز أن يكون كون الكل عنه على سبيل قصد منه كقصدنا لتكوين الكل ولوجود الكل فيكون قاصدا لأجل شيء غيره ... وليس كون الكل عنه على سبيل الطبع بأن يكون وجود الكل عنه لا بمعرفة ولا لرضى منه، وكيف يصح هذا وهو عقل محض يعقل ذاته؟

Translation

It has been established that the Necessary Existent through itself is one, neither a body nor located in a body, and in no respect divisible. It follows that the being of all existents is from it. It cannot be the cause of their existence in the manner of deliberation like our own, intending the production of the cosmos for the sake of something other than itself. Yet the whole does not proceed from it as a blind natural process, without knowledge or satisfaction: how could that be, when the First is pure intellect understanding itself?

Why this is a more difficult position than the textbook formula suggests

Ibn Sīnā excludes two different inadequate accounts:

  1. Human purposive deliberation that seeks an additional benefit outside the perfect First; such benefit would imply something the First lacked.
  2. Unknowing natural production that would contradict the First's pure intellectual self-knowledge.

The First's agency proceeds through intellectual understanding without a temporal transition into intending or realising a new purpose. One cannot responsibly say “Avicenna's First has no knowledge of effects,” because the quoted text expressly rejects unknowing operation. Equally, one must not assume that “the First knows what proceeds” establishes libertarian alternative possibilities in the Thomistic sense.

Printed variant warning: The same passage reports “لا بمعرفة” versus a variant “لمعرفة” in د. Such a change alters the local positive/negative wording, and cannot be ignored when discussing knowledgeable production. The running text contrasts action “without knowledge” with the First's actual intellect; the surrounding argumentative grammar governs the reading. A further variant «ولا لرضى» / «رضى» is recorded. The text should be checked against the edition's witness key before privileging one interpretation of divine approval.

IV. Aquinas's reconstruction and criticism: the First's simplicity does not impose a single first effect

*Latin — De potentia III.16*

Alii pluralitatem rerum et modos diversos earum ex necessitate causae efficientis ponebant, sicut Avicenna, et eius sequaces. Posuit enim quod primum ens, in quantum intelligit se ipsum, producit unum tantum causatum, quod est intelligentia prima.

Translation

Others, such as Avicenna and his followers, placed the plurality of things and their diverse modes of being under the necessity of the efficient cause. He held that the First Being, insofar as it understands itself, produces only one immediate effect, namely the first intelligence.

This is Aquinas's report of Avicenna's cosmological inference, not a verbatim quotation from Ibn Sīnā. The correct comparison pairs it with Ibn Sīnā's IX.4 and the explicit elaboration of separate intellects, not just with the general doctrine of causes.

Aquinas's first objection: God is not limited to one effect

*Latin — De potentia III.16*

Sed haec etiam positio stare non potest. Primo, quia ponit potentiam divinam terminari ad unum effectum, qui est intelligentia prima.

Translation

This position cannot stand: first, because it makes the divine power terminate in only one effect, the first intelligence.

The second objection: intermediary substances are not creators in their own right

Latin

Secundo, quia ponit alias substantias praeter Deum esse aliarum creatrices, quod esse impossibile in alia quaestione, art. 4, ostensum est.

Translation

Second, because it holds that substances other than God are creators of other substances; but we showed in article 4 that this is impossible.

This is an important distinction between secondary causal agency and creation of existence absolutely. Aquinas is not arguing that no angel or intelligence can affect another thing; he denies that a created intellect can be an independent source of creative power.

The third objection: rational order rather than restriction by natural necessity

Latin

Sequitur etiam ad hanc positionem ... quod decor ordinis universi sit casualis, ex quo rerum diversitatem non adscribit intentioni finis, sed terminationi potentiarum activarum ad suos effectus.

Translation

It also follows from this position, Aquinas says, that the beauty of the universe's order becomes accidental, since the diversity of things is not attributed to an intention ordered to an end, but merely to the restriction of productive powers to their effects.

A possible challenge to Aquinas: Avicenna certainly recognises final causality, knowledge, cosmic hierarchy, and providence. Aquinas's criticism therefore cannot be taken as a historical demonstration that Avicenna simply denied all finality. Its precise force is whether the diversity of this very cosmos is intended as an end through unconstrained divine wisdom or instead follows necessarily from the degrees and limitations of emanated causes. The strongest formulation must engage Avicenna's account of ʿināya rather than an artificially stripped-down Avicennian cosmology.

V. Aquinas's positive account: divine wisdom, many effects, no natural necessity

*Latin — De potentia III.16*

Quod enim Deus tale universum constituere voluerit, non est necessarium neque debitum, neque ex fine neque ex potentia efficientis, neque materiae ... Sed supposito quod tale universum producere voluerit, necessarium fuit quod tales et tales creaturas produxerit ...

Translation

That God should will to establish this particular cosmos is neither necessary nor due to an end, to the power of the efficient cause, or to matter. But given that God wills to produce a cosmos of this kind, it becomes necessary for him to produce the particular creatures from which its intended constitution follows.

Here Aquinas himself draws the essential modal distinction:

  • Absolute necessity: God had to produce this world.
  • Conditional necessity: if God has freely willed this world, then its required constituents and ordering follow.

The latter does not imply the former, and therefore the Thomistic account can affirm necessary connections within a freely chosen causal order.

Multiple things can proceed from one simple First

Latin

Sic igitur dicendum est quod ab uno primo multitudo et diversitas creaturarum processit ... ex ordine sapientiae, ut in diversitate creaturarum perfectio consisteret universi.

Translation

Therefore we must say that from the one First Principle arose the plurality and diversity of creatures—not through material necessity or the limitation of productive power, but through the ordering of wisdom, so that the perfection of the cosmos should consist in the diversity of creatures.

Unification and division: The First is absolutely simple; the many dependent effects do not constitute parts inside the First. The First can know diverse creatures and will their contingent existence without acquiring a new internal accident.

VII. What would make a synthesis defensible?

A mature comparative system must distinguish four meanings of necessity:

  1. Necessary in itself (wājib bi-l-dhāt).
  2. Necessary through an actually complete cause (wājib bi-l-ghayr).
  3. Necessary given the First's free willing of a particular order (ex suppositione).
  4. Necessary by an unfree production of one effect from divine nature.

Confusing (2) with (4) makes Avicenna appear to deny freedom by a merely formal inference. Confusing (3) with (4) makes Aquinas appear equally necessitarian. The real disagreement concerns what additional principle of cosmic specification—knowledge and will, or intrinsic causal emanation—explains why the particular cosmic order obtains.

A possible reconciliation can preserve Avicenna's layered causes and account of necessary dependencies while replacing the claim that the First can only immediately produce one intelligence with a Thomistic account of freely intended plurality. But this is a new proposed synthesis, not the doctrine already found in one historical thinker. The precise ramifications for the Giver of Forms, intellective cognition and divine science must then be reworked.

Book IV, Chapter 2 — God, being, unity and plurality in Mahdī al-Narāqī

*A critical translation of a decisive post-Sadrian argument from Jāmiʿ al-afkār wa-nāqid al-anẓār II:165–167*

Textual status: A complete translation of the bounded two-interpretation and response unit from the latter part of II:166 to II:167, with its introductory quoted mystical argument from II:165–166 as selected excerpts. The Arabic base is printed below, separated by authorial voice. This is a translation and printed-text comparison, not a fresh collation of manuscript witnesses. Source: Jāmiʿ al-afkār vol. II, p.165, p.166, p.167.

1. The preceding claim is reported—not yet Narāqī's conclusion

Narāqī surveys the philosophical literature on the First's knowledge of existing things. He reports a statement from later interpreters that all the perfections of existents are included within the First's absolutely simple perfection and that the absolutely simple reality can therefore be called “all things.”

Arabic, II:165 — quoted/reported argument

وبيانه ـ على ما ذكره بعض العرفاء المحققين ـ هو: أن كل ما هو بسيط الحقيقة فهو بوحدته كل الأشياء، ولا يخرج عنه شيء منها إلا ما هو من باب النقائص والأعدام والإمكانيات.

Translation

Its explanation—as stated by some accomplished mystical philosophers—is that whatever is absolutely simple in reality is, in its very unity, all things. Nothing is excluded from it except what belongs to deficiencies, privations and possibilities.

The argument contends that if the subject of a true negation were absolutely simple, it could not be excluded from any positive perfection without thereby possessing a privative determination or a division in itself. It concludes that the absolutely simple First encompasses all positive existential perfections and therefore knows them supremely.

Arabic — source, II:165

فيثبت أن البسيط كل الموجودات من حيث الوجود والتمام لا من حيث النقائص والأعدام ولهذا ثبت علمه بالموجودات علماً بسيطاً وحضورها عنده على وجه أعلى وأتم.

Translation

It follows that the absolutely simple reality is all existents in respect of being and perfection, not in respect of deficiency and privation. For this reason its knowledge of existents is a simple knowledge, and they are present to it in a higher and more perfect manner.

Voice warning: The author here reports an argument from other metaphysicians and mystics. The next step is his analysis of whether their formulation entails numerical identity of God and creatures. A reader who quotes only the bold statement “the simple is all things” and labels it Narāqī's unqualified position will reverse the conclusion of the passage.

2. The stronger statement of mystical unity — II:166

Narāqī quotes or paraphrases the reported account of the First as pure existence, from which contingent things' existence and perfections emanate, and notes that privations and limitations belong to finite beings.

Arabic — reported position

وتوضيح ذلك: أن واجب الوجود لما كان عين حقيقة الوجود بحيث لا يشوبه نقص ولا عدم ولا قصور ولا ماهية ولا تركيب بوجه من الوجوه، بل هو صرف الوجود ومحض الخيرية وعين الكمال، وكل وجود أو كمال وجود إنما هو يفيض عنه ويرشح منه ...

Translation

The explanation is that the Necessary Existent is the very reality of existence, unmixed with deficiency, privation, limitation, quiddity or composition in any respect; rather, it is pure existence, pure goodness and perfection itself; and every existence and every existential perfection flows and emanates from it ...

Arabic — last words of reported account

وذلك هو التوحيد الوجودي فاعرفه إن كنت أهلاً لذلك!؛ انتهى.

Translation

“That is existential unity [al-tawḥīd al-wujūdī]; understand it if you are qualified to do so.” [End of the reported account.]

The terminal «انتهى» explicitly closes the reported statement. The subsequent position is Narāqī's critical response.

3. Narāqī's two interpretations — complete translated argument

Arabic base — II:166–167

وغير خفي أن ما ذكره بعض العرفاء يحتمل وجهين: أحدهما أن الواجب لما كان صرف الوجود وتأكده ووجودات الأشياء وجودات مشوبة بالقوة والعدم فائضة منه، وهذه الوجودات المشوبة بالعدم إذا فرض تحليلها إلى وجودات صرفة وعدماتها يكون وجود الواجب مشتملاً على تلك الوجودات الصرفة. يعني أن وجوده لما كان فوق التمام فيشتمل على كل مرتبة دونه مع الزيادة من غير أن يكون عين سائر المراتب، بمعنى أنه إذا حلل الأشياء الموجودة إلى وجوداتها الصرفة وأعدامها كان وجودها عين الواجب. وذلك كما أن السواد الأشد يشتمل على جميع السوادات التي تحته مع الزيادة من غير أن يكون الأشد عين سائر المراتب؛ فإذا قلنا أن السواد الأشد كل السوادات، يكون المراد أن كل مرتبة دون الأشد يوجد في الأشد مع الزيادة، لا أن يكون الأشد عين باقي المراتب. وثانيهما أن يكون المراد أن وجود الواجب هو عين وجودات الممكنات إذا خلصت عن أعدامها. وأنت خبير بأن مبنى الوجه الأول على كون الوجود مقولاً بالتشكيك، وكون وجود الواجب فرداً أقوى وأشد منه؛ ومبنى الثاني على وحدة الوجود بل الموجود؛ والقول بكل منهما يشتمل على مفاسد، وقد بينا ذلك بما لا مزيد عليه في رسالتنا المسماة «قرة العيون». فالحق أن مراد المعلمين من أن الواجب كل الوجود مع وحدته البسيطة الصرفة: أن جميع الوجودات لوازم ذاته ورشحات وجوده وهو علة تامة لها، وهي ناشئة صادرة عنها، وهو كل الأشياء في الشهود العلمي بمعنى أن حضور ذاته عند ذاته هو حضور الأشياء، بل هو في انكشاف الأشياء أقوى من حضورها بأنفسها. فمن قال أن الأشياء عين الواجب أراد بالعينية العينية باعتبار الشهود العلمي وباعتبار ملزوميته وعليته لها وانطوائها فيه إجمالاً، وهذا المعنى هو مرادهم من قولهم إنه مجمل الأشياء أو كلها، لا أن تكون تلك الوجودات عين وجود الواجب بعد تخليصها عن أعدامها الإمكانية أو يكون وجود الواجب مشتملاً عليها مع الزيادة؛ لأنه إن تحقق حينئذ بين وجود الواجب وسائر الوجودات ما به الاشتراك وما به الامتياز لزم التركيب؛ وإن لم يكن بينهما ما به الامتياز أصلاً لزم وحدة الوجود؛ وإن لم يكن بينهما ما به الاشتراك بل كانا متخالفين بأنفسهما لم يكن لاشتمال وجود الواجب على سائر الوجودات معنى.

English — connected translation

It is clear that the statement reported from certain mystical philosophers admits two interpretations.

The first is that, because the Necessary Existent is pure and intensified existence, whereas the existences of things are mixed with potentiality and privation and flow from it, if those existences were analytically separated into pure existence and their privations, the existence of the Necessary Existent would encompass their pure existences.

That is to say: because its existence transcends all ordinary completeness, it encompasses every lower degree with a surplus of perfection, without being numerically identical to those lower degrees. On this reading, were actual things analytically divided into their pure existences and their privations, their existential perfection would be identical [in the relevant respect] with that of the Necessary Existent. This resembles the way the most intense black contains all lesser degrees of blackness with an additional intensity, without being numerically identical to each weaker shade. Thus, if one says that the most intense black is “all blackness,” the meaning is that every lower degree is included within the more intense shade plus something further, not that the most intense shade is literally each of the others.

The second interpretation is that the existence of the Necessary Existent is numerically identical with the existences of contingent things, once their privations are removed.

You should recognise that the first interpretation rests upon the gradation of existence (tashkīk al-wujūd) and upon the Necessary Existent being its most intense and strongest instance. The second rests upon the unity of existence, indeed the unity of the existent. Each of these two positions, Narāqī says, entails serious difficulties, which he has explained at length in his treatise entitled Qurrat al-ʿUyūn.

The correct interpretation of the teachers' claim that the Necessary Existent is “all existence” while remaining absolutely simple and one is this: all existences are necessary concomitants of its essence and emanations of its existence. It is their complete cause, and they proceed from it. It is “all things” in the order of intellectual witnessing or divine knowledge, meaning that its own self-presence to itself is also the presence of things; indeed, its disclosure of things is more complete than their presence to themselves.

Thus, anyone who says that “things are identical with the Necessary Existent” must mean identity with respect to the intelligible witnessing in which they are known, and with respect to the First's being their necessary ground and complete cause, in whose perfection they are contained in an undifferentiated way. That is what is meant by saying that the First comprehends all things or is all things.

It does not mean that those finite existences become numerically identical to the Necessary Existent's existence when stripped of their contingent privations, nor that the existence of the Necessary Existent contains them simply as degrees plus a greater degree.

For if one posited between the Necessary Existent and the other existences something shared alongside something that differentiates them, this would entail composition in the First. If there were nothing whatsoever differentiating them, one would collapse into numerical unity of existence. But if there were no common respect at all and the two were utterly unrelated in themselves, then the assertion that the Necessary Existent encompasses other existences would lose its meaning.

Editorial note: The English qualification “[in the relevant respect]” in the first interpretation flags the tension in Narāqī's original compressed exposition: he reports “identity” in the reconstructed argument yet uses a graded-black analogy to deny numerical identity of levels. It is not a doctrinal correction silently put into his mouth.

5. Thomistic parallels and differences

Compare Aquinas, De potentia III.16, ad 3–4 and ad 6, on the one and the many, the distinct kinds of unity, and the ability of the one First to cause differentiated beings.

A. Causal containment is not numerical identity

Aquinas says the First causes all beings and their diverse modes because the source of being as being cannot leave the determinations of being wholly unexplained. This resembles Narāqī's refusal to dissolve derivative existents into the existence of their source.

B. Participation is neither a genus nor simple univocity

The standard AT approach is that finite beings participate in being and God is not a generic maximal case within the same genus. It therefore attempts to avoid the argument that graded existence forces God into a common externally differentiated species.

This is promising but not identical to Narāqī's account. His positive reading stresses scientific/intellectual witnessing (shuhūd ʿilmī) and the necessary emanation of all creatures as concomitants of God's self-presence. Aquinas stresses divine knowledge and willing without the claim that the First is cosmologically necessitated to produce this actual order.

C. Divine knowledge without real multiplicity

Narāqī interprets “all things are present in the First” as the First's knowing itself, whose perfection contains the ground of all things. Aquinas likewise holds that God's knowledge of things is not a collection of accidental representations subsequently acquired. But the mode of creaturely dependence and the necessity or freedom of cosmic production remain contested.

D. Why “one existence” is an ambiguous phrase

A mature summa should state exactly which of these is being asserted:

  • one intelligible meaning of existence (conceptual unity);
  • graded participation in being (analogical/metaphysical structure);
  • one source of all existence (causal unity);
  • one existential act numerically identical in all things (strong monism);
  • all existents known at once in God's simple self-knowledge (epistemic unity).

Only the fourth is directly a claim of numerical identity of all contingent existence with divine existence. Narāqī endorses the third and fifth here while rejecting the simplistic formulations of the second and fourth.

6. A historical correction to the previous GNAMMA research

The earlier statement “Narāqī rejects waḥdat al-wujūd” is too blunt. He reports and interprets an Akbarian/perfectionist formula, distinguishes two metaphysical readings and rejects the corresponding strong claims, but then retains a powerful causal and epistemic unity. This is not merely an anti-mystical position.

Likewise, the fact that a contemporary modern historian calls his position “transformed Avicennism” does not imply wholesale fidelity to the earlier Ibn Sīnā. At the level of divine knowledge, his Jāmiʿ exhibits a sophisticated post-Sadrian, Akbarian-aware philosophical problem even when rejecting some forms of ontological unity.

Book I, Chapter 3 — Universals, the three considerations of quiddity, and nafs al-amr

*Ibn Sīnā, Ilāhiyyāt al-Shifāʾ V.1, pp.195–196, read together with Lāhījī's mental-existence analysis and Thomistic universals*

Textual status: Continuous, directly source-linked translation of two bounded argument units: (a) the three senses of universal at Ilāhiyyāt V.1 p.195, and (b) the passage on horseness qua horseness at p.196, along with published printed variants. Not a full translation of the whole of V.1–2, and not an assertion that nafs al-amr is a term employed in these exact passages. It is introduced below as a later problem requiring further evidence.

Primary texts: Ibn Sīnā, Ilāhiyyāt V.1 p.195, p.196; Lāhījī, Shawāriq I:50–52; Aquinas, ST I q.85 a.2 and Aristotle's De interpretatione and De anima commentaries (comparative loci, not full Latin edition here).

I. The universality of a concept has three distinguishable meanings

Arabic — Ibn Sīnā, V.1 p.195 (the whole threefold classification)

وبالحري أن نتكلم الآن في الكلي والجزئي، فإنه مناسب أيضاً لما فرغنا منه، وهو من الأعراض الخاصة بالوجود، فنقول: إن الكلي قد يقال على وجوه ثلاثة: فيقال كلي للمعنى من جهة أنه مقول بالفعل على كثيرين، مثل الإنسان. ويقال كلي للمعنى إذا كان جائزاً أن يحمل على كثيرين وإن لم يشترط أنهم موجودون بالفعل، مثل معنى البيت المسبع، فإنه كلي من حيث إن من طبيعته أن يقال على كثيرين، ولكن ليس يجب أن يكون أولئك الكثيرين لا محالة موجودين بل ولا الواحد منهم. ويقال كلي للمعنى الذي لا مانع من تصوره أن يقال على كثيرين، إنما يمنع منه إن منع سبب ويدل عليه دليل، مثل الشمس والأرض، فإنها من حيث تعقل شمساً وأرضاً لا يمنع الذهن عن أن يجوز أن معناها يوجد في كثير، إلا أن يأتيه دليل أو حجة يعرف به أن هذا ممتنع. ويكون ذلك ممتنعاً بسبب من خارج لا لنفس تصوره.

English — connected translation

It is appropriate now to discuss the universal and the particular, since this is related to what we have just treated, and universality and particularity belong among the features associated with existence.

We say that the term universal can be used in three ways.

First, a meaning is called universal insofar as it is actually predicated of many things—as, for example, the meaning human being.

Second, a meaning is called universal if it can be predicated of many things, without requiring that those many things actually exist—as with the meaning of a seven-sided house. It is universal because its nature permits predication of many things, though there is no necessity that the many actually exist, or even that a single instance exist.

Third, a meaning is called universal if nothing in its conception prevents its being predicated of many things, even if some further reason or evidence prevents it—as in the meanings sun and earth. So far as these are understood as sun and earth, the mind is not prohibited from thinking that their meaning could be instantiated in many things; only a further argument or demonstration may establish that this is impossible. Such impossibility would arise from a reason external to the bare conception, not from the concept's nature as conceived.

Distinguish these propositions

  • Actual predicability over many existing individuals is different from possible predicability with no current individuals.
  • Conceptual non-contradiction of multiple instances is different from a demonstrated impossibility arising from an extrinsic metaphysical or cosmological thesis.
  • Therefore being a universal intelligible is not a property that requires many extramental instantiations or a real separated universal existing in a third realm.

A supposed “universal” that happens to have a single instance is not necessarily internally contradictory.

II. What a thing is in itself is not intrinsically either a mental universal or an existing individual

Arabic — V.1 p.196, through the passage on horseness

وقد يمكن أن يجمع هذا كله في أن هذا الكلي هو الذي لا يمنع نفس تصوره عن أن يقال على كثيرين. ويجب أن يكون الكلي المستعمل في المنطق وما أشبهه هو هذا. وأما الجزئي المفرد فهو الذي نفس تصوره يمنع أن يقال معناه على كثيرين، كذات زيد هذا المشار إليه، فإنه مستحيل أن تتوهم إلا له وحده. فالكلي من حيث هو كلي شيء، و«من حيث هو شيء تلحقه الكلية» شيء. فالكلي من حيث هو كلي هو ما يدل عليه أحد هذه الحدود، فإذا كان ذلك إنساناً أو فرساً فهناك معنى آخر غير معنى الكلية وهو الفرسية. فإن حد الفرسية ليس حد الكلية، ولا الكلية داخلة في حد الفرسية، فإن الفرسية لها حد لا يفتقر إلى حد الكلية لكن تعرض له الكلية. فإنه في نفسه ليس شيء من الأشياء البتة إلا الفرسية، فإنه في نفسه لا واحد ولا كثير ولا موجود في الأعيان ولا في النفس ولا في شيء من ذلك بالقوة ولا بالفعل على أن يكون داخلاً في الفرسية، بل من حيث هو فرسية فقط. بل الواحدية صفة تقترن إلى الفرسية، فتكون الفرسية مع تلك الصفة واحدة. كذلك للفرسية مع تلك الصفة صفات أخرى كثيرة داخلة عليها، فالفرسية بشرط أنها تطابق بحدها أشياء كثيرة تكون عامة، ولأنها مأخوذة بخواص وأعراض مشار إليها تكون خاصة. فالفرسية في نفسها فرسية فقط.

English — connected translation

All these senses can be brought together under the statement that a universal is something whose very conception does not prevent its being predicated of many things. This is the sense of “universal” required in logic and related disciplines.

A singular particular, by contrast, is something whose very conception prevents the relevant meaning from applying to many—for example the individual Zayd, this person to whom one points, whose particularity cannot be imagined as belonging to anyone other than himself.

Now a universal considered as universal is one thing, and the thing to which universality belongs is another. Universality as such is what the preceding definitions indicate. If that to which universality belongs is a human or a horse, there is another meaning besides the meaning of universality itself: namely, horseness. The definition of horseness is not the definition of universality; nor is universality included in the definition of horseness. Horseness possesses its own definition, which does not depend upon the definition of universality, although universality may belong to it.

For horseness in itself is nothing at all other than horseness. Considered in itself, it is neither one nor many, nor existent among external individuals nor existent in the soul, nor any of these things potentially or actually as a constituent within the meaning of horseness. It is simply horseness insofar as it is horseness.

Unity is, rather, a determination that can accompany horseness, so that horseness together with this determination is one. Likewise, many other determinations can accompany horseness together with unity. When horseness is taken under the condition of its definition corresponding to many things, it is general; but when taken together with individualising and demonstrable features, it is particular. Horseness in itself is simply horseness.

A crucial clarification

The sentence “horseness in itself is neither externally nor mentally existent” does not mean that an independently established nonexistent horseness inhabits a third ontological region. It means that the definition of horseness as such is not the definition of mental existence, external existence, universality, or individuality.

One may then ask how this nature is actually present in an intellectual act, and how it is individuated within actual horses. The formal consideration abstracts from those modes without creating a new concrete entity.

This matters for the broader debates about Muʿtazilī shayʾiyyat al-maʿdūm, conceptual being, nafs al-amr, and Ṣadrā's mental existence: none can simply be deduced from a compressed slogan “essence exists before existence.”

IV. How later Lāhījī develops the problem

In Chapter 1, Lāhījī distinguishes three questions:

  1. What the concept is about—a quiddity such as horseness, substance, unity or colour.
  2. How it exists mentally—a form in intellect, with a particular mode of intentional presence.
  3. The act of knowing as an actual quality of the existing intellect.

His Shawāriq I:50–52 explicitly resists the inference that the represented essence is literally an extramental substance present in someone's skull or that the real psychological act's categorical quality determines the nature represented.

The development is not merely chronological repetition. Lāhījī's formulation connects Avicenna's indifference of quiddity in itself to the logical distinction between what is understood and the mode in which the understanding inheres.

V. Comparing Aquinas without confusing terms

Aquinas's doctrine of intellective species as id quo intelligitur and the nature as primarily quod intelligitur is strongly relevant. Both frameworks reject identifying the extramental object of knowledge with the mental act's categorical status.

But neither provides an instant proof that Avicennian mental existence is numerically identical to Thomistic intentional presence. Their differences about the Active Intellect, the ontological category of mental representations, and how the nature attains universal intentio must be separately explored.

Further Thomistic question: An essence considered absolutely need not be actually instantiated. But how does the intellect's concept of a horse relate to the nature's actuality in the horse, and to the divine idea of the horse? Do not confuse those three levels with an independent third realm of nonexistent quiddities.

VI. Toward a rigorous theory of nafs al-amr

The objective factual status of true propositions (nafs al-amr) is broader than actual things presently existing in the extramental world. Mathematics, universal conditionals, modal claims, and truths about absence or non-existence make this obvious.

But one cannot solve the problem simply by postulating an extra universe where every true proposition is eternally stored. A suitable theory should distinguish:

  • What makes a statement true: a relation to being, essence, formal determination, mathematical structure, or the absence of what is denied;
  • Where the intellectual act is: a knower's mind;
  • What it signifies or represents: a possible, actual or impossible object;
  • Whether its truth requires an actual worldly referent: which varies by statement form;
  • What it means for the truth to hold independently of anyone's judgement.

The present Ibn Sīnā passage supports the distinctions needed to frame this enquiry. It does not state a late scholastic doctrine of nafs al-amr verbatim. That doctrine needs the Tajrīd and later logic commentaries (including those already studied in the separate GNAMMA and Asad Q. Ahmed projects).

VII. A connected contribution to the AT project

Logical universal → quiddity conceptually considered → actual existence under mental/extramental modes → individuation → cognition of the nature → truth of predication → nafs al-amr.

The order prevents these common inferences:

  • from conceivability to a third realm of real uninstantiated essences;
  • from mental existence to the existence of the understood object in the knower as a physical substance;
  • from one meaning shared across many to one numerically identical real object in them;
  • from the unity of an existential concept to the numerical identity of all created beings with God.

The next question is whether existence as a metaphysical actuality is really distinct from each quiddity, and what the cause does in conferring actuality. The subsequent making debate addresses that problem.

I. The original hylomorphic demonstration from division and continuity

Matn, gloss, and explanation must be kept distinct

The passage in Mawāqif VII:34 argues that corporeal extension is not by itself the whole of the body's reality. When one connected body is divided, the continuous unity is destroyed, yet something remains receptive of separation and connection.

Arabic lemma, p.34 — embedded matn

فان ثمة أمرا قابلا للاتصال تارة والانفصال أخرى ... وهذا الأمر الذي هو قابل للانفصال ومغاير للاتصال هو الذي نسميه بالهيولى.

English translation

There must be something capable at one time of connection and at another of separation. That which receives separation and is distinct from the connected condition is what we call prime matter (hayūlā).

Arabic lemma, p.34 — embedded explanation

فالقابل للاتصال والانفصال يغاير كلا منهما.

English translation

The underlying subject receptive of connection and separation is distinct from either of those determinations.

The argument has three steps:

  1. A connected body can become separated.
  2. The same receptivity must be distinguished from the opposed determinations of connection and separation, since what is destroyed cannot simply be the very subject that remains receptive.
  3. Therefore, beyond the corporeal form of extension considered as an actuality, a material principle is posited.

This is an intelligible proof for hylomorphism, not a verbal stipulation that matter exists. But the argument requires a substantive premise about continuity and persistence.

The first objection: why not corporeal form itself?

The later discussion (p.45) points out that the corporeal form of a body, while it entails some extension, need not entail one determinate magnitude. Perhaps the same corporeal actuality can receive varied magnitudes. If so, the argument has not demonstrated a second entity called prime matter.

Arabic — p.45

وجوابه أن الصورة الجسمية وإن كانت مستلزمة في الوجود والتعقل للمقدار إلا أنها لا تستلزم مقداراً مخصوصاً، فجاز أن تكون هي قابلة لتلك المقادير المختلفة فلا يثبت وجود أمر آخر.

English translation

The reply is that, although corporeal form involves magnitude both in being and in intelligibility, it does not entail any specific magnitude. It could therefore itself receive the differing magnitudes, and there would be no proof that some further principle exists.

Critical distinction: The exchange exposes a problem common to Avicennism and Thomism: what exactly persists when a body changes its dimensions, continuity, or substantial form? Establishing that some receptive subject persists does not by itself establish that the subject is pure potency rather than corporeal substance with some actuality.

II. The objection from substantial generation is not an automatic victory

A natural objection is that, in generation and corruption, one substantial form is taken off and another put on; a persisting matter must underlie those forms.

Arabic — p.45, layered text

والكون والفساد ... إذ لا بد فيهما من أمر يخلع صورة ويلبس أخرى وهو الهيولى.

English translation

Generation and corruption seem to require something that removes one form and receives another: that would be prime matter.

The counterargument questions this inference by suggesting that what changes are the specific substantial forms, and perhaps corporeal form could itself be the receptive subject. It adds that if the phenomenon of generation is already explained by assuming prime matter, using it to prove prime matter would be circular.

Arabic — p.45

على أنا نقول وجود هذه الأمور التي استعين بها مبنى على وجود الهيولى فيلزم الدور.

English translation

Moreover, we say that the existence of the very things appealed to in the proof rests on [the assumption of] prime matter; circular reasoning would follow.

A genuine late-kalām challenge

The kalām commentators are not merely saying “Aristotle asserts matter, therefore matter exists.” They are exposing a possible begging of the question in one traditional hylomorphic demonstration. A Thomist must answer with a non-circular argument from potency, substantial continuity, or change, rather than invoke inherited terminology.

III. The theologians' trilemma against matter — p.45

The display introduces what it identifies as a principal argument among theologians against prime matter. Matter either has location or it does not. If it has location independently, it appears already to be a body. If located through dependence on corporeal form, it looks like an attribute of form rather than the substrate receiving form. If it lacks location altogether, the relation of bodily form to it becomes obscure.

Arabic — three core clauses

أما أن يكون لها حصول في الحيز أو لا ... فإن كان ... على سبيل الاستقلال فجسم ... [وإن] على سبيل التبعية للصورة الجسمية فالهيولى حينئذ صفة حالة في الجسمية ... وإن لم يكن لها حصول في الحيز ... فلا تختص الجسمية بها.

English translation

Either [prime matter] exists in a spatial region or it does not. If it has spatial emplacement independently, it would already be a body. If it is spatially located only in dependence upon corporeal form, matter would instead appear to be an attribute inhering in corporeal form. If it has no spatial emplacement at all, it seems impossible for corporeal form to pertain specifically to it.

Voice warning: The page includes subordinate objections and corrective glosses (including questioning the inference from similarity of the two elements to their strict identity). The trilemma is therefore a reported theologians' argument within a layered edition, not demonstrated as al-Jurjānī's final position simply by its presence in his commentary.

Reply to be tested

The strongest Peripatetic reply would say that prime matter is not itself an actually spatial body, but is the potential material principle of a body, and that its emplacement belongs to it through the actual composite without making it an accident of corporeal form. The trilemma could therefore conflate “is in a place as a constituent of an actual body” with “has its own independently actual spatial extension.”

That reply is editorial reconstruction; it is not being attributed to a particular commentator until the response in a controlled primary edition is translated.

IV. Maqāṣid: why the concepts of nonexistent things require careful epistemology

The Sharḥ al-Maqāṣid pp.78–79 treats mental existence (wujūd dhihnī) in direct relation to universal affirmative judgements and things without extramental instances.

The argument from universals and affirmative judgements — p.78

Arabic

الثاني أن الكلي مفهوم وكل مفهوم ثابت ضرورة تميزه عند العقل ... وليس في الخارج لأن كل ما هو في الخارج مشخص فيكون في الذهن.

English translation

Second, the universal is understood, and everything understood has some established status, because it is distinguished by intellect. Yet it cannot be established as a universal outside the mind, since everything actual outside the mind is individuated. It must therefore be established mentally.

Arabic

الثالث أن من القضايا موجبة حقيقية وهي تستدعي وجود الموضوع ضرورة ... كقولنا كل عنقاء حيوان.

English translation

Third, some propositions are genuinely affirmative, and they seem to require the existence of a subject; yet that subject may have no actual extramental instance, as in “Every phoenix is an animal.”

A further point follows: sciences use universal judgements extending over all possible members, not just the presently actual specimens.

The opposing reply: truth need not mean an independent internal image

Arabic

وأعترض بأنا لا نسلم أن الإيجاب يقتضي وجود الموضوع ... بل اللازم هو تميز الموضوع والمحمول عند العقل بمعنى تصورهما.

English translation

An objection is raised: we do not concede that affirmative predication requires the actual being of the subject. Rather, it requires only that the subject and predicate be distinguished in the intellect, through their conception.

The objection then charges the advocate of mental existence with assuming what is disputed: if one argues that understanding a thing proves it is present as a form in the mind, one is already assuming the very theory under dispute.

Arabic — p.78

وهذا نفس المتنازع لأن القول بكون التعقل بالحصول في العقل إنما هو رأي القائلين بالوجود الذهني.

English translation

That is precisely what is in dispute: saying that intellection consists in something's obtaining within the intellect is already the view of those who affirm mental existence.

The response and its limit

The main response insists that intellectual apprehension requires a relation between knower and known and that pure nonbeing cannot serve as a term of such a relation. Since the known may lack extramental being, its suitable mode of establishment must be mental.

But p.79 then raises competing hypotheses: perhaps forms exist in Platonic separated Ideas, or are present in a separate Active Intellect, instead of being internally actual in the human subject. These possibilities must be addressed rather than silently excluded.

V. Three objections against mental existence — Maqāṣid p.79

The later part of p.79 presents three very precise objections.

1. If heat is in intellect, does the intellect become hot?

Arabic

لو كان تصور الشيء مستلزماً لحصوله في العقل لزم من تصور الحرارة والبرودة أن يكون الذهن حاراً وبارداً.

English translation

If conceiving a thing meant its obtaining in the intellect, conceiving heat and cold would make the intellect hot and cold.

2. If heaven is understood, must the whole immense cosmos be physically inside the mind?

Arabic

أنه يلزم أن تحصل السماوات بعظمها في العقل عند تعقلها الكل وفي الخيال عند تخيلها.

English translation

It would follow that the vast heavens themselves obtain inside the intellect when the intellect knows them, or inside the imagination when they are imagined.

3. Does a nonexistent thing become extramentally real simply because the mind is physically real?

Arabic

أنه يلزم من تعقل المعدومات وجودها في الخارج لكونها موجودة في العقل الموجود في الخارج.

English translation

It would follow that nonexistent things become extramentally existent when we understand them, because they would be inside an intellect that itself exists outside the mind.

The primary reply: differentiate a thing's real identity from its intellectual form

Arabic — p.79

والجواب أن مبنى الكل على عدم التفرقة بين الوجود المتأصل الذي به الهوية العينية وغير المتأصل الذي به الصورة العقلية.

English translation

The answer is that all three objections fail to distinguish rooted or proper existence, through which a thing has its real extramental identity, from the non-primary mode of existence proper to its intelligible form.

Arabic

وبالجملة فماهية الشيء أعني صورته العقلية مخالفة لهويته العينية في كثير من اللوازم.

English translation

In short, the quiddity of a thing understood as its intellectual form differs from its real external individual identity in many of their concomitants.

This is not the thesis that knowing a horse forces a physical horse into one's brain. It is a differentiation of what is signified, the intellectual form, and the body's external actuality.

A genuine historical route to Lāhījī

Lāhījī Shawāriq I:50–52 does not suddenly invent the distinction between an intellectual form and external reality. The Maqāṣid had already stated the core objection and reply in a scholastic form. Lāhījī develops additional distinctions concerning the particular psychic actuality of the knowing act, the represented object's categorical nature, and its mode of intentional existence.

The question for our historical narrative is thus not “who first invented mental existence?” but how the commentators refined the semantic and ontological relation between a mental representation, its content, and the actual known thing.

VI. Connections to the four causes and the AT comparison

Natural philosophy: The Mawāqif passage tests Avicennian matter/form theory at the level of its most fundamental proof, not just its terminology. An AT philosopher defending prime matter must respond to the trilemma without treating matter as a tiny hidden spatial body.

Epistemology: The Maqāṣid passage tests the intelligible representation without turning it into an extramental body or a mere arbitrary mental name. Aquinas's distinction between the species intelligibilis as that by which one knows and the actual object known provides a useful contrast.

Method: Both text families apply a common scholastic discipline: distinguish senses before deciding whether an objection defeats a doctrine. A modern synthesis should retain this method of determination, including its technical distinctions, rather than treat medieval kalām as a set of anti-Peripatetic slogans.
