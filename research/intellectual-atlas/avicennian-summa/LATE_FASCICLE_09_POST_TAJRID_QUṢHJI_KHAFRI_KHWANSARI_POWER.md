# Late-commentator fascicle 09 — Power, necessity and choosing: al-Ṭūsī → Qūshjī → al-Khafrī → Khwānsārī in a later *Tajrīd* commentary

**31 October 2026 · Arabic scholastic argument-chain translated with separately named layers**

**Direct witness:** Muḥammad Jaʿfar al-Astarābādī, *al-Barāhīn al-qāṭiʿa*, vol. II, printed pp. **96–99**, online [large transcription whose page marker 96 introduces the argument](https://lib.rafed.net/view.php?b_id=718&page=91&type=c_fbook). **This is the author directly read in this fascicle.** The text itself **reports or quotes** the distinct historical positions of the *Tajrīd* author Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī, al-Ḥillī's *Kashf al-murād*, ʿAlī Qūshjī's *Sharḥ al-Tajrīd* (cited there pp.310–311), Ghiyāth al-Dīn al-Khafrī (manuscript fol.5), and Āqā Jamāl al-Khwānsārī (via another gloss). Until those original publications/manuscripts are individually verified, the present document is **one directly read later author with a nested transmission chain**, not five independent source collations.

## 1. The challenge: if the cause is complete, can it choose? (p.96–97)

**Objection, in the direct text (working translation):** Divine power is understood as the possibility of both acting and refraining. But an effect of an agent must either be necessary or impossible: when the agent possesses every condition for effectiveness and is a complete cause, its effect necessarily proceeds, since a complete cause cannot exist without its effect. Without these conditions the effect is impossible. Thus there appears to be no interval in which doing and refraining are both possible, and divine power seems impossible.

**Ṭūsī's lemma, as embedded by Astarābādī:**

> ويمكن عروض الوجوب والإمكان للأثر بالاعتبارين

**Translation:** “Necessity and possibility may both apply to the effect under two different considerations.”

**Qūshjī's explanation, as *reported* by the later author:** The effect, considering the power and the agent's essence without the determining will, is **possible**; considering the will directed to the most fitting action, it becomes **necessary**. This is *necessity through choosing*, and such necessity does not negate choice but expresses its efficacy. A capable agent is one who, on willing to act, produces the act; on willing not to act, refrains. The effect is also impossible under the consideration that its conditions are absent. The three modal predicates do not contradict each other because the respect differs. The later text cites both Qūshjī and al-Ḥillī's *Kashf al-murād* for substantially this response, not an identical verbatim passage in each.

## 2. Al-Khafrī's objection to the reconciliation (p.97–98)

**Embedded objection, expressly attributed to al-Khafrī:** If divine will is *identical to the divine essence*, it cannot fail to be present. Then the necessity arising from such will is incompatible with “power” understood in the theologians' stronger sense of genuinely being able to do or leave undone. “Necessity through will” may coexist with the philosophers' **conditional** definition of choice (“if he wills he acts, and if he does not will he refrains”), but since the eternal divine will does in fact will, the effect proceeds eternally from the willing essence. A complete cause cannot be detached from its effect in time; how, then, could a will identical with the divine essence be separated temporally from its willing a created effect? The text demands a real solution rather than a merely verbal appeal to choice.

**Witness boundary:** the Astarābādī note cites al-Khafrī's *Ḥāshiya ʿalā Ilāhiyyāt Sharḥ al-Qūshjī*, folio 5, **as a manuscript lead**, not a newly inspected manuscript.

## 3. The later author's response and the Khwānsārī pressure (p.98)

**Astarābādī's own response:** The operative will producing an effect is an additional, newly arising *mashīʾa*, rather than precisely the essential attribute of divine knowledge. Consequently one may reject al-Khafrī's alleged incompatibility. Another solution emphasises the object's *being the best thing to create at a specified time*: the effect willed is a temporal thing, not an eternal thing, so its lack of eternity need not imply that the cause was incomplete. Further arguments refer to limits in the receptive object rather than limits in the agent.

The author then reports an objection associated through his notes with **Āqā Jamāl al-Khwānsārī**: if a possible thing's eternal existence is intrinsically impossible, why would its essence require absence only at one time rather than forever? Positing time-specific absence threatens to presuppose time itself. Here the **source layer is Astarābādī's report of a prior marginal controversy**. It is not yet textual evidence that Āqā Ḥusayn al-Khwānsārī (Jamāl's father) authored this objection; the two must never be conflated.

## 4. Further reply and rival reading (pp.98–99)

A further scholastic gloss (attributed in the text only by a title, “Sun of the Marginalists”) reformulates the problem from the **effect's own contingency**. Whether the cause's conditions are present or absent, the effect remains contingent *in itself*; it becomes necessary through a completed cause and impossible through the absence of required conditions. There is no contradiction, because intrinsic possibility excludes intrinsic necessity and intrinsic impossibility, but not **necessity or impossibility through another**. The gloss criticizes Qūshjī for framing the problem too strongly in terms of an effect's possible **proceeding** rather than the effect's intrinsic modal status.

**Synthesis:** this is concrete evidence that post-*Tajrīd* commentators did not merely repeat a formula “a possible effect becomes necessary through a complete cause.” They contested **which modal respect** is relevant to genuine freedom, whether divine will is identical with divine essence, whether a temporal effect is a different intentional object from an eternal one, and whether one can satisfy a theological requirement of alternate possibilities by a conditional definition of power.
