# Late-commentator fascicle 03 — Lāhījī, *Gawhar-i murād*, II.3.8: divine willing and agency by satisfaction

**31 October 2026 · Persian primary source → working English translation**

**Witness**: *Gawhar-i murād*, Article II, Part 3, **Chapter 8**, printed Persian pp.307–309 in the Nashr-i Sāya edition: [307](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/17438/307?lang=ar), [308](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/17438/308?lang=ar), [309](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/17438/309?lang=ar). Printed notes list A/B/C variants; these are reports in the edition, not newly inspected manuscripts.

## Heading and p.307 — agency without self-perfection

> فاعليّت خداى تعالى به علم و اراده است ، نه به طبع

**Heading:** “On what mode of agency the Necessary Existent exercises with respect to things.”

All scholars agree that God's agency operates through knowledge and will, not through unconscious nature. Philosophers and critical theologians agree, moreover, that divine knowledge and will, like God's other attributes, are identical with the divine essence, not additional things. And they agree that God acts for no aim, profit, perfection or preference returning to the divine essence—far exalted is God above this. They disagree, however, whether God can intend such things **not for himself but for benefiting another**. The philosophers maintain that although divine acts contain the fullest goodness and the greatest benefit to creatures, God transcends an intention aimed at those benefits. They argue that intention concerns something in view of which an agent is better off, so that by acting the agent acquires a perfection which the agent lacked before; such acquisition cannot apply to a true first cause.

## p.308 — physician, carpenter, *riḍā* versus *qaṣd*

No genuine cause becomes more perfect through its effect, for the effect's perfection comes from the cause: how could it in turn perfect the cause? Intention can proceed from an agent acting by will that is not the effect's **true existential cause**. Instead such an agent uses intention to make matter ready for an effect to receive existence from its true cause. Thus a physician prepares the patient's body for the influx of health from the **Giver of Health and Life**, and a carpenter prepares wood for the influx of the form of a bed from the **Giver of Forms**. In short, the agency of beings that act through animal willing is, strictly considered, only the moving of matter. It is appropriate to ascribe *intention* to these agents, not to the true cause. In a genuine existential cause, willing resolves into *riḍā* (approval or satisfaction), not *qaṣd* (purposeful intending). Consequently the Necessary Existent is an agent through *riḍā*, not through *qaṣd*, and the same holds for the true intermediary causes, namely separate intellects and celestial souls.

The majority of theologians, by contrast, describe God as acting through intention. Lāhījī criticises the Ashʿarites for rejecting purposes and denying the necessity that divine actions embody good and benefits, while the Muʿtazilites affirm ends directed toward others and deny that benefiting another necessarily completes the agent.

## p.309 — an attempted reconciliation

The more exact analysis suggests that the Muʿtazilī position on benefit can be reduced to what the philosophers mean when they say divine works include wise purposes and benefits. Whether these are **intended** or **approved** may then be a merely verbal difference: Muʿtazilites do not restrict the technical meaning of intending as the philosophers do, and the philosophers call divine intention *riḍā* under their terminology. This reconciliation is particularly plausible in the case of those Muʿtazilites who affirm some degree of causal necessitation: the will necessarily entails the act and the act does not exist until necessary. That need not conflict with the philosophical account of acts as consequences of the goodness of the Necessary Existent's perfect essence. In that restricted comparison there may be no substantive disagreement.

## Apparatus — what cannot be collapsed

1. **Not unqualified determinism.** Lāhījī denies that God's agency is blind natural compulsion, while accepting a type of causal necessitation consonant with the divine will under the *riḍā* model. Whether that leaves genuine alternative possibilities for creation needs a separate modal demonstration.
2. **Two uses of “will.”** *Qaṣd* is deliberative intention directed to an end improving the agent; *riḍā* is an approving, non-acquisitive relation to effects. Translation as the undifferentiated “volition” would erase the substantive terminology.
3. **A crucial later-Avicennian continuity.** The physician and carpenter prepare material dispositions; the true cause confers actuality. Compare Ibn Sīnā [*Ilāhiyyāt* VI.2](PRIMARY_TRANSLATION_FASCICLE_B_AVICENNA_VI2.md) and [IX.5](PRIMARY_TRANSLATION_FASCICLE_D_AVICENNA_IX5.md).
4. **School generalizations are Lāhījī's historical characterisations**, not a modern proof that every Ashʿarī or Muʿtazilī held the position exactly as stated. In particular the closing reconciliation concerns only certain Muʿtazilites.
5. **Boundary:** the entire brief Chapter 8 spans pp.307–309 in this witness. This new working translation closely follows the full connected argument, but no direct manuscript collation or independent verification of every cited theologian has been attempted.

**Result:** a non-Ṣadrian late Avicennian account of **divine and intermediate agency by approval** that preserves natural preparatory agents, separates them from existential causes, and proposes a conditional rapprochement with Muʿtazilī teleology.
