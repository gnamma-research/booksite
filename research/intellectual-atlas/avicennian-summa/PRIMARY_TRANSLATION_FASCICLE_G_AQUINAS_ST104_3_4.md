## Article 3 — Whether God can reduce something to nothingness

### Objections

**1. [32775]** It seems that God cannot reduce anything to nothingness. For Augustine says in the *Eighty-Three Questions* that God is not the cause of a thing's tending toward nonexistence. But that would be the case if God brought a creature back to nothing. Therefore God cannot bring anything to nothingness.

**2. [32776]** Further, God is the cause of the existence of things through his goodness. As Augustine says in *On Christian Teaching*, we exist insofar as God is good. But God cannot fail to be good. Therefore he cannot bring it about that things fail to exist—which he would do if he reduced them to nothingness.

**3. [32777]** Further, if God were to reduce a thing to nothingness, this would have to occur through some action. But this is impossible, since every action terminates in some being. Even a corrupting agent's action terminates in something generated, since the generation of one thing is the corruption of another. Thus God cannot reduce anything to nothingness.

### On the contrary — [32778]

Scripture says in Jeremiah 10: “Correct me, Lord, yet with judgment and not in your anger, lest you bring me to nothing.”

### I respond — [32779]

Some have maintained that God produced things in being by acting from **necessity of nature**. If that were correct, God would be unable to reduce anything to nothingness, just as he cannot be changed from his own nature. But, as already established, this position is false and altogether contrary to the Catholic faith, which confesses that God produced things in being through his **free will**. Thus the Psalm says that the Lord did whatever he willed.

That God communicates existence to a creature therefore depends upon his will. Nor does God conserve things in existence otherwise than by **continuously bestowing existence upon them**, as previously explained. Consequently, just as before things existed he was able not to communicate existence to them and thus not to make them, so after things have been made he can withhold this influx of being from them, and thus they would cease to be. That is what it means for God to reduce them to nothingness.

### Replies

**To objection 1 — [32780].** Nonbeing has no essential cause, since nothing can be a cause except insofar as it is a being, and being, speaking essentially, is the cause of being. God therefore cannot be the cause of a thing's tending toward nonbeing. Rather, the creature has that from itself, insofar as it is **from nothing**. But God can be the cause of things' being reduced to nothingness **accidentally**, by withdrawing his action from them.

**To objection 2 — [32781].** God's goodness is the cause of things, but not by necessity of nature, since divine goodness does not depend on created things; it causes them **through free will**. Thus, just as God was able not to produce things without any diminution of his goodness, so he can fail to conserve things in being without any injury to that goodness.

**To objection 3 — [32782].** If God reduced a thing to nothingness, this would not be through an action, but through his **ceasing to act**.

## Article 4 — Whether anything is actually reduced to nothingness

### Objections

**1. [32783]** It seems that something is reduced to nothingness. An end corresponds to a beginning. But at the beginning there was nothing except God. Therefore things will be led back to that end, so that nothing will remain except God; creatures will thus be annihilated.

**2. [32784]** Further, every creature has a finite power. But no finite power extends to infinity: accordingly Aristotle argues in *Physics* VIII that a finite power cannot move for infinite time. No creature, then, can endure forever. Consequently, at some time it will be reduced to nothingness.

**3. [32785]** Further, forms and accidents have no matter as one of their parts. But they sometimes cease to exist. It seems, then, that they are reduced to nothingness.

### On the contrary — [32786]

Ecclesiastes 3 says: “I have learned that all the works which God has made endure forever.”

### I respond — [32787]

Among the things God does with regard to creatures, some occur in accordance with the **natural course** of things; others he brings about miraculously, outside the order instilled in creatures, as will be explained below. What God will do according to the natural order placed in things can be considered from their own natures. Miraculous actions, meanwhile, are ordered to the manifestation of grace, as the Apostle says in 1 Corinthians 12, “To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for some benefit,” before proceeding to speak of the working of miracles.

The natures of creatures, however, show that none is reduced to nothingness. For creatures are either **immaterial**, so that in them there is no potentiality for nonbeing, or **material**, so that at least matter always remains, since matter is incorruptible as the underlying subject of generation and corruption.

Nor does reducing something to nothingness belong to the manifestation of grace, since divine power and goodness are shown more clearly through the **conservation** of things in being. We must therefore say simply that **nothing at all is reduced to nothingness**.

### Replies

**To objection 1 — [32788].** That things were brought into existence after not having existed manifests the power of the producer. But their being reduced to nothingness would impede this manifestation, since God's power is chiefly shown by conserving things in being. Thus the Apostle says in Hebrews 1 that God sustains all things by the word of his power.

**To objection 2 — [32789].** The creature's power for existing is only **receptive**, whereas the active power belongs to God, from whom the influx of being proceeds. Therefore the infinite endurance of things follows the **infinity of divine power**. Nevertheless some things have an appointed ability to persist only for a definite time, insofar as an opposing agent can prevent their reception of the influx of being; their finite power cannot resist that contrary agent indefinitely but only for a limited period. Therefore creatures without contraries, despite possessing finite power, endure forever.

**To objection 3 — [32790].** Forms and accidents are not complete beings, because they do not subsist; rather, each is a **constituent aspect of a being**, being called a being insofar as something is through it. Even though they cease, they are not altogether reduced to nothingness in the mode of existence proper to them. This is not because a part of them remains, but because they remain in the **potency of matter or of the subject**.

**End of *Summa theologiae* I, Question 104.** The next question (105) concerns God's action upon creatures, including the ability to move matter immediately to form and God's operation in every agent.

## Interpretive adjudication: Aquinas and Avicenna

1. **Continuing dependence and free will are logically distinct.** *ST* I q.104 aa.1–2 gives the demonstration that being depends continuously upon a cause and permits the activity of intermediate conserving causes. Article 3 adds the explicitly further thesis that God *could have refrained* from creation or conservation because his effect depends on **free will**, not necessitation by nature. Avicenna's VI.1–2 argues for continual dependence, but it cannot therefore be identified without further premises with Aquinas' free act of creation.
2. **Possibility versus actuality of annihilation.** Aquinas affirms the **possibility** that God could withhold being (a.3), while denying that any creature actually is or will be reduced to nothingness (a.4). Mixing these two would create a contradiction absent from his text.
3. **No positive causal process produces nonbeing.** Both Avicenna VI.1's distinction between making existence and the incidental feature “after nonexistence,” and Aquinas a.3's account of annihilation by withdrawal, recognize a meaningful distinction between an act of bestowing being and descriptions of nonbeing.
4. **Scope of historical natural philosophy.** The natural incorruptibility of celestial bodies in q.104 a.4 is a historical Aristotelian model, not an astronomical claim we adopt. The philosophical contrast between receptive finite capacity and unlimited primary power must be evaluated separately.
5. **Forms and accidents.** Aquinas distinguishes the loss of a form from the complete annihilation of a *subsistent being*. This complicates readings of prime matter, substantial change, and the unity of a generated composite in the corresponding Avicennian and Lāhījī passages.
