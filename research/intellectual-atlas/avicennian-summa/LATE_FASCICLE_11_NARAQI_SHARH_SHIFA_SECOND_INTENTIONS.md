# Late-commentator fascicle 11 — Narāqī's *Sharḥ Ilāhiyyāt al-Shifāʾ*: second intentions, being and manuscript-level negation

**31 October 2026 · Arabic primary commentary → new English translation**

**Direct witness:** Muḥammad Mahdī al-Narāqī, *Sharḥ al-Ilāhiyyāt min Kitāb al-Shifāʾ*, vol. I, edition of Ḥāmid Nājī Iṣfahānī, Qum, Congress of the Narāqī Scholars, 1380 Solar Hijrī; [printed p.106](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/15109/138?lang=ar), [p.107](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/15109/139?lang=ar), [a separate textual note at p.110](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/15109/142?lang=ar). The **connected argument unit** is pp.106–107; the textual note at p.110 is a separate later anchor, not hidden inside the translated continuous sequence. The online witness is a modern edited text; no fresh manuscript images collated.

## A. What is a common metaphysical attribute? p.106

> والإعتباريات ليس لها وجود في الخارج وإنّما وجودها وجود ماينتزع عنه

**Reported distinction and Narāqī's analysis:** Some distinguish general metaphysical attributes from other accidents as follows. The first are actual beings, whether substances or accidents, bodily or mental. The latter are descriptions whose existence is **identical with the existence of their subjects**, even though the mind distinguishes and defines them by abstracting them from existing things. They are *iʿtibārī* and *intizāʿī*, things of consideration and abstraction: they have no extra reality detached from what they describe. By contrast a real accident has its existence within a subject but also differs from the subject's identity. This must be qualified, Narāqī says, because the division will not work straightforwardly for unity and plurality as understood by Ibn Sīnā, nor for all second intentions, some of which are indeed conceptual abstractions. The distinction needs a stricter criterion.

**Objection:** Is the proposed criterion merely a way of naming the categories without clarifying the difference in their realities?

**Answer:** A general metaphysical attribute belongs to an existent **as existent**, without requiring it to be prepared for a special kind of further accident. A second intention, however, is intelligible **only as predicated of a prior intelligible object** (*maʿqūl*), not simply of being as such.

## B. Why metaphysics is the home of this inquiry, p.107

Narāqī continues: a second intention cannot be understood except as an attribute of another concept, whether or not that attribute is also maximally general. Accordingly general metaphysical attributes and second intentions overlap, but neither is simply identical with the other in every respect. He then explains an Avicennian statement that some things must be **defined and established in the soul**: either their boundaries and intellectual truth must be made known by conception and assent, or a well-defined meaning must attain determinacy in thought through investigation. Such notions are not self-evident; they occur as common principles in many sciences, yet none of the particular sciences has them as an exclusive subject or proper attributes. Their definition and truth therefore belong to the science with the most general subject, **being qua being**.

**Textual note**: the p.107 main edition marks a variant `تصديقنا` in witness F against `تصديقاً`; its logical impact is slight, though it changes the grammatical construction of conceptual knowledge and assent.

## C. A genuine printed witness variant on negation, p.110 (separate supplementary locus)

> ثمّ في بعض النّسخ وجدت لفظة « لا » قبل « يكون »

Narāqī reports directly that **some copies contain the word “not” before “is/exists.”** He takes it likely to be a scribal slip but explains how the sentence can be understood if the variant is retained: the distinction would then concern inherent versus extrinsic properties of substances, not the same comparison under the first reading. This is a textual-critical report **by the historical commentator**, not a newly discovered manuscript variant of our own. The attribution should remain exactly this strong: *Narāqī reports* that copies differ and proposes a possible reconciliation.

## Apparatus and comparison

- **Ontological subtlety:** *iʿtibārī* need not mean “false” or “unfounded”; an abstract property may be grounded in the reality of its subject without constituting an additional independently existing thing.
- **Why this matters for the project:** later Avicennian metaphysics inherits Avicenna's distinction between being as such and the objects of special sciences, while refining the relation of **second intentions**, real accidents and general attributes. The distinction is central to *nafs al-amr*, metaphysical predication, and ontology of relation.
- **Two kinds of “in mind”:** a concept's being an object of second-order attribution is not identical to the act of imagining it; conflation would render the distinction between conceptual and external accidents uninformative.
- **Attribution discipline:** Narāqī reports a prior account using `وقيل` (“it was said”), then criticises and qualifies it. Do not assign the whole reported definition to Narāqī without qualification.
- **Scope:** pp.106–107 are translated as a connected bounded argument unit; p.110 supplies a separate verifiable textual-critical note. No independent manuscript collation. The historical work as published remains unfinished.

**Result:** an original Narāqī commentary witness sharpening the metaphysical/logical distinction between what belongs to **being qua being** and what belongs to intelligibles considered as such.
