Late-commentator fascicle 04 — Lāhījī, Gawhar-i murād, II.3.4, opening argument: One, first intellect, and intermediaries

31 October 2026 · Persian → working English, pp.290–293

Work: ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Lāhījī, Gawhar-i murād, Article II, Part 3, Chapter 4, “The first emanated being and the order of existents.” Textual boundary: the opening pp.290–293, not the entirety of Chapter 4 (whose later discussion of transmitted sayings continues thereafter). Digital Persian print: 290, 291, 292, 293, Nashr-i Sāya, Tehran.

p.290 — why only one immediate effect proceeds from the true One

از واحد حقيقى ... صادر نتواند شد بالذّات ... مگر واحدى

Lāhījī reports the philosophers' position: from the true One, which contains no plurality either real or conceptual, only one effect can proceed directly at a single level, insofar as it is a true One. To produce a determinate effect rather than any other possible effect, the cause would need some distinguishing respect. In the absolutely simple One, such a distinction cannot be an intrinsic part or added property, for that would import plurality. It must be identical to the essence. One and the same respect identical to the essence cannot directly explain two genuinely different effects; if it could, the two effects would fail to differ, or that single respect would be composite. The only coherent alternative is that one effect follows the cause essentially and the other by the mediation of an accidental or derivative respect. Since the Necessary Existent is absolutely one, the first immediate effect must itself be one.

Reported variant: p.290 n.2 B/C شركت in place of مركّب; the resulting sentence should be checked in the Persian print image before choosing a definitive edition. We translate the main text's “composite.”

p.291 — the first effect must be an intellect

The first effect must have a simple extra-mental quiddity, and neither its existence nor its constitution may depend on some still earlier effect: otherwise that earlier effect would be the first. Only an intellectual substance meets the conditions. Accidents require substances; body is constituted of matter and form; form needs matter for individuation, matter needs form for subsistence; and soul requires bodily preparedness either inherent (as in the celestial case) or newly acquired (as in human souls). None is first except a separate intellect. Once the first intellect proceeds, it contains derivative plurality, not as several immediate first effects but through its own concomitants. Its particular identity or existence is first; it has quiddity, intrinsic possibility relative to that quiddity, necessity through the first cause, cognition of itself, and cognition of its first cause. Hence six considerations arise: existence and quiddity (the same externally, distinct in thought); possibility and self-knowledge relative to itself; necessity through another and knowledge of its cause relative to its cause.

Variant note: p.291 n.1 B reads استبعادى فطرى for استعداد ذاتى فطرى; n.2 C بالعروض for بالعرض, n.3 B مخصوصه for the longer form. The nature of the celestial soul's “preparedness” merits review.

p.292 — multiple effects through nonindependent respects

The respects belonging to the first intellect by relation to its first cause are nobler; through such respects proceeds the second intellect, and through the lesser respects the first sphere. The sphere itself has a nobler soul/form and a lower bodily matter. These respects are not separately existing external things, but they have a foundation in nafs al-amr: explanatory considerations differ in ways relevant to causation. Nor do such concomitants require a distinct act of making; their production is derivative upon the production of what has them. Hence nothing like immediate plurality follows from the absolutely One. The same reasoning proceeds from the second intellect to the second sphere and third intellect, and so on until the ninth gives rise to the tenth. The tenth intellect, with celestial cooperation, provides the material basis for sublunary things capable of acquiring various successive dispositions.

Variant note: B omits افتاده مر; A/C give plural توانند instead of singular تواند, affecting agreement but not the theory that dependent respects need no independent making.

p.293 — affirming the single divine existential agent despite intermediaries

The tenth intellect supplies, according to differing dispositions, the elemental forms of simple and composite generated things. But from the proposition that multiple effects require multiple causal respects, it does not follow conversely that every cause with multiple respects must produce equally many effects. Universal affirmative statements do not simply reverse; moreover the intellects do not all possess the same specific reality. There is therefore no entailment to infinitely many intellects and spheres.

The Rule of the One and the hierarchy of procession, Lāhījī says, need not contradict religion or divine will. Arbitrariness and the preponderance of an alternative without a determinant are impossible; the will's attachment to a specified alternative also requires a specifying respect. Nor does affirming intermediaries compromise the unity of divine agency. The intermediate cause does not independently give existence or produce the effect as its true creative agent: it mediates the arrival of the effusion of existence from the remote cause to the effect. Thus the critical philosophers who affirm necessary intermediaries may nevertheless say: “There is no agent conferring existence except God” (lā muʾaththira fī l-wujūd illā Allāh). Lāhījī then connects the theory with the report “The first thing God created was intellect”; the transmitted-text interpretation proceeds beyond the portion translated here.

Source note: p.293 includes a reference to Biḥār al-anwār I:97 for the reported hadith; this is a religious gloss in Lāhījī's own discussion and is not an independently validated prophetic/Imāmī isnād in this edition.

Critical interpretive results

  • Important correction to the initial description “Active Intellect is a giver of existence”: p.293 explicitly distinguishes an intermediate conduit of the effusion of existence from the one true existential agent. This is not a denial that the tenth intellect bestows forms at its level. Compare Chapter 6; separate higher existential agency, separate intelligible mediation and material disposition.
  • **Importance for nafs al-amr:** explanatory causal respects are not independent external existents but are nonetheless nafs al-amrī, i.e. not arbitrary inventions of the mind. This is a direct Persian witness to later Avicennian use of a real-founded, not simply independently subsistent, explanatory order.
  • Difference from Aquinas remains real: both can affirm that contingent existence is sourced from the First, while disagreeing about necessary procession and divine freedom. Compare ST I q.104 aa.3–4.
  • Critical limit: This is a translation of pp.290–293, a coherent opening argument, not the rest of Chapter 4, and does not independently verify the printed variant witnesses.

Late-commentator fascicle 01 — Lāhījī, Gawhar-i murād, II.3.6: the tenth intellect, souls and natural forms

31 October 2026 · Persian primary source → new working English translation

Author: ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Lāhījī (d. 1661/2). Work: Gawhar-i murād (گوهر مراد), Article II, Book/Part 3, Chapter 6. Witness: searchable Persian edition, Nashr-i Sāya, Tehran, print pp. 301–303, p.301, p.302, p.303. The HTML includes abbreviated notes reporting readings of witnesses الف، ب، ج, but their manuscript identities and stemma have not been independently verified. The complete original Persian is at the linked pages; brief Persian anchors are printed below.

Heading, printed p.301

فصل ششم ... در بيان آنكه علّت فيضان نفوس ناطقه و ساير صور نوعيّهء مركبات ، عقل عاشر است

Translation: “Chapter Six, on showing that the cause of the emanation of rational souls and of the other specific forms of composite things is the tenth intellect, also called the Active Intellect.”

p.301 — why a single species has multiple individuals

Rational souls, as already established, are many in number but agree in specific reality. On account of their number they cannot all emanate immediately, at one level, from the Necessary Existent, who is an absolutely true One. And on account of their agreement in species, they cannot emanate in a sequence in which some souls are causes of other souls, for that would introduce a gradation in an essential determination: if the quiddity is the same in all individuals, causal priority among them would introduce an essential priority that is not present in the species. The situation differs for intellects that are distinct in their realities. Rational souls likewise cannot emanate from a celestial body, for the soul is more noble than a body, and the nobler is not the effect of the baser. Nor can they emanate from the first effect: if its many respects differed in reality, what issued through each would differ in specific reality; if those respects were of one reality, they could not account for many individuals without matter.

Apparatus: at p.301 n.1, صدورشان (“their procession”) has صدورش in B and C. This does not change the argument's contrast between one species and multiple rational souls.

p.302 — preparedness and individuating bodies

Many individuals of a single species come about only through matter and material accidents, which are absent from the first effect. The same problem excludes the other separate intellects preceding the tenth. Although each of these intellects is associated with a sphere containing matter, celestial matter does not receive generation and corruption and therefore cannot be responsible for a numerical plurality within one species. The tenth intellect, by contrast, is the source of the matter of generated things. Thus a single respect in it can produce the species of rational soul while its individuals multiply through matters with different dispositions arising from different temperaments. The source of the rational soul must accordingly be an intellect also responsible for sublunary matter. The individuality of each soul is derived from matter made ready by a particular disposition—that is, the body. If the existence of an intellectual principle for each sphere is otherwise established, this principle will be the tenth intellect. Lāhījī asks whether the present argument itself can establish the entire number of intellects and says that, on close examination, it can, as Ibn Sīnā argued in al-Mabdaʾ wa-l-maʿād.

Variants: p.302 n.1 B,C read معلولات اوّل for معلول اوّل; n.2 witness A omits نيز; n.3 A has باشند; n.4 C adds كه; n.5 B,C omit امّا.

p.303 — a hierarchy, not separate species-lords

The different dispositions of the matter of generated things, which account for the numerical multiplication of rational souls, arise with the assistance of celestial motions and mixtures. The corresponding intellect must therefore be placed at the level of the lunar sphere, the last of the spheres. It cannot be the first effect, since other spheres precede it; nor can it be the product of the sphere, since it is nobler than the sphere. It must proceed from a further intellect, and the same argument may be repeated until it reaches an intellect ordered with the first sphere and proceeding, along with that sphere, from the first intellect. The familiar ordered series of ten intellects follows.

Once the tenth intellect has been established as the source of rational souls, it is likewise the source of the different species of generated things, through its different causal respects and the different dispositions of receptive matter. Specific diversity belongs to diverse causal respects; numerical diversity within a species belongs to diverse dispositions. Since the rational soul is the noblest of generated species, it derives through the noblest respect; other composites proceed through other respects. What the Illuminationists call a “lord of the species” (rabb al-nawʿ)—the source that oversees and pours out each species' perfections—can thus be referred to the tenth intellect under its various respects. There is no need to posit additional horizontally arranged separate intellects, one for every natural species.

Variants: p.303 notes 1–5 record B براى for بنابر, A اوّل for معلول, A و for پس, B omission of انواع, and B به ترتيب for به تربيت. The last variant affects whether “ordering” or “nurturing” is the stated relation, so the main translation retains the base's stronger nurturing formulation.

Editorial adjudication

What the text demonstrates: a named late Safavid post-Tajrīd philosopher explicitly retains the separate tenth intellect as the bestower of human rational souls and other natural specific forms, and uses matter's differentiated istiʿdādāt to explain distinct individuals. He further rejects the need to multiply species-lords horizontally. This is a first-hand textual answer to the earlier open question whether Lāhījī had abandoned a separate Giver of Forms: he had not, at least here.

What it does not show: that the rational soul is intrinsically material, that every late commentator agrees, that Lāhījī denies real natural agent efficacy, or that the ten-intellect astronomy is empirically credible. In particular, later Chapter 7 distinguishes sustaining existence from time-specific material preparation.

Coverage: the source's entire Chapter 6 is short and spans exactly printed pp.301–303; this translation follows its argument paragraph by paragraph, with selectively reported printed variants, not manuscript autopsy. The edition is a new English translation, not an independently established Persian critical text.

Late-commentator fascicle 02 — Lāhījī, Gawhar-i murād, II.3.7: how temporal events depend on an eternal cause

31 October 2026 · Persian primary source → new working English translation

Work: ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Lāhījī, Gawhar-i murād, Article II, Part 3, Chapter 7, “On the cause of the origination of events and the connection of temporal events with the eternal.” Witness: Nashr-i Sāya Persian text pp. 304–306: 304, 305, 306. Printed notes record variant sigla A, B, C but no manuscript images have been collated here.

p.304 — two different grounds of causal dependence

سبب حاجت ممكن به علت ، امكان است ... نه حدوث

Translation: “The cause of the contingent being's need for a cause is its contingency, not its temporal origination.”

As already established, the ground of a contingent being's need for a cause is possibility—this is the doctrine of most philosophers and the critical investigators among theologians—not temporal beginning, as another group of theologians maintains. “Cause” in this first claim means what bestows existence and makes existence prevail over nonexistence. In truth, the character of possibility is the ground of that dependence, whether the possible being begins in time or not. By a “temporally originated thing” here we mean something whose being is preceded by a time in which it did not exist, as in everyday events. By an “eternal” possible thing we mean something not so preceded, even if its being follows absolute nonbeing in an order other than time, as in the traditional example of the celestial sphere. This pair of terms must not be confused with the earlier discussion of origination and eternity of the world: what is “eternal” in the present temporal sense can nonetheless be originated in the earlier essential sense. All possible things are originated in that latter sense, but only some in the present temporal sense. Hence all possible things, temporal or not, need the being-giving cause equally. A temporal event has an additional need.

p.305 — mubqiya and muḥditha; why motion matters

Its additional need is for a cause of origination (ʿilla muḥditha), meaning what accounts for the fact that the existence-bestowing cause favors its existence at this particular segment of time, rather than an earlier segment. Otherwise this time would be selected without a selector, just as without a cause bestowing existence, being rather than nonbeing would prevail without a determinant. A possible being with no preceding time needs no such additional cause.

Scholars therefore call what makes existence itself prevail the conserving cause (ʿilla mubqiya), since the contingent thing needs it throughout its continuance, and what determines the specific time the originating cause (ʿilla muḥditha), needed only for the event's temporal inception. The temporally determining cause must itself become operative at the event's time; otherwise the effect would fail to accompany its complete cause. Repeating the question for every new cause threatens an infinite regress of newly arisen causes. There must therefore be something that is new in one respect yet stable in another: motion, which is stable with respect to its own nature but constantly renewed in relation to the boundaries of its course. Here Lāhījī's model presupposes a motion without a temporal beginning in this specific sense.

Printed variants: B omits چون at p.305 n.1, B,C omit گويند n.2, A has نماند versus باقى نمانده n.3. These do not alter the explicit distinction between conserving and time-specifying causes.

p.306 — the gradual acquisition of preparedness

In this cosmological account the required continuous motion is celestial motion. It is by that motion that a temporally originated thing can be the effect of the eternal Necessary Existent without the effect being delayed after a completed sufficient cause; thus temporal things are connected with the eternal. Celestial movement specifies the time of an event when the event has a matter or underlying subject whose preparedness for it grows gradually through celestial movements and configurations. When that matter or subject has become fully ready, the event's existence necessarily flows to it from the giver of existence. Complete preparedness is thus the proximate cause determining the time of its occurrence, while celestial motions and configurations are remoter causes. Accordingly, a temporal event is preceded both by motions/configurations and by matter or subject. This explains the philosophers' maxim that every originated thing is preceded by matter and duration. “Duration” means time measured by celestial motion; “matter” is used broadly, embracing both prime matter (hayūlā) and an underlying subject of accidents.

Printed variants: A omits به (p.306 n.1), reads حادثى for حادث (n.2), استعدادى for استعداد (n.3); B موضوعى for موضوع (n.4), تخصيص for تخصّص (n.5), and omits از مادّه به معنى (n.6). The n.5 change makes the proximate cause's act of selecting the event's time more explicit; either reading preserves the distinction.

Philosophical apparatus

  1. Three causal tasks must be distinguished: (i) giving a possible thing existence, (ii) conserving it while it exists, and (iii) specifying the exact moment when a temporally beginning event comes to be. Lāhījī's p.305 mubqiya/muḥditha pair refers to (i)/(ii) versus (iii), not to a denial of real natural effects.
  2. Crucial relation to Avicenna: compare Ilāhiyyāt VI.1–2 and VI.2. Lāhījī adopts a structured Avicennian resolution of temporal novelties under an eternal source; he does not simply replace the natural world with instantaneous creation.
  3. Link to form-bestowal: Gawhar, Chapter 6 explicitly assigns natural forms to the tenth intellect. Chapter 7 specifies how a new form appears at this time: material preparedness is completed and an existential gift arrives. The combined account is strikingly close in structure to Ibn Sīnā Ilāhiyyāt IX.5.
  4. Historical limits: the celestial mechanics belongs to premodern cosmology and is not empirically vindicated by translation; the metaphysical distinctions of temporal determination, existential dependence and causal priority can be analysed independently.
  5. Source boundary: this is a continuous, short Chapter 7 as printed on pp.304–306, translated with selected reported print variants; it is not an edition of the entire Persian work or an original manuscript collation.

Result: Direct Persian evidence for a mature later Avicennian account of the relation of temporal effects to eternal causes, with a precise distinction between existential and temporal causal explanation.

Late-commentator fascicle 03 — Lāhījī, Gawhar-i murād, II.3.8: divine willing and agency by satisfaction

31 October 2026 · Persian primary source → working English translation

Witness: Gawhar-i murād, Article II, Part 3, Chapter 8, printed Persian pp.307–309 in the Nashr-i Sāya edition: 307, 308, 309. Printed notes list A/B/C variants; these are reports in the edition, not newly inspected manuscripts.

Heading and p.307 — agency without self-perfection

فاعليّت خداى تعالى به علم و اراده است ، نه به طبع

Heading: “On what mode of agency the Necessary Existent exercises with respect to things.”

All scholars agree that God's agency operates through knowledge and will, not through unconscious nature. Philosophers and critical theologians agree, moreover, that divine knowledge and will, like God's other attributes, are identical with the divine essence, not additional things. And they agree that God acts for no aim, profit, perfection or preference returning to the divine essence—far exalted is God above this. They disagree, however, whether God can intend such things not for himself but for benefiting another. The philosophers maintain that although divine acts contain the fullest goodness and the greatest benefit to creatures, God transcends an intention aimed at those benefits. They argue that intention concerns something in view of which an agent is better off, so that by acting the agent acquires a perfection which the agent lacked before; such acquisition cannot apply to a true first cause.

p.308 — physician, carpenter, riḍā versus qaṣd

No genuine cause becomes more perfect through its effect, for the effect's perfection comes from the cause: how could it in turn perfect the cause? Intention can proceed from an agent acting by will that is not the effect's true existential cause. Instead such an agent uses intention to make matter ready for an effect to receive existence from its true cause. Thus a physician prepares the patient's body for the influx of health from the Giver of Health and Life, and a carpenter prepares wood for the influx of the form of a bed from the Giver of Forms. In short, the agency of beings that act through animal willing is, strictly considered, only the moving of matter. It is appropriate to ascribe intention to these agents, not to the true cause. In a genuine existential cause, willing resolves into riḍā (approval or satisfaction), not qaṣd (purposeful intending). Consequently the Necessary Existent is an agent through riḍā, not through qaṣd, and the same holds for the true intermediary causes, namely separate intellects and celestial souls.

The majority of theologians, by contrast, describe God as acting through intention. Lāhījī criticises the Ashʿarites for rejecting purposes and denying the necessity that divine actions embody good and benefits, while the Muʿtazilites affirm ends directed toward others and deny that benefiting another necessarily completes the agent.

p.309 — an attempted reconciliation

The more exact analysis suggests that the Muʿtazilī position on benefit can be reduced to what the philosophers mean when they say divine works include wise purposes and benefits. Whether these are intended or approved may then be a merely verbal difference: Muʿtazilites do not restrict the technical meaning of intending as the philosophers do, and the philosophers call divine intention riḍā under their terminology. This reconciliation is particularly plausible in the case of those Muʿtazilites who affirm some degree of causal necessitation: the will necessarily entails the act and the act does not exist until necessary. That need not conflict with the philosophical account of acts as consequences of the goodness of the Necessary Existent's perfect essence. In that restricted comparison there may be no substantive disagreement.

Print variants: p.307 B بر for در and omits در; p.308 A has حقيقة for حقيقيه, B او for معلول, A omits در, A omits را; p.309 C بالمعنى for معنى, B/C give بناء بر تخصيص اصطلاح كنند in the terminology sentence, A تخصيص for تحقيق. The p.309 variants affect the phrasing of the attempted reconciliation and deserve comparison with the actual manuscripts.

Apparatus — what cannot be collapsed

  1. Not unqualified determinism. Lāhījī denies that God's agency is blind natural compulsion, while accepting a type of causal necessitation consonant with the divine will under the riḍā model. Whether that leaves genuine alternative possibilities for creation needs a separate modal demonstration.
  2. Two uses of “will.” Qaṣd is deliberative intention directed to an end improving the agent; riḍā is an approving, non-acquisitive relation to effects. Translation as the undifferentiated “volition” would erase the substantive terminology.
  3. A crucial later-Avicennian continuity. The physician and carpenter prepare material dispositions; the true cause confers actuality. Compare Ibn Sīnā Ilāhiyyāt VI.2 and IX.5.
  4. School generalizations are Lāhījī's historical characterisations, not a modern proof that every Ashʿarī or Muʿtazilī held the position exactly as stated. In particular the closing reconciliation concerns only certain Muʿtazilites.
  5. Boundary: the entire brief Chapter 8 spans pp.307–309 in this witness. This new working translation closely follows the full connected argument, but no direct manuscript collation or independent verification of every cited theologian has been attempted.

Result: a non-Ṣadrian late Avicennian account of divine and intermediate agency by approval that preserves natural preparatory agents, separates them from existential causes, and proposes a conditional rapprochement with Muʿtazilī teleology.

Late-commentator fascicle 08 — Lāhījī, Sarmāya-i īmān: preference, continual existence and the builder

31 October 2026 · independent Persian theological work → new English translation

Source: ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Lāhījī, Sarmāya-i īmān dar uṣūl-i iʿtiqādāt, Persian text, p.37 and p.38; p.39 concludes a separate theological argument. This is an independent work from Gawhar-i murād and Shawāriq al-ilhām, not another copy of the same wording. The translation is focused on the contiguous two-page argument on preference, causality and conservation; the page-38 excerpt closes mid-proof at the p.39 transition.

p.37 — awlawiyya cannot explain an actual contingent effect

الشيء ما لم يجب لم يوجد

Translation of the argument: Suppose an existence is preferable to nonexistence, whether that preference derives from the possible being's essence or from another cause. The contingent thing still cannot exist until its cause renders its existence necessary. If, given the preference, nonexistence is no longer possible, the alleged preference is really necessity and should be named as such. If nonexistence remains possible, the actual occurrence of existence requires another determinant; otherwise an equally possible alternative occurs without sufficient explanation. Introducing a second, third, or infinitely many “preferences” does not help. Either the totality of these makes nonexistence impossible, in which case necessity has been reached, or nonexistence remains possible and the difficulty recurs. In the hypothetical-syllogistic formulation: if preference alone sufficed for occurrence, non-occurrence would be impossible; but non-occurrence is possible in the case of a contingent thing. Therefore preference alone does not suffice. “A thing does not exist until it has become necessary.”

p.38 — why need persists as long as the contingent thing exists

ممكن ... در بقاء وجود نيز محتاج باشد به علت

Translation: Once it is understood that the ground of dependence is contingency (imkān), not origination (ḥudūth), and that contingency is inseparable from a possible quiddity even after the thing exists, it follows that the contingent being needs a cause both at the beginning and throughout its continuance. Indeed existence flows into it afresh, instant after instant, from the cause: persistence is nothing but continued existence. At every instant in which existence occurs it must proceed from its cause. Without the true principle's giving existence, the whole world would fall away into complete nonbeing.

The builder objection and reply: Some who make temporal origination the ground of need imagine a completed house or pot should disappear as soon as builder or potter dies, if continuing existence depended on a cause. But craftspeople are not the true existential efficient causes of houses and pots: they cause movements which prepare materials to receive form and existence from the bestower of forms and being. The absence of a preparatory cause does not imply the disappearance of the already constituted effect. Moreover, denying need during continuance damages the argument for the Necessary Existent: an allegedly beginningless contingent could terminate an explanatory chain without any cause.

Apparatus and comparisons

  1. Important conceptual subtlety: Lāhījī's imagery that existence is continually renewed “instant after instant” should not automatically be construed as Ṣadrā's substantial motion, or as Ashʿarite re-creation of every accident. In context the point is existential dependence; the mode of substantial persistence is a further question.
  2. Two works, one coherent doctrine: this independent Persian argument agrees with Gawhar-i murād Chapter 7 about contingency and continuance and Chapter 6 about a distinct bestower of forms. A historical author may formulate the same principle differently in a short creed and a long philosophical treatise.
  3. Comparison: Avicenna Ilāhiyyāt VI.1 already states the argument; Lāhījī provides a Persian pedagogical exposition, not its first invention. Aquinas ST I q.104 likewise differentiates a builder's fieri agency and an existential conserving source, though his divine freedom doctrine diverges.
  4. Document state: direct searchable printed text consulted; modern print transcription, no manuscript variants established; p.37–38 translated as a bounded passage, not a full edition of the creed.

Result: independent Persian corroboration from Lāhījī that preserving a contingent being is a continuous act of dependence, not something once brought about by a physical builder.

Late-commentator fascicle 06 — al-Taftāzānī, Sharḥ al-Maqāṣid, the three objections to mental existence

31 October 2026 · Arabic primary source → working English translation

Direct source: Saʿd al-Dīn al-Taftāzānī (d. 792/1389), Sharḥ al-Maqāṣid fī ʿilm al-kalām, printed ed. 1401/1981, pp. 78–79 as transcribed at Usul p.78 and p.79. Boundary: a connected argument dossier from the discussion of proofs and counterarguments for al-wujūd al-dhihnī, not the entire work or full surrounding chapter. The entire three-objection-and-answer sequence of p.79 is translated below in distinct units. The long preparatory discussion on p.78 is selectively translated with explicitly bounded sentences.

I. Arguments for mental existence — p.78 (selected connected unit)

أن الكلي مفهوم وكل مفهوم ثابت ضرورة تميزه عند العقل

Translation: “The universal is conceived, and everything conceived has some establishment, necessarily, by virtue of its being distinguishable for the intellect.” But a universal as universal cannot be a singular thing in extra-mental reality, where everything is individuated; hence the kind of establishment in question is mental. Likewise certain genuinely universal affirmative judgments must have intelligible subjects despite lacking any external instance, as in the claim “Every phoenix is an animal.” Their subject cannot be restricted to extant particular birds.

The opponent's objection: Predication does not require an externally or mentally existent subject: in predicating we may only mean that whatever answers to a subject term answers to the predicate; recognition of the terms is not yet proof of an additional mode of existence. The supposed three proofs collapse into the fact that we conceive what lacks an external instance. If the defender simply assumes that conception occurs through a thing's form being present in the intellect, the disputed thesis has been smuggled into a premise.

Reply: There must be a relation between knowing intellect and object known, whether cognition is conceived as an acquired form, a special relation, or a relational quality. Relation to absolute nothingness is impossible; therefore the object known must possess at least some determinate standing. Since universals, absent things and impossibilia do not exist externally, their intelligible determination must lie elsewhere.

II. The rival location hypotheses — pp.78–79 (selected)

Some propose independently subsisting Platonic forms, an imaginal world, or forms imprinted in a separate Active Intellect. Taftāzānī answers that what is absolutely impossible cannot subsist externally as an individual form or be imprinted in a separate intellect as a self-standing impossible individual. What that intellect could possess is the conception of the impossible, itself bringing the explanation back to representation. Yet the exact inference from objectivity of conception to a form inhering in this knower is contested; al-Taftāzānī reports that even the Mawāqif formulation may be disputed at precisely this point.

III. The deniers' three objections — p.79

الأول لو كان تصور الشيء مستلزما لحصوله في العقل

First objection, translation: If imagining a thing requires its presence in the intellect, then conceiving heat and cold would make the intellect hot and cold. That is impossible, since it would combine contraries and attribute to the intellect properties of bodies.

Second objection: The heavens, with all their vastness, would have to be present in the intellect when conceived, and in imagination when imagined. This is evidently false.

Third objection: Conceiving nonexistent things would make them exist externally, since they exist in an intellect that itself exists outside the mind, and what exists inside a thing existing outside the mind is supposed to exist where that thing is—just as water in a jug in a house is in that house.

IV. The answer — p.79

الفرق بين الوجود المتأصل ... والوجود ... الذهني

Translation: All three objections fail to distinguish rooted, extra-mental existence—through which a thing has its concrete individuality—from the non-rooted existence of a mental form. The thing that is literally hot is a subject in which the extra-mental reality of heat subsists, not one that possesses its mental form; the opposition of heat and cold concerns their external realities, not their representations. What plainly cannot be contained within an intellect or imagination is the actual cosmic reality of the heavens, not the universal or particular form through which they are grasped. Finally, the inference from an interior to an enclosing exterior applies when both are independently existing concrete realities (water, jug, house), not when an absent object's form has mental being while the mind has external being. The mental representation and the external individual differ in their necessary concomitants: the former may be universal, abstract and causally inactive, the latter individuated and productive of effects. Their correspondence means that a quiddity actualised externally would be that external reality, and stripped of individuating attachments could be considered as the intelligible quiddity.

V. Editorial and philosophical apparatus

  1. The direct voice is Taftāzānī. A nested report of al-Ījī's Mawāqif is identified as such, and an objection to it remains Taftāzānī's presentation. We do not count it as independently collated al-Ījī text.
  2. Key terms: wujūd aṣīl / mutaʾaṣṣil is extra-mental, concrete actuality; wujūd dhihnī is mental representational actuality; ṣūra need not be a second physical individual; hawiyya ʿayniyya is extra-mental individuated reality. The source's ʿāriḍ argument should not be paraphrased as “ideas are physical heat.”
  3. Cross-commentary test: compare Jurjānī's Sharḥ al-Mawāqif and Lāhījī's Shawāriq mental-existence argument. The logical difference concerns what is represented versus the representing act's ontological inherence.
  4. Real issue not settled automatically: one can reject the opponents' crude physical objections and still debate whether the intelligible species is formally identical with the represented nature, whether conception entails mental wujūd or a merely intentional relation, and what constitutes nafs al-amr for true judgments about impossible objects.
  5. Boundaries: p.79 provides the direct sequence of three objections and the connected answer, translated without eliding a substantive objection. The surrounding discussion p.78 is selective; no manuscript witnesses or variant readings were checked.

Status: new complete bounded objections-and-answer sequence from a primary post-Avicennian kalām commentary, not an exhaustive translation of Sharḥ al-Maqāṣid.

Late-commentator fascicle 07 — al-Mawāqif and al-Jurjānī's Sharḥ, with the printed Siyālkūtī–Ḥalabī marginal layers

31 October 2026 · Arabic primary text → working layered English translation

Witness: al-Ījī, al-Mawāqif, within al-Sayyid al-Sharīf al-Jurjānī's Sharḥ al-Mawāqif, printed vol. VI, pp. 7–8, in a composite offset edition labelled “with the two marginal glosses of al-Siyālkūtī and al-Ḥalabī”; searchable page 7, page 8. Editorial caution: parenthesised lemmata are generally the matn, connected unparenthesised argument belongs to the commentator, and interleaved قوله notes belong to marginal layers—but this digital stream does not consistently identify which anonymous note belongs to which named glossator. Therefore we translate a clearly bounded exchange and do not claim any anonymous marginal sentence as directly verified Siyālkūtī's or Ḥalabī's.

p.7 — the deniers' arguments (matn and sharḥ)

Matn lemma (al-Ījī):

وقال المتكلمون هو ... باطل لوجهين

Translation: “The theologians held that this [equating cognition with mental existence] is false for two reasons.”

Al-Jurjānī's reported explanation: “This” refers to the claim that knowledge consists in mental existence. First, if intellection consists in the essence or quiddity of the known thing being present in the knower's intellect, someone who understands blackness and whiteness and judges them contraries would possess them both mentally. The intellect would then itself be black and white, since being black or white is simply the possession of such qualities; it would moreover house two contraries in one subject, an absurdity. Second, the actual quiddities of a mountain and of the heavens plainly cannot occupy the narrow mind: to concede the presence of their vast actual magnitudes is to deny the evident.

Embedded gloss, attribution unresolved: the editor's interleaved note argues that, considered simply as a quiddity, a nature can be abstracted from its specifically mental determination; when considered precisely as mental, it cannot acquire extra-mental existence under the same individuating mental identity. Another note asks whether the physical impossibility of a vast thing fitting in a little body really proves that a representation cannot occur; the answer depends on the precise sense of “occurring within”.

p.8 — the commentator's paired replies

First reply (matn lemma and Jurjānī's expansion):

لو حصل فيه هوية السواد والبياض ... لا ماهيتهما

The objection would follow only if the concrete external realities of blackness and whiteness were present within the intellect, not their mentally existing intelligible quiddities. A mental form exists in a dependent, representational way; actual physical colour, physical opposition, and real inherence presuppose the extra-mental reality. Thus the mind does not literally become two coloured bodies or receive mutually excluding physical qualities when it understands colour.

Second reply:

الممتنع حصول هوية الجبل والسماء

What cannot be received by intellect or imagination is the enormous external identity and magnitude of mountain and heaven; it does not follow that their intelligible representations—universal or individual—are too large to be understood.

Marginal objections and replies, anonymous within the composite stream: Some distinguish “occurring in the mind” as a container-like relation from a physical quality's inhering in a subject. A critic replies that the subject of knowledge must nonetheless be described as knowledgeable, so mere containment does not eliminate the issue. Others invoke a mirror containing a small image of an immense object; an answer distinguishes optical reflection from a bodily image literally stamped in a surface. Still other marginal notes insist that the solution ultimately depends on which effects belong only to extra-mental existence and do not belong to a mental representation.

Edition-critical controls

  1. Three historical layers, one concatenated digital stream. (a) al-Ījī's matn; (b) al-Jurjānī's sharḥ; (c) al-Siyālkūtī and al-Ḥalabī marginalia, together with possibly further interleaving. The host labels the book but does not identify every note. No independent source-witness division was verified visually; do not pretend the third layer has been separated by author.
  2. The gender of “thing present”. One can understand heat without becoming literally hot; a represented nature does not possess in the knower all the extra-mental accidents that its outward instance would produce. This is the same logical answer Taftāzānī gives to a similar objection, though the texts' exact dependence is not demonstrated merely by parallel content.
  3. **Interpretation of nafs al-amr.** The distinction between quiddity considered absolutely and quiddity qualified by mental or external existence is not yet a proof of an independently subsisting “third world”; it is a discipline of different respects (iʿtibārāt).
  4. What to edit next: inspect a physical scan of this composite edition and compare separately identified al-Siyālkūtī and al-Ḥalabī gloss prints, then align folio-level entries with the present p.7–8 digital concatenation. Recompute speaker labels at that time.
  5. Extent: two directly inspected printed pages, one complete objection–reply topic, not a diplomatic critical edition of all four distinct authors or of the complete sixth volume.

Status: layered working translation and attribution audit. Real post-Tajrīd scholastic material incorporated without manufacturing anonymous Indian glosses.

Late-commentator fascicle 05 — Indian philosophical lexicography on jaʿl: al-Tahānawī and the reported Mīr Zāhid gloss

31 October 2026 · Arabic primary text → new working translation with attribution control

Direct witness: Muḥammad ʿAlī al-Tahānawī (Indian scholar, eighteenth century), Kashshāf iṣṭilāḥāt al-funūn wa-l-ʿulūm, vol. I, entry الجعل, opening on printed p.620 and continuing/ending p.621. Reported embedded witness: Mīr Zāhid, gloss on al-Jurjānī's Sharḥ al-Mawāqif in the section on general matters and the question “Is quiddity made?”. The two are not independent direct collations: Kashshāf is the direct witness here and Mīr Zāhid is cited within it.

Entry's first distinction — simple and compound making

وهو عند الحكماء على قسمين: جعل بسيط ... وجعل مركب

Translation of the connected definitions: For the philosophers, “making” (jaʿl) is of two types. In simple making a thing itself is produced: its effect is the thing itself, requiring only one term, the thing made. Its upshot is bringing something from nonexistence into existence. In compound making, one makes a thing be something: the effect is what the copulative construction signifies, the quiddity's being qualified by existence, considered not as an independently conceived thing but as the relation through which the two terms are grasped. This requires both a thing made and a determination it is made to have.

The South Asian report of the classical debate

كذا ذكر ميرزا زاهد في حاشية شرح المواقف في الأمور العامة

Translation: “So Mīrzā Zāhid stated in his gloss on Sharḥ al-Mawāqif, among the General Matters.”

The Kashshāf attributes to Illuminationists the first view: the agent makes the quiddity itself; and to Peripatetics the second: the agent makes a quiddity existent. This is the position-labeling transmitted by Tahānawī on Mīr Zāhid's authority, not an independent survey of every Illuminationist and Peripatetic.

The dispute and its arguments — p.621

For simple making, three arguments are transmitted. First, one must arrive at simple making directed to existence or its attribution: whatever is supposed to be made is itself a quiddity, and treating every such quiddity as requiring a further act threatens regress. Second, existence and the relation of existence to the quiddity are conceptual considerations, whereas what the making effects seems to be real. Third, existence is predicated of the Necessary Existent through its own reality; of a contingent quiddity, only insofar as it is related to a producer. Were the possible quiddity independent of the producer in itself, existence would be predicable at the rank of its own essence, destroying its contingency.

For compound making, two arguments are given. First, there cannot be a making that intervenes between a quiddity and itself; the text observes that this argument fails if one mistakenly construes simple making as though it were a third relation inserted between two already actual things. Second, the ground of causal need is possibility, understood by a relation of existence to quiddity; hence the quiddity is made in respect of its existence, not simply in respect of its being a quiddity.

Reply and further study: The final answer observes that possibility explains dependence with respect to existence and does not thereby rule out dependence in other respects. The entry refers the reader to the glosses on al-Sullam and to the **Zāhidiyya glosses on *Sharḥ al-Mawāqif*** for fuller analysis. This final appeal demonstrates the functioning of an Indian ḥāshiya network rather than supplying the original Mīr Zāhid text in full.

Variant apparatus

  • The modern edition's note on p.621 has أحقية for the main حقية in the introduction to the simple-making proofs; another note has يعرض for يفرض in the first proof.
  • The printed editor identifies Mīr Zāhid's gloss on the second Mawqif (general matters). This is useful witness identification but the edition's biographical footnote is not the original author's wording.
  • Causal “simple” and “compound” jaʿl must not be conflated with making a simple vs complex essence. The former distinguishes the logical shape of making, the latter its object.

Comparison to the longer Iranian Tajrīd reception

The argument should be compared with the already translated Lāhījī, Shawāriq I:158–161. Both discuss whether the actuality of a contingent individual or its quiddity as such is produced; the Indian entry reports a Mīr Zāhid scholastic classification, while Lāhījī independently develops objections and replies in the Tajrīd commentary line. Close similarity does not prove direct textual copying.

Edition limits: this is a new, bounded English working translation of the jaʿl entry's technical divisions and argument units with identified source pages. The original Mīr Zāhid ḥāshiya has not been collated independently; do not treat the nested testimony as a second directly inspected Persian/Indian manuscript. No source-manuscript autopsy is claimed.

Late-commentator fascicle 09 — Power, necessity and choosing: al-Ṭūsī → Qūshjī → al-Khafrī → Khwānsārī in a later Tajrīd commentary

31 October 2026 · Arabic scholastic argument-chain translated with separately named layers

Direct witness: Muḥammad Jaʿfar al-Astarābādī, al-Barāhīn al-qāṭiʿa, vol. II, printed pp. 96–99, online large transcription whose page marker 96 introduces the argument. This is the author directly read in this fascicle. The text itself reports or quotes the distinct historical positions of the Tajrīd author Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī, al-Ḥillī's Kashf al-murād, ʿAlī Qūshjī's Sharḥ al-Tajrīd (cited there pp.310–311), Ghiyāth al-Dīn al-Khafrī (manuscript fol.5), and Āqā Jamāl al-Khwānsārī (via another gloss). Until those original publications/manuscripts are individually verified, the present document is one directly read later author with a nested transmission chain, not five independent source collations.

1. The challenge: if the cause is complete, can it choose? (p.96–97)

Objection, in the direct text (working translation): Divine power is understood as the possibility of both acting and refraining. But an effect of an agent must either be necessary or impossible: when the agent possesses every condition for effectiveness and is a complete cause, its effect necessarily proceeds, since a complete cause cannot exist without its effect. Without these conditions the effect is impossible. Thus there appears to be no interval in which doing and refraining are both possible, and divine power seems impossible.

Ṭūsī's lemma, as embedded by Astarābādī:

ويمكن عروض الوجوب والإمكان للأثر بالاعتبارين

Translation: “Necessity and possibility may both apply to the effect under two different considerations.”

**Qūshjī's explanation, as reported by the later author: The effect, considering the power and the agent's essence without the determining will, is possible; considering the will directed to the most fitting action, it becomes necessary**. This is necessity through choosing, and such necessity does not negate choice but expresses its efficacy. A capable agent is one who, on willing to act, produces the act; on willing not to act, refrains. The effect is also impossible under the consideration that its conditions are absent. The three modal predicates do not contradict each other because the respect differs. The later text cites both Qūshjī and al-Ḥillī's Kashf al-murād for substantially this response, not an identical verbatim passage in each.

2. Al-Khafrī's objection to the reconciliation (p.97–98)

Embedded objection, expressly attributed to al-Khafrī: If divine will is identical to the divine essence, it cannot fail to be present. Then the necessity arising from such will is incompatible with “power” understood in the theologians' stronger sense of genuinely being able to do or leave undone. “Necessity through will” may coexist with the philosophers' conditional definition of choice (“if he wills he acts, and if he does not will he refrains”), but since the eternal divine will does in fact will, the effect proceeds eternally from the willing essence. A complete cause cannot be detached from its effect in time; how, then, could a will identical with the divine essence be separated temporally from its willing a created effect? The text demands a real solution rather than a merely verbal appeal to choice.

Witness boundary: the Astarābādī note cites al-Khafrī's Ḥāshiya ʿalā Ilāhiyyāt Sharḥ al-Qūshjī, folio 5, as a manuscript lead, not a newly inspected manuscript.

3. The later author's response and the Khwānsārī pressure (p.98)

Astarābādī's own response: The operative will producing an effect is an additional, newly arising mashīʾa, rather than precisely the essential attribute of divine knowledge. Consequently one may reject al-Khafrī's alleged incompatibility. Another solution emphasises the object's being the best thing to create at a specified time: the effect willed is a temporal thing, not an eternal thing, so its lack of eternity need not imply that the cause was incomplete. Further arguments refer to limits in the receptive object rather than limits in the agent.

The author then reports an objection associated through his notes with Āqā Jamāl al-Khwānsārī: if a possible thing's eternal existence is intrinsically impossible, why would its essence require absence only at one time rather than forever? Positing time-specific absence threatens to presuppose time itself. Here the source layer is Astarābādī's report of a prior marginal controversy. It is not yet textual evidence that Āqā Ḥusayn al-Khwānsārī (Jamāl's father) authored this objection; the two must never be conflated.

4. Further reply and rival reading (pp.98–99)

A further scholastic gloss (attributed in the text only by a title, “Sun of the Marginalists”) reformulates the problem from the effect's own contingency. Whether the cause's conditions are present or absent, the effect remains contingent in itself; it becomes necessary through a completed cause and impossible through the absence of required conditions. There is no contradiction, because intrinsic possibility excludes intrinsic necessity and intrinsic impossibility, but not necessity or impossibility through another. The gloss criticizes Qūshjī for framing the problem too strongly in terms of an effect's possible proceeding rather than the effect's intrinsic modal status.

Synthesis: this is concrete evidence that post-Tajrīd commentators did not merely repeat a formula “a possible effect becomes necessary through a complete cause.” They contested which modal respect is relevant to genuine freedom, whether divine will is identical with divine essence, whether a temporal effect is a different intentional object from an eternal one, and whether one can satisfy a theological requirement of alternate possibilities by a conditional definition of power.
