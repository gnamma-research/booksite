Between the Greeks and the Islamic philosophers

The philosophical inheritance of the Arabic-reading world passed through a long and internally contested late antique world: Platonists who commented on Aristotle, Syrian Christian teachers who translated and reshaped texts, Greek Christian philosophical critics, and Arabic editors who did far more than translate verbatim.

This is a guide to the interactive atlas, not a claim of a seamless succession or of an exhaustive manuscript census. The editorial unit is a text or transmission witness, linked when a specific manuscript, commentary, translation, critique, or historically documented intellectual relation warrants it. Political simultaneity and regional proximity alone do not warrant an arrow.

The five overlapping settings

Plotinus and Porphyry c. 245–305

Plotinus' pupils and Porphyry's editorial work formed a major surviving corpus. The Enneads are Porphyry’s organization of Plotinian treatises, not six original books authored in that order. Porphyry’s Isagoge and Sentences supplied different introductory and philosophical projects. Neither the Arabic Theology nor a continuous direct Arabic translation of all six Enneads belongs to this period.

Sources: Porphyry, SEP; Theology of Aristotle, SEP.

Athenian Platonism c. 410–529

Syrianus' surviving commentary addresses Metaphysics B, Γ, M, N; Proclus wrote extensive commentaries on Timaeus and Parmenides, the Platonic Theology and Elements of Theology. Damascius’ Problems and Solutions Concerning First Principles is a philosophical critique, not simply another retelling of Proclus. The conventional restriction of the Athenian school around 529 did not end Greek philosophical learning.

Sources: Syrianus; Proclus; Damascius.

Alexandrian Aristotle c. 470–630

Ammonius son of Hermias, his students including Philoponus, and Olympiodorus represent a late antique curriculum in which Aristotelian logic and natural philosophy were frequently interpreted through late Platonic assumptions. Philoponus' anti-eternalist arguments sharply departed from competing Aristotelian and Neoplatonic positions. Simplicius, mainly associated with Athenian training, was a contemporary critic whose commentaries preserve earlier otherwise-lost sources. This is not a single consensual school.

Sources: Ammonius; Philoponus; Olympiodorus; Simplicius.

Syriac scholarly worlds c. 490–800

Syriac schools and translators transmitted substantial logical and theological material in several independent channels. Sergius of Reshaina's encounter with the Alexandrian curriculum, his version of Alexander’s On the Principles of the Universe, and an early Syriac Dionysian corpus show both faithful reception and interpretive alteration. The presumption of one uninterrupted Greek → Syriac → Arabic route should be tested per work, not imposed generally.

Sources: Daniel King; SergiusNet; Greek Sources in Arabic Philosophy.

ʿAbbāsid translation and adaptation c. 790–920

The Kindī circle and other translators and editors transmitted Greek philosophical material in Arabic, sometimes mediated through Syriac and sometimes not. The Arabic Theology of Aristotle reworks Plotinus' Enneads IV–VI. Several other Arabic Plotinus pieces form a related but non-identical family. The Arabic Book of the Pure Good reworks Proclus’ propositions through a different redactional and metaphysical framework. These works later entered Islamic, Jewish and Latin philosophical discussions through distinct routes.

Sources: Greek Sources in Arabic Philosophy; Theology of Aristotle; Proclus.

Editorial safeguards

Source confidence is attached to each link, not inherited from a school label. An author's region, theological affiliation or chronological overlap never creates a direct transmission arrow. A scholar's commentary on a Māturīdī creed does not make that scholar a Māturīdī by allegiance; similarly, Neoplatonic themes in an Arabic work do not make its author a member of the Athenian school.

Where the dating is particularly uncertain
  • Many dates label the author's activity span or a terminus before death, not a known composition year. These are marked as approximate or upper bounds in the atlas.
  • The Arabic Proclus Timaeus fragment is an extant source witness but has no securely established date of translation in this register. It is catalogued and left unplaced.
  • The “Harmony of the Opinions of Plato and Aristotle” is still attributed to al-Fārābī in parts of the tradition, but the authorship is contested. The route is labeled attributed.
  • The ancient Roman Latin Boethian branch is a parallel Porphyrian transmission, not a missing step between Alexandria and Baghdad.
What this revision does not yet establish
  • A full manuscript stemma for every Greek, Syriac and Arabic witness or exhaustively collated lemma-level textual relations.
  • Complete Greek and Syriac school histories, or secure dates for every Arabic translation.
  • An exhaustive assignment of Ashʿarī/Māturīdī doctrinal affiliation from the general Sunni kalām register. The atlas applies a separate, sourced, selective overlay.
  • A precise prosopographic graph of each scholar's teachers, students, residence and itinerary. Regional classes remain qualified and partially based on catalogue descriptions.
