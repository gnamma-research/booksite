تهذيب المنطق والكلام
Tahdhīb al-manṭiq wa-l-kalām

Taftāzānī’s two-part treatise (completed Rajab 789 AH / 1387 CE) generated overlapping but distinctly distributed commentary families: a very extensive logic tradition and a smaller yet important kalām tradition. The networks span Ottoman, Iranian, Imāmī, Egyptian and South Asian scholarship.

Taftāzānī · Tahdhīb
1387 · one original work

Part I · Logic

Dawwānī → Saʿīdī / Lārī / Mīr Zāhid; separate Yazdī → Lāhījī; al-Khubayṣī → Egyptian glosses. Ottoman and Indian branches.

Part II · Kalām

Al-Ṣundūqlī (1845), al-Sanandajī (before 1887), Jār Allāh al-Ḥusaynī (printed 1894–95). Reported relationship to al-Maqāṣid awaits textual collation.

Critical qualifications

Dates: a death date, dated copy or print is an upper bound or witness, not a composition date. Text sections: analytical section nodes are not independently authored works. Intermediaries: Gelenbevī comments on a named Sharḥ al-Mīrī, whose exact author still needs verification. Printed appendices: the 1901 Sanandajī printing does not prove every included text is a gloss on his commentary. Yazdī: 1560 composition is attested; his death is disputed. Lāhījī: Persian direct commentary and gloss on Yazdī are independently listed. Curricular acceptance: a frequently printed work need not be a standard text at every school.

Why this is relevant to kalām

The short second section follows the systematic subjects of later kalām: general ontology, substances/accidents, God and attributes, prophecy, and eschatology. It is a compressed sibling to the larger Maqāṣid/Mawāqif corpora—not a commentary on Mawāqif.

Why the logic branch matters

Commentaries on logic cross confessional boundaries. Dawwānī’s commentary has its own glossing family, Yazdī directly glosses Taftāzānī, and later Azhar and South Asian schools develop different subtraditions.
