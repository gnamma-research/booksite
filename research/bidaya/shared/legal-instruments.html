How a legal argument is constituted

Uṣūl, legal maxims and purposes answer different questions. They can cooperate within one argument, but the name of a field does not tell us which work a proposition performs. We identify its input, operation, warrant, scope and result.

This is an editorial reconstruction anchored in Ibn Rushd’s texts. The later taxonomy of maxims and the modern presentation of maqāṣid are comparative controls; neither is projected backwards as his complete historical vocabulary.

Practical knowledge and the regulation of inquiry

The opening of the Ḍarūrī distinguishes knowledge ending in assent, knowledge ending in action, and knowledge that regulates inquiry. Within practical knowledge, universal knowledge is more remote from action and particular knowledge more proximate. The latter includes rulings concerning prayer and zakāt: “particular” here need not mean a verdict on one unique event. A general rule about a class of acts can still be particular relative to the foundations of the science. [D2]

The third kind supplies qawānīn wa-aḥwāl—rules and conditions—by which the mind proceeds correctly. Ibn Rushd then distinguishes the conventional four-part curriculum from its properly instrumental core: evidence employed to derive each ruling from its source, and how to employ that evidence. His proposed comparative treatment asks how derivation works given different accepted foundations. Thus historical uṣūl al-fiqh is a composite discipline; its distinctive instrument is not simply identical with all general practical knowledge. [D5–6]

We may call that core an organized instrument containing subordinate instruments, provided we state the level: it regulates the evidential use of linguistic rules, contextual indications, classificatory and inferential procedures. It does not thereby absorb all substantive fiqh, ethics or knowledge of purposes into one historically named science. Nor does it make these subordinate procedures automatically demonstrative.

This map is a reconstruction of relations, not an automatic sequence. A case may make us reconsider its classification, the scope of a maxim, or our interpretation of a purpose. Ibn Rushd explicitly describes induction from earlier legal opinions as validating many of the craft’s universals; this must be distinguished from proving every such universal by complete induction. These are analytical distinctions, not sealed compartments. “Certainty is not removed by doubt” can express a practical maxim; an argument about the authority and limits of continuity asks a methodological question. The same verbal formulation can occupy different levels. Conversely, two general statements need not be the same kind of instrument. The Ḍarūrī itself distinguishes several senses of continuity and asks whether the subject has changed. [D142]

Al-umūr bi-maqāṣidihā concerns the purposes or intentions of acts; its word maqāṣid does not make it identical to a science of the Lawgiver’s ends. In the checked Jamʿ al-Jawāmiʿ passage, the first four maxims are attributed to al-Qāḍī Ḥusayn, whereas intention is added with qīla, “it is said.” This later text places maxims within an uṣūl work without making their substantive object identical to every rule of inference. [J111]

A maxim can derive its force from a revealed statement, from juristic accumulation and induction, or from a combination of sources. Calling it a maxim neither increases nor reduces that force by itself. Some are heavily qualified generalizations; some rest on stronger evidence. We therefore record warrant and exceptions individually rather than declare that all maxims are either independent proofs or merely convenient summaries. The supplied modern compilation is a finding aid and comparative control, not evidence that every listed maxim was available in that wording to Ibn Rushd. [QC5–6]

When purposes and maxims function as qawānīn

A purpose does not turn into a qānūn merely by being stated abstractly. It becomes instrumentally usable when a warranted procedure tells the jurist what to do with it. A useful schema is: given an established purpose or general rule, under specified conditions, identify or prefer a legally supported conclusion, subject to named counterevidence and exceptions. This is our reconstruction of an instrumental function, not Ibn Rushd’s definition of a later maqāṣid science.

  1. Establish the content. Is this an end intended by the law, a pattern among decided cases, or an editor’s hypothesis about a beneficial effect?
  2. Establish the warrant. Which text, indication, accepted practice or induction supports it? Discovering a promising explanation is different from authorizing reliance on it.
  3. Specify the operation. Does it interpret a term, identify a relevant ground, classify a case, reconcile evidence, guide preference, constrain a remedy, or explain the result after it is reached?
  4. Delimit the domain. State subjects, exceptions, competing rights and the school whose premises are in use. Test a counterexample.
  5. Identify the output and its status. Does it yield a ruling, a presumption, a question for inquiry, or an explanation? Do not turn probability into certainty.

This makes the user’s proposed relation intelligible: a substantive maxim or a purpose can supply material for an operative qānūn, while uṣūl examines how its use is warranted. The source proposition is not thereby transformed into a different historical genre. Sometimes the instrument is the rule for using the maxim, rather than the maxim alone. Sometimes a directly warranted general legal rule already supplies an applicable major premise; the remaining task is establishing the case beneath it.

The limits are especially important for Ibn Rushd. He accepts consideration of benefits within a legally witnessed extent and kind. He distinguishes a proximate suitable ground from an exceedingly general appeal to benefit, and criticizes the latter for becoming legislation rather than derivation. His account of much juristic qiyās instead investigates contextual indications of an intended general meaning. A proposed “maqṣad” cannot supply a missing indication merely by sounding humane. [D146; D212–213; D217; D223–226]

Yet purpose is not reduced to decorative explanation. In the salam chapter, facilitation bears on the interpretation of delay; in injuries, preventing an excessive outcome bears on feasibility and timing; in judging, undisturbed understanding identifies conditions extending beyond anger. The task is to show exactly where the purpose enters the argument and what it can establish there. These are not interchangeable applications of an unrestricted maṣlaḥa principle.

Reconstruct the argument, not a list of labels

Begin with Ibn Rushd’s sabab al-ikhtilāf. Divide a chapter into its actual questions and alternatives. For each alternative record the evidence and each inferential step: premise → operation → conclusion. A proposition may be explicit in the passage, supplied by a clearly cited parallel, reconstructed by the editor, or still unestablished. These statuses must not be merged.

The minimum record is: disputed question; reported position; evidence; authority claim; semantic or classificatory step; operative ground; substantive maxim if actually used; purpose and its evidential status; factual assessment; competing inference; result and degree of confidence. Add the conditions of competent judgment and enforcement. Record “not established here” when a proposed tool does no demonstrated work.

Ghazālī’s Mustaṣfā gives a particularly useful control: taḥqīq al-manāṭ ascertains a known criterion in a case; tanqīḥ al-manāṭ separates the relevant ground from accompanying features; takhrīj al-manāṭ seeks the ground where it has not been stated. He treats ijtihād as wider than qiyās. His examples of maintenance, compensation, the prayer direction and testimony show why factual assessment cannot be replaced by a universal rule. These distinctions clarify our reconstruction; they do not prove that Ibn Rushd uses each label in the same way. [G436–439]

The resulting commentary has three connected levels: constitution of the judgment (what makes the argument work); explanation of the legal form (what good its distinctions can serve); and effects of practising it (formation and institutional consequences, where that inquiry is included). A consequence may illuminate a law without having been its historical inferential warrant. The boundary remains explicit even when a single argument connects all three.

Eight applications

These analyses are anchored in bounded discussions. They distinguish the tools doing work from later controls; they are not a completed reconstruction of every subordinate ruling in those discussions.

Intention: classify the act before applying the general rule

Does ablution take its legal character chiefly from ritual obedience or from an intelligible act of cleaning?

The argument Ibn Rushd reports. The shared requirement of intention for worship does not settle whether ablution belongs to the relevant class. Ibn Rushd locates disagreement in its resemblance to two kinds of act. Classification does the decisive work: which resemblance governs this condition of validity?

Uṣūl: the inferential work. Interpret the scriptural and prophetic language about intention, identify its scope, then establish whether this act falls within it. The Ḍarūrī’s account of voluntary obedient action supplies a conceptual control; it does not itself decide the ablution dispute.

Maxims and chapter-level rules. “Matters are judged by their purposes” is a later cross-chapter formulation useful for comparing cases. It cannot resolve the very dispute about what kind of purpose is legally required here. The agent’s intention (qaṣd al-mukallaf) is distinct from the Lawgiver’s purpose.

Purpose: its actual role. Purification and obedient worship help identify the practical object. This is stronger than adding an ethical benefit after the ruling, but weaker than proving that every purposeful act requires a ritual intention.

Application and the remaining burden. Ask whether intention is a condition of legal validity in this classified act, not whether any intentional conduct is morally preferable. Neither the maxim nor the general theory of worship supplies the disputed minor premise.

Zakāt on a minor’s wealth: locate the obligation

Is the obligation being considered chiefly as the owner’s worship or as a right attaching to wealth for the poor?

The argument Ibn Rushd reports. Ibn Rushd expressly makes this characterization the reason for disagreement over minority. A rule about personal responsibility and a rule about financial entitlement can both be general without governing the same object. He also reports differentiated positions on kinds of property and admits that he does not presently know the evidence for some of those distinctions.

Uṣūl: the inferential work. Separate a condition of personal legal responsibility from the incidence of a financial claim. The Ḍarūrī’s explanation of puberty as a commonly reliable sign concerns the first; its transfer to every property obligation would require another argument.

Maxims and chapter-level rules. A general release from personal obligation cannot silently become immunity of property from others’ rights. A later maxim is useful only after identifying which liability or entitlement is at issue; no decisive maxim is established by this passage.

Purpose: its actual role. The poor’s right is an explicit legal characterization here. Generosity and communal sharing in the closing Bidāya passage provide a wider teleology, but do not independently choose the person or property on which the duty falls.

Application and the remaining burden. Where an obligation attaches to wealth, administration by a guardian and the child’s inward intention are different questions. The passage supports contrasting structures of obligation, not a claim that one school values society and another values spirituality exclusively.

Bidāya I, pp.283–284 (Nyazee); Arabic scan I, p.245 · Ḍarūrī, reader §42

Nursing: textual scope, operative conditions and proof

Which nursing establishes kinship, and how is it known?

The argument Ibn Rushd reports. Ibn Rushd separates nine questions. Quantity turns on Qurʾānic generality, competing reports and reconciliation. Age and weaning turn partly on how “nursing through hunger” identifies the relevant condition. Mixture and routes of ingestion introduce classification and factual uncertainty. Testimony asks what establishes the event. These are distinct arguments, not nine applications of one purpose.

Uṣūl: the inferential work. General and specific evidence, chronology where relevant, explicit and implied indication, and the force of reports govern different subquestions. The Ḍarūrī passage on specification helps formulate one problem; it does not answer all nine.

Maxims and chapter-level rules. A maxim about certainty or evidence can organize what must be established, but cannot supply a missing number of feedings. In the separate care passage, customary practice is expressly invoked for differentiated nursing duties: custom here performs a concrete legal task rather than merely illustrating a maxim.

Purpose: its actual role. Nutrition is implicated by the cited prophetic expression; the precise issue is whether the law attends to an age-defined need or the individual child’s continuing dependence. Wider family protection is a reasonable explanatory horizon, not sufficient evidence for any chosen numerical threshold.

Application and the remaining burden. Distinguish an authoritative criterion from establishing that this infant, this milk and this event meet it. Ghazālī’s distinction between deriving, refining and ascertaining the operative ground is a comparative control for these separate tasks.

Bidāya II, pp.41–46 and 66–67 (Nyazee); Arabic scan II, pp.35–40 and 56–57 · Ḍarūrī, reader §181 · Ḍarūrī, reader §212

Salam: determine the intended class, then test deliverability

Does the permission concern only named measured goods, or every good that can become a sufficiently determinate future liability?

The argument Ibn Rushd reports. The discussion combines the report’s wording, disagreements among reports, the ability to specify an object, and disagreements over delivery time. Extension to describable goods and deciding whether animals can be adequately described are different steps. Ibn Rushd explicitly seeks controlling principles rather than an unlimited enumeration of cases.

Uṣūl: the inferential work. Recover the intended reach of the report, assess competing indications, and distinguish that task from subsuming a particular kind of good. The Ḍarūrī’s account of an indicated universal is a close methodological comparison. It does not prove that every extension in this chapter is the same operation.

Maxims and chapter-level rules. A tolerance for slight gharar appears in Ibn Rushd’s explanation of seasonal delivery dates. It is an operative generalization about cases; the disputants still differ over whether this uncertainty is slight. The later maxim concerning need can illuminate why a special transaction is intelligible but is not its historical warrant.

Purpose: its actual role. Facilitating provision and benefiting both parties enter the reported Mālikī interpretation of delay. Al-Shāfiʿī’s argument for immediate delivery instead stresses reduced uncertainty. These are different uses of an intelligible good within a dispute, not a single modern maqāṣid formula deciding both.

Application and the remaining burden. A description must be adequate for this market and commodity; the delivery date must be sufficiently determinate; the object must have the required prospect of availability. Purpose cannot replace these assessments. The permission for salam cannot be generalized into permission for any useful financing arrangement.

Interdiction: distinguish the end of protection from authority to restrict

What authorizes imposing, continuing or ending a restriction on financial acts?

The argument Ibn Rushd reports. The book separates the persons restricted, entry and release conditions, and the effects of their acts. The argument for restricting a prodigal adult identifies prodigality as the relevant ground in the minor’s case. The opposing position resists that transfer, invokes a prophetic remedy short of interdiction, and distinguishes withholding wealth from nullifying transactions.

Uṣūl: the inferential work. Textual indication and the identification of the operative ground must precede extension. Puberty as a sign of responsibility is relevant but is not identical to financial discretion (rushd). The legal consequences of a restriction require their own evidence.

Maxims and chapter-level rules. “Harm is to be removed” states a broad demand; it does not confer this particular jurisdiction. A chapter-specific rule about capacity or the effect of guardianship may operate as a ḍābiṭ, but its reach must be stated separately for gifts, exchanges, divorce and other acts.

Purpose: its actual role. Preserving wealth helps explain the restriction; protecting competent agency constrains it. This conflict shows why an abstract beneficial end cannot determine the office, evidentiary threshold or duration of the power. The two sides must not be reduced to concern versus indifference.

Application and the remaining burden. Establish the person’s financial discretion, identify who may judge it, and distinguish the existence of a relevant quality from a formal release. Ghazālī’s taḥqīq al-manāṭ clarifies the assessment step; it does not resolve the institutional disagreement for Ibn Rushd.

Deposit: the trust relation, its evidentiary effects and its alteration

What follows from having entrusted property, and which acts change that legal relation?

The argument Ibn Rushd reports. Ibn Rushd begins from deposit as a trust ordinarily free of compensatory liability, then distinguishes claims of return, loss, delegated delivery, transgression and profit. The strength and reach of the entrustment relation explain several evidentiary differences. A rule about loss is not automatically a rule about proving return.

Uṣūl: the inferential work. Read the command to restore trusts, the requirement of witnessing delivery to orphans, and the analogical or distinguishing arguments about recipients and claims. The Ḍarūrī’s continuity discussion offers a question about change of subject; it is not the expressly stated master explanation of this book.

Maxims and chapter-level rules. The trustee’s ordinary non-liability is a substantive generalization with exceptions. Custom supplies the standard of appropriate safekeeping. A maxim becomes useful only by identifying the entrusted property, authorized custody, breach and resulting liability; “certainty” alone cannot decide whose assertion is accepted.

Purpose: its actual role. Making safekeeping possible and preserving property illuminate the institution. They do not establish whether the trustee’s oath suffices in a witnessed deposit, or how profit from unauthorized trading is allocated. Those conclusions require the distinct arguments Ibn Rushd reports.

Application and the remaining burden. Determine whether the conduct counts as transgression and whether restitution restores the earlier relation. This is why the unchanged physical object does not guarantee an unchanged legal situation. The rule’s application depends on facts, permissions, custom and proof together.

Injury: equivalence and the prevention of an excess

When can a response to injury remain equivalent without creating a different or greater injury?

The argument Ibn Rushd reports. Ibn Rushd distinguishes the offender, injured person, kind of injury and consequence. The permission or obligation of retaliation is not sufficient to establish that an operation can reproduce an injury safely. He also reports different positions on group action, intention, delay until healing, and unintended death during execution.

Uṣūl: the inferential work. Identify the scope of the scriptural rule and the significance of the report excluding specified dangerous injuries. Extension to injuries with the same relevant danger can be compared with the Ḍarūrī’s indicated universal. It must not be collapsed into resemblance-analogy in fixed penalties.

Maxims and chapter-level rules. The later rule that harm is not removed by equivalent harm supplies a critical comparison only after “equivalent harm” is legally specified. It cannot erase the authorized remedial distinction between wrongdoing and a proportionate response, or decide the disputed measure of equivalence.

Purpose: its actual role. Justice, protection of life and restraint of excess explain why the feasibility conditions matter. They are not a licence to invent a penalty thought socially useful. The purpose helps account for the constraint; the authorization and precise consequence remain separate questions.

Application and the remaining burden. Medical assessment and the anticipated course of the wound matter for whether equivalence is possible and when judgment can be executed. A universal condition requires contingent expertise. Injury compensation and coercive authority therefore cannot be inferred from an abstract end alone.

Bidāya II, pp.490–494, §56.2 (Nyazee); Arabic scan II, pp.405–409 · Ḍarūrī, reader §212 · Ḍarūrī, reader §219

Judgment: competence, a rule of conduct and the validity of its result

Who may judge, under what conditions of attention, and with what effect if those conditions are breached?

The argument Ibn Rushd reports. The discussion of anger is not just advice about character. Mālik extends the prohibition to thirst, hunger, fear and similar disturbance of understanding. Ibn Rushd then distinguishes the improper condition of judging from whether a correct judgment issued in that condition takes effect, and raises the alternative reading that prohibition entails invalidity.

Uṣūl: the inferential work. An indicated reason permits comparison among states affecting attention; another methodological question concerns prohibition and validity. The qualification passages in the Ḍarūrī concern the jurist’s instruments and issue-specific competence, while the Bidāya also examines appointment and delegated authority.

Maxims and chapter-level rules. A public-interest maxim can evaluate the exercise of office, but does not decide who is authorized or whether an otherwise correct judgment is void. A prudential rule for choosing the time to judge must be distinguished from a rule determining the legal effect of the judgment.

Purpose: its actual role. Reliable and just judgment explains the restriction on disturbed attention. The closing account of virtue gives a wider end of legal practices; it does not supply a short circuit around evidence, competence or office. Formation of a just person and validity of a particular decision are connected but distinct.

Application and the remaining burden. Ascertain the judge’s disturbance, competence and jurisdiction; assess the decision separately. The wider duty to preserve the means of judgment becomes an operative rule through stated conditions and consequences, not through the word “justice” alone.

What still needs testing

The model is deliberately falsifiable. A proposed qānūn must survive examination of its scope and counterexamples. An apparent maqṣad may be only an after-the-fact explanation. A maxim may summarize a school’s conclusions without authorizing them. The same purpose may support rival practical forms, and a different source reading may change the conclusion without changing the purpose.

The next substantive checks are: collate the complete passages against their scans; reconstruct each school’s inference separately; compare the relevant Mustaṣfā argument rather than its title; add primary qawāʿid witnesses, especially the introductions and distinctions in Qarāfī’s Furūq; test the role of benefit against the illness-marriage discussion; and investigate how Ibn Rushd’s uses of qānūn in the Bidāya relate to the stricter instrumental opening of the Ḍarūrī and the logical works. The separate qawānīn dossier establishes important distinctions but does not complete this legal concordance. [QD]
