REP-02. Ablution: repeated washing and renewed attention

Division 2: Ablution.

Ibn Rushd: summary of the ruling and disagreement. Ibn Rushd reports that Mālik, al-Shāfiʿī, Aḥmad, Abū Thawr and Dāwūd make intention a condition of valid ablution; Abū Ḥanīfa and al-Thawrī do not. His stated reason is ablution’s double character: it combines worship with cleanliness. Its closer resemblance to worship whose purpose is not rationally intelligible, or to cleansing whose purpose is understood, changes whether intention is required for validity. REP-09. Zakāt: provision and the transformation of attachment

Follow the argument RulingLegal reasoningScripture

Division 9: Zakat.

Ibn Rushd: summary of the ruling and disagreement. Ibn Rushd reports disagreement over zakāt on a minor’s wealth. Mālik and al-Shāfiʿī are among those who require it; other jurists deny it, while Abū Ḥanīfa distinguishes agricultural produce from other assets. The central reason is whether zakāt is understood as worship, which requires maturity, or as an obligatory right of the poor in the wealth of the rich. The same transfer can therefore be considered from the giver’s responsibility and the recipient’s claim. Bidāya Arabic I, printed/PDF pp.244–246

Text and reported setting. Bukhārī2239–2241 situates advance payment in Medina, where people paid ahead for produce over two or three years. The instruction specifies known measure, known weight and a known term.

Ethical force of the passage. The parties cooperate across time: one receives resources before delivery, and the other relies on future performance. Naming a measure and term makes that reliance answerable and reduces opportunities to exploit incompatible expectations. It does not eliminate harvest risk or prove every later credit instrument permissible.

How the commentators read it. Ibn Ḥajar explains why local measures must be specified when they differ and preserves disagreement over whether salam is a concession for need or a contract intelligible within the general rules. The concrete wisdom is coordination under delayed performance; its legal classification remains disputed.

Follow the argument RulingLegal reasoningScripture

Division 43: Interdiction.

Ibn Rushd: summary of the ruling and disagreement. Ibn Rushd distinguishes reaching maturity from showing sound judgment in managing property (). Mālik and al-Shāfiʿī permit a judge to impose restriction on an adult whose wastefulness is established; Abū Ḥanīfa rejects initiating restriction on adults. The first argument extends the protection of minors to anyone displaying the relevant imprudence. The opposing argument treats minority as the operative impediment and maturity as the usual sign of reason and sound judgment. The reach of protection, and what warrants removing a person’s control, are therefore disputed. Bidāya Arabic II, printed/PDF pp.279–281

How the legal argument works. · . These notes identify the relevant mechanism or comparative control; later maxims do not establish Ibn Rushd’s historical vocabulary.

Text and reported setting. commands return of trusts to their owners and just judgment, ending with God as hearing and seeing. considers reliance on personal trust where documentary security is absent: the entrusted person must discharge the trust and fear God his Lord; testimony must not be concealed, and concealment makes the heart culpable.

Ethical force of the passage. The two endings address the gap between an obligation and another person’s ability to enforce it. The trusted holder must not treat the absence of a record as permission to retain what is owed; the witness must not make the truth disappear through silence. God’s hearing, seeing and knowing deny that an undetectable breach becomes morally nonexistent. The reference to the heart concerns deliberate suppression of truth, not a substitute mystical method of proving a claim.

How the commentators read it. Ibn ʿĀshūr reads as addressing everyone entrusted with something and everyone judging, with delivery to the rightful recipient at its centre. He notes the weak chain of the Kaʿba-key occasion rather than treating it as certain. On he explains that an unsecured debt is called a trust to intensify responsibility: lack of pledge or witnesses does not diminish what is owed. This is related to deposit, though a debt and custody of a particular object remain different legal relations. Al-Rāzī distinguishes trusts owed to God, to other people and to oneself. His examples include truthful speech, uncorrupted measures, rulers’ justice, and scholars refusing to recruit ordinary people into partisan error; self-trust requires not sacrificing enduring good to appetite or anger. He also records al-Qāḍī’s narrower reading of this verse as returnable property. On , his preferred explanation is reciprocal: the creditor has dealt well with the debtor by trusting him, so the debtor must not answer that kindness with denial or delay. A witness can prevent that confidence from being exploited. The Study Quran places at the opening of a passage on authority and reports a reciprocal reading: rulers are first charged with justice, then subjects with obedience in 4:59. Its broader account includes deposits, borrowed or found property and spiritual responsibilities. This is the editors’ synthesis of several commentators; its report of the Kaʿba-key occasion must be read alongside Ibn ʿĀshūr’s criticism of the chain. The Study Quran also extends responsible authority to household and body, and reads the paired names Hearing and Seeing as affirming divine knowledge. Its suggestion about hearing’s primacy and the creative command is presented as a possibility, not an established explanation of the deposit rule; this manuscript does not use it to derive a legal or alchemical conclusion. On , The Study Quran distinguishes unsecured trust from a pledge and then connects concealed testimony with accountability for what is inward. It also records the broader reading that troubled the Companions. Its discussion distinguishes being called to account from necessarily being punished; passing thoughts must not be equated with deliberately hiding a claimant’s evidence.

It combines withholding unrestricted control, provision, honourable speech, testing judgment, timely return and accountability for the guardian. Ibn ʿĀshūr reads the reference to “your wealth” through the connected livelihood of the community: another’s property sustains hiring, exchange, giving and inheritance. REP-50. Deposit: being trusted and proving what happened

Follow the argument RulingLegal reasoningScripture

Division 50: Deposits.

Ibn Rushd: summary of the ruling and disagreement. Ibn Rushd presents a deposit as an entrusted holding rather than a guarantee against every loss. He then distinguishes safeguarding it from proving its return. The well-known Mālikī position requires proof of return where the original handover was witnessed, while accepting the custodian’s account of destruction. Al-Shāfiʿī, Abū Ḥanīfa and one report from Ibn al-Qāsim reject that distinction. The disagreement asks whether the manner of entrusting property changes the evidential force of the custodian’s word; neither position makes actual betrayal impossible. Ibn Rushd: summary of the ruling and disagreement. Ibn Rushd distinguishes intentional injury from accidents and disputed intermediate acts. An oath, a hidden reservation and another person’s right. Al-Nawawī gives his school’s detailed distinction: in an authorized judicial oath the judge’s intended matter controls, so equivocation does not avoid the oath. Outside that setting, a reservation can affect whether the oath is broken; nevertheless it is impermissible to use it to cancel someone’s rightful claim. He then reports more differentiated Mālikī accounts, including whether the oath was demanded or volunteered and whether deceit was intended. His account preserves the difference between the oath’s technical effect and the wrong of depriving another.

Al-Nawawī explains the need to teach commanders what is obligatory, permitted and forbidden, together with care for their followers. Method and reading guide

al-Ḍarūrī · method · Arabic / EnglishKnowledge, action and the work of legal method

A science, a rule and a judgment. In the opening of al-Ḍarūrī, Ibn Rushd distinguishes knowledge by its end, by its degree of generality, and by the instrumental inquiry that guides correct thinking. Knowledge directed toward action may be general and comparatively remote from action, or particular and closer to it. Rules concerning prayer and zakāt are examples of particular practical knowledge; “particular” does not mean only a single event. The inquiry governing warranted thought is another distinction. These axes prevent the shortcut “uṣūl is general meaning, fiqh is its mechanical application.” The historical discipline of uṣūl has a broader organization than the instrumental core singled out here. al-Ḍarūrī §§2–9, Akl edition, PDF pp.128–133
