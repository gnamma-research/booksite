From a common concept to metaphysical unity

The transitions between intelligibility, true predication, dependence and unity each need their own argument.

This is an editorial reconstruction from the linked passages. It is not a translated passage, a completed history of the controversy, or proof that every author directly answered every other author.

1. A concept and its instances

Wujūd means existence; mawjūd means existent. A claim that one concept is predicated across different subjects must be distinguished from a claim that those subjects share one numerically identical extramental reality. The former does not entail the latter without additional premises. Likewise, calling a concept universal does not by itself identify an externally existing universal entity.

Lāhījī’s closing objection makes the distinction concrete. Even if a self-subsisting existential reality must be a real particular, why must the concept of existence cease to be universal? Why could it not have real instances, some self-subsisting and others dependent upon contingent quiddities? The passage challenges an inference, not merely a choice of terminology. Read C31L06.

2. What makes predication true?

Ḥaml is predication; ittiṣāf is a subject’s being qualified by a predicate. In Yazdī’s reported objection, the mere external presence of a body and of whiteness does not establish that the body is white. The relation needed for the particular predication must also be accounted for. The relevant title, al-Sayyid al-Sanad, is probably Dashtakī here on the edition’s contextual evidence; it does not identify which original Ṣadrī gloss supplies the passage. Read WIT07 and the continued argument.

This also prevents an immediate slide from “existence and quiddity can be considered distinctly” to a finished account of how existence belongs to a thing. Māhiyya, quiddity or what a thing is, and its being existent must be distinguished before their relation can be explained. Their conceptual distinction alone does not settle the mode of their extramental relation.

3. Objective truth and merely supposed subjects

Nafs al-amr, the fact of the matter, concerns the ground of a judgment’s truth. The catalogue-reported ending of Ghiyāth’s work distinguishes a subject-description that obtains in the fact of the matter from one introduced only by supposition. In the latter case the relevant condition must be retained when evaluating the proposition.

This supplies a bounded comparison for absent or negative subjects. It does not establish a complete Ghiyāth theory. The catalogue prints الإنصاف; the translation conjecturally reads الاتصاف, “qualification.” The original terminal folio and local speaker remain unverified. Read the text and apparatus.

4. Conceiving an opposite and combining opposites

Qūshjī’s first objection distinguishes one opposite’s belonging to the other from both opposites’ belonging to a single subject. His second tests whether the proposed appeal to different respects would also excuse the genuinely contradictory case. His third asks whether the explanation makes the word “together” redundant. The fuller directly inspected second objection makes the reductio’s repeated structure visible. Read the interpretation under criticism, then all three objections.

The criticism does not deny noncontradiction. It asks whether the proposed account has correctly identified the locus and respects of opposition. The earlier abbreviated quotation through Lāhījī remains a separate reporting witness, WIT14.

5. Gradation and the reality that is said to vary

Tashkīk denotes graded or unequal predication in this discussion. Priority or greater appropriateness (awlawiyya), precedence (aqdamiyya), greater intensity (ashaddiyya), and increase or decrease (ziyāda wa-nuqṣān) must not be made interchangeable. Causal precedence is not simply earlier time.

Lāhījī first presents a charitable interpretation: distinct existential realities may vary in manifestations and determinations without a variation in the essential reality considered apart from quiddities. He speaks of differing manifestations of existence’s effects. This interpretive proposal must be read through its qualifications, rather than treated as a bare affirmation of numerical monism. C31L01, C31L02, C31L03.

6. What the argument for the Necessary establishes

Lāhījī then reports another argument. A concept other than existence needs existence in order to be existent. What needs another for existence is contingent. The Necessary must therefore be existence that exists through itself. The reporting passage explicitly includes either needing another as producer or needing it as one’s existence; those are not the same relation. Read the complete reported argument.

The next passage reports an interpretation associated with al-Muḥaqqiq al-Sharīf and adopted by Dawwānī: existence is a real particular, while “existent” is a universal predicate applying through relations to self-subsisting existence. This is Lāhījī’s mediated report, not an independently collated original Dawwānī gloss. C31L05.

7. The missing inference to strong unity

Lāhījī’s final objection asks how the reported argument excludes other existential realities that depend upon quiddities. Granting self-subsisting existence does not itself show that every existential reality is self-subsisting, or that dependent instances are unreal. The argument still needs a premise excluding them. C31L06.

The provisional comparative result is therefore a set of distinctions: conceptual unity, universal predication, objective truth, graded manifestation, causal dependence and numerical unity. The current passages establish particular arguments and objections about these relations; they do not warrant collapsing every position into one doctrine of waḥdat al-wujūd, unity of existence.

8. A separate causal comparison

Iṣfahānī and Jurjānī’s persistence discussion belongs to Q13–Q16. Jurjānī affirms continuing dependence while denying that the agent must produce a separate external property called persistence. His builder analysis also separates an existence-giving cause from preparatory movements. Iṣfahānī’s dilemma and reply; Jurjānī on the builder; sustaining qualification.

These distinctions provide questions for a future comparison with Aquinas on conservation and divine concurrence. They do not yet establish doctrinal equivalence: the corresponding Thomist primary loci must be selected, translated and compared on their own terms.
