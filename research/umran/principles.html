Foundations · an introduction

Work belongs within a life ordered towards God.

Our first concern is not to identify prestigious occupations. It is to clarify what human beings are for, what goods a community must secure, and how different people can contribute without confusing worldly usefulness with spiritual rank.

1. The end of human life

The ultimate end of the human person is not employment, productivity or the maximisation of measurable effects. For the Islamic account, the end is knowledge of God, worship and nearness to Him. A street cleaner, a parent, a researcher and a person currently without employment are not spiritually ordered by their occupational titles.

Work matters, nevertheless. It can meet responsibilities, cultivate habits, sustain a household, create legitimate goods and enable the institutions through which people live well together.

Two evaluations must remain separate.

We may assess the adequacy of a communal function and a person's fitness for contributing to it. We do not claim to measure that person's spiritual merit.

2. Self, household and city

A classical division of practical philosophy, articulated by Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī, distinguishes the formation of character, the governance of the household and the ordering of the city. These dimensions continually affect each other. The self is not first perfected and only then permitted to contribute to the world.

Al-Ghazālī's account of livelihood and useful crafts, al-Fārābī's orientation of civic association towards human felicity, and Ibn Khaldūn's analysis of occupational cooperation enrich this picture. Comparative study of Guénon, Coomaraswamy and traditions of vocation must preserve their differences rather than assert that their social doctrines are interchangeable.

3. Farḍ kifāya and communal sufficiency

Farḍ kifāya is an obligation whose adequate performance by some generally discharges it from others, subject to its legal conditions. The obligation cannot be defined merely by naming a desirable career. It concerns a good or function whose provision is required.

Whether there is a deficiency depends upon the object of provision, the affected population, the available means and the competence, accessibility and reliability of what is already supplied. Legal classification and empirical assessment are different tasks.

Classical discussions are not uniform. Al-Ghazālī in the Iḥyāʾ treats essential sciences and crafts as communal obligations, while al-Nawawī records disputes over the legal status of some crafts. That disagreement must remain visible in the research.

4. Seven tests for a candidate communal obligation

Normative foundation

What recognised evidence establishes that the required good is obligatory, rather than simply worthwhile?

Object of provision

What precise function must be secured—not which fashionable occupation should be promoted?

Relevant community

Whose duty and whose need are involved: household, locality, region or a wider association?

Standard of adequacy

What quantity, competence, access and continuity would constitute sufficient provision?

Actual deficiency

What dated and place-specific evidence demonstrates that provision is inadequate?

Means and alternatives

Which effective and permissible arrangements would remedy the deficiency?

Qualification and responsibility

Who can perform, prepare or enable the required work, and what would make a personal duty arise?

5. Vocation is not a job ranking

Al-Shāṭibī explicitly distinguishes the collective obligation from the qualified persons upon whom its performance may fall in particular circumstances. Those unable to perform a specialised function may still be able to support or prepare qualified practitioners.

A person's abilities matter, but so do their household responsibilities, opportunities, interests and acquired malakāt. An aptitude is not the same as an inherited job title. Nor does being well suited to a field automatically establish that entering it is personally obligatory.

6. What we learn from 80,000 Hours

80,000 Hours usefully connects foundational arguments, problem profiles, occupation reviews, skill-building, career plans and concrete opportunities. We retain that progression. We do not inherit its underlying commitment to rank careers by expected impartial social impact.

Our sequence runs from human ends, through legitimate communal functions and actual deficiencies, to suitable people and practical means. A stable and honourable ordinary livelihood may be the most appropriate path for a person, even where it has no exceptional global impact.

7. What practice can—and cannot—tell us

Malaysian institutional research helps us examine the relationships between legal responsibility, household sufficiency, education and the work of community organisations. Crowdfunding campaigns, such as those on LaunchGood, show the interventions that communities propose, but campaign popularity, a financial target or an organiser's narrative cannot by themselves prove an unmet collective obligation.
