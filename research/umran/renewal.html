Tajdīd · Iḥyāʾ · Iṣlāḥ

Renew the intellectual life, not just its institutions.

A traditional project must attend to what a community understands, teaches, transmits, practises and creates. A large library of books or an increase in educational institutions can coexist with the loss of intellectual judgment, scholarly succession and moral formation.

Civilizational renewal is not a contest to reproduce the past unchanged or to adopt the present uncritically. It is the disciplined recovery and living exercise of truth, competence, and right order amid changing circumstances.

1. What renewal means—and what it does not

The well-known report in Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4291 provides a classical reference for renewal of the religion. Its interpretation and its applications belong to a broader history of tajdīd, iḥyāʾ and iṣlāḥ. A research institution should not claim to be the promised renewer, nor treat its preferred modern programme as religiously compulsory merely because it is described as renewal.

Al-Ghazālī's Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn is especially instructive. The problem it addresses is not a lack of religious information alone but the failure of learning and practice to form the person. The ordered relation between worship, ordinary life, vices and saving virtues is integral to the project.

Renewal may therefore involve continuity, recovery, disciplined criticism, correction, and new intellectual expression. It need not entail inventing a new school or a new institution every time a question arises.

2. What must actually be renewed?

Some necessary capacities are relatively visible: competent Arabic reading, legal method, trained teachers, sound information systems, or the ability to deliver medical care. Others are qualitative: sound judgment, trustworthy disagreement, the capacity to reason from first principles, or the relationship between knowledge and spiritual practice.

Loss of capacity can occur even when a tradition possesses large numbers of books, graduates and public lectures. Conversely, a small and competent teaching community may sustain a discipline across generations. The unit of enquiry must therefore often be a living practice and its transmission, not simply a count of publications or schools.

3. Four movements of intellectual renewal

Recover the sources

Establish responsible access to primary texts, methods, historical contexts and legitimate varieties of interpretation. Avoid making machine-readable text the same as a critically established reading.

Reconstitute competence

Develop learners into practitioners through ordered instruction, guided reading, argument, exercise and acquired malakāt. Capable teachers and scholarly communities are indispensable.

Engage living questions

Investigate modern science, technology, law, political institutions and economic systems with competence in both the traditional and contemporary disciplines. Neither uncritical borrowing nor automatic rejection counts as understanding.

Express and test the results

Produce curricula, scholarly commentaries, public guidance, institutional designs and actual practices, and subject them to criticism, empirical examination and qualified review.

4. The positive aim: formation of intellectual life

A curriculum in logic, metaphysics, uṣūl, jurisprudence, language or the philosophy of technology need not be justified merely as a response to an emergency. It may positively cultivate the conditions of truth-seeking and mature judgment. Such a programme may serve the cultivation of intellect and religious understanding without thereby creating an automatic legal obligation to fund any particular textbook or project.

For instance, a serious programme engaging contemporary computing could begin with logical, philosophical and jurisprudential foundations; continue through mathematics, software and the structures of information; then examine how technical systems mediate perception, authority, memory and decision. It should generate competent researchers and practitioners, not just slogans about technology.

5. Institutions support the purpose; they do not replace it

An institute, waqf, university or digital library can sustain renewal by providing teachers, time, tools and coordination. It can also become insulated from critique, too dependent on patrons, or preoccupied with credentials and prestige. Its success must be judged by the capabilities and practices it actually sustains, not merely by the fact that it exists.

Contemporary scholarship on collective iḥyāʾ raises precisely the question of when renewal can be institutionalised without becoming a narrow political or ideological movement. The answer cannot simply be “make everything a new institution.”

Intellectual and spiritual restraint

Renewal does not confer moral superiority

A researcher publishing a sophisticated metaphysical study cannot infer spiritual rank from the supposed historical importance of that work. The same holds for an administrator establishing a school. A sincere caregiver, a skilled sanitation worker and a scholar are subject to different circumstances and responsibilities, but their nearness to God cannot be determined from the visibility or influence of their occupations.

Nor may a proposed renewal programme claim legal compulsion solely by invoking tajdīd. The legal status of its functions, the adequacy of existing provision and the legitimacy of its means remain separate issues.
