# How Ibn Rushd establishes qawānīn

## Primary-text findings, passage dossier, and a research program

**Expanded research edition, 19 October 2026.**

This dossier investigates three connected questions: how Ibn Rushd develops procedures for discovering and warranting rules; which earlier texts supply the closest antecedents; and where comparable inquiries occur in later logic and legal theory. It reports a substantial direct reading of the sources, distinguishes textual dependence from comparison, and specifies the remaining work needed for a publishable genealogy.

The expanded investigation adds complete readings of the early Rushdian Burhān and Ibn Ṭumlūs's Taḥlīl; a continuous reading of the final definition and principle-acquisition discussion in the Middle Burhān; selected complete argumentative units in Ghazālī's Qisṭās and Miḥakk; and new readings of Ṭūsī, Quṭb, Jurjānī, and the Nasafī–Samarqandī tradition. **Sections 16–20 present the detailed new evidence.** Sections 1–15 have been revised to reflect it. Section 11 records the precise reading coverage; sections 12 and 20 distinguish completed investigation from the remaining critical comparisons.

References to the **Ḍarūrī** use Jamāl al-Dīn al-ʿAlawī's 1994 edition. References to Avicenna's **Burhān** use Badawī's 1954 edition. These paragraph/page systems must not be silently replaced by those of other editions. A complete source and access register follows the findings.

## 1. The principal finding

The strongest immediate logical antecedent is **al-Fārābī's Kitāb al-Taḥlīl**. Ibn Rushd's early treatise on the **Qawānīn/Mawāḍiʿ** develops a corresponding productive inquiry and explicitly calls the relevant operation **taḥlīl**, a part of logic. It also examines the rules themselves: defining the logical instrument from its characteristic operation, correcting a predecessor's definition, proving less evident consequences, and rejecting invalid variants. The source therefore supplies more than a catalogue of rules for application. [R2; F1]

The now-read **Middle Topics 73–78** retains the functional requirement that loci supply premises and form. It explains a qānūn through finite general resources that make indefinitely many particular constructions available, and separates loci proper from other preparations for finding premises. This gives an explicit account of the rule’s nature, to be distinguished from proofs of individual rules. [R4]

For **legal methodological rules**, the Ḍarūrī and Ghazālī's works supply another indispensable line of evidence. Rules are established or corrected through linguistic investigation, earlier juristic practice, arguments about authorized evidence, and the requirements of a functioning practice of adjudication. Ghazālī's Miʿyār contains substantial legal methodology inside a logic book; Shifāʾ al-ghalīl explicitly investigates the warrant behind the warrant of analogy. [R1; G1–G3]

The Posterior Analytics tradition supplies accounts of definitions, principles, causes, experience, and scientific organization. Its conjunction with constructive syllogistic and topical inquiry is explicit: **Ibn Rushd, Middle Burhān § 136, p. 167**, says that discovering definitions requires the loci of the Topics. His Middle Topics p. 12 describes preparing a science's premises, questions, and syllogisms for examination by demonstrative qawānīn; Ibn Ṭumlūs's Jadal p. 308 closely parallels it. [R4; R6; T1]

Later commentators also discuss **how to establish the rules themselves**. Ṭūsī distinguishes discovering the exact modal consequence from confirming an already proposed consequence. Jurjānī and Quṭb distinguish proving entailment universally from defeating a stronger proposed rule with a counterexample. Samarqandī's commentary asks literally how a universal principle can be confirmed through examples, while exposing the difficulty of missing causal conditions. These are specific methodological arguments in logic and regulated inquiry, not deductions from suggestive book titles. [L4b; L6; S12]

These findings support **several connected practices of methodological inquiry**, rather than a single procedure whose historical name was simply “uṣūl.” The terminology must be tied to the object and level of inquiry.

### Four questions that must remain distinct

| Question | Example | What would answer it? |
|---|---|---|
| What makes something the relevant instrument? | What is a locus/topos? | A definition identifying its characteristic function. |
| Why is this general rule legitimate? | Why does a particular inferential pattern conclude? Why may a jurist act on a kind of evidence? | A logical proof, a field-specific authorization argument, or another appropriate warrant. |
| How does one discover a result using the instrument? | Find a middle term, definition, intended universal meaning, or operative legal cause. | A productive procedure guiding investigation. |
| What epistemic status does the result deserve? | Is the conclusion demonstrative, probable, or merely accepted by an interlocutor? | Assessment of the premises, relation, scope, and manner of acquisition. |

The second row is closest to “figuring out the qawānīn themselves.” A passage about the third row is relevant evidence, but does not by itself establish a method for the second.

## 2. What the Ḍarūrī actually does

### 2.1 The opening gives a program and evidence for its historical construction

In §§ 2–4 Ibn Rushd characterizes an instrumental kind of knowledge by its capacity to direct inquiry correctly. His comparison with measuring instruments concerns the role of rules in correcting judgment. He explains why a later, more dispersed and complicated legal practice requires an explicit art even if earlier practitioners possessed its capacity without a formal textbook. Many of its universals, he says, were validated by examining the Companions' individual judgments. [R1, §§ 2–4]

Section 6 gives a more precise account of the discipline. The inquiry proper to it is the third part: evidence used to derive particular rulings and how to use that evidence. The other parts can be stated according to a received position, or treated comparatively by showing how different foundational commitments generate different forms of derivation. The latter would make the art useful across juristic positions. Section 9's omission of Ghazālī's logical introduction is consequently a statement about the organization and sequence of learning; it does not establish rejection of logic. [R1, §§ 5–9]

The name itself receives comment in § 6: Ibn Rushd says the received title *uṣūl al-fiqh* derives from one of the elements included in the conventional curriculum, while the properly methodological inquiry is the third part. Thus the discipline's inherited name, its actual contents, and its appropriate scientific genus must be distinguished. This is a particularly relevant passage for asking whether methodology simply means *uṣūl*. [R1, § 6]

**Interpretation:** this gives a conception of a field-specific instrumental discipline. It permits an account of inference relative to different accepted starting points. It does not make every substantive legal premise a theorem of general logic.

### 2.2 A rule about delayed clarification is corrected by classifying the cases

The discussion of delayed clarification distinguishes different kinds of indeterminacy and partial information. At the end of § 163, Ibn Rushd diagnoses the unrestricted rival positions as partly false in the way conclusions from an incomplete induction can be false. The work of improving the rule consists in distinguishing the kinds of linguistic case that the rival formulations had grouped together. [R1, §§ 161–163, pp. 104–107]

This is a direct instance of a procedure for correcting a methodological generalization:

1. Identify the opposed unrestricted rules.
2. Inspect the linguistic cases adduced for them.
3. Determine whether those cases belong to one relevant kind.
4. Restrict the rules in accordance with the distinctions the cases require.

Ghazālī's corresponding Mustaṣfā discussion already distinguishes an unintelligible utterance from a command conveying useful partial information. The innovation should therefore be studied as a particular reclassification and criticism within an existing debate. [G1, third quṭb, discussion of delaying bayān]

### 2.3 Apparent meaning: linguistic evidence and legal authority are different warrants

Section 166 is especially valuable. Ibn Rushd separates the induction through which an expression's apparent meaning is recognized from the grounds for treating that meaning as legally authoritative. The latter involve Companion practice and the absence of a new legislative convention. He also argues from the consequences for religious practice of demanding complete unambiguity. He then relates the strength of the apparent meaning to the strength of evidence needed to depart from it. [R1, § 166, pp. 107–108]

The methodological lesson is that **a fact about meaning and a norm authorizing reliance on that meaning are different propositions**. Finding the former does not automatically establish the latter. This passage should be central to any account of how the legal qawānīn are warranted.

### 2.4 Solitary reports: a functional argument is disputed and repaired

In §§ 81–82 Ibn Rushd supports reliance on solitary reports partly by considering what the alternative would do to ordinary legal practice. The relevant institutions cannot require mass transmission for every fact. Ghazālī had answered a related argument by invoking original non-liability when evidence fails. Ibn Rushd distinguishes that exceptional evidential collapse from the normal circumstances under which individual reports circulate. [R1, pp. 71–72; G1, discussion of the obligation to act on khabar al-wāḥid]

This is an argument about the conditions under which a practice can perform its function. The logical form alone does not supply the institutional and historical premises.

### 2.5 The final discussion limits the ambition of complete codification

At §§ 256–257 Ibn Rushd describes detailed rules of preference as almost without limit. He nevertheless gives a warrant for following the stronger probability: Companion agreement and reflective awareness of what happens when competing probabilities are assessed. [R1, p. 146]

This endpoint matters. The work does not promise a finitely enumerated algorithm that generates every warranted preference. It combines general norms with cultivated judgment about particular evidence.

### 2.6 Further worked cases and a control against inflated originality

| Passage | Methodological operation | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| §§ 26–35, pp. 47–51 | Distinguish opposed normative predicates and respects; apply this to prayer on usurped land. | Ghazālī already has the central two-respects argument and evidence from Companion practice. |
| §§ 185–186, pp. 114–115 | Reconcile apparent disconnected exception with its function by examining contextual meaning. | A later explicit attribution supplies an actual reception case; see § 8. |
| §§ 212–224, pp. 125–132 | Distinguish semantic substitution from analogical transfer; enumerate and eliminate possible intended universals. | Discovering the intended universal is distinct from warranting the rule licensing this investigation. |
| § 239, p. 139 | Preserve the possibility of error relative to an intended legal meaning. | Probable inquiry does not entail that all opposed answers are true. |

The common research error would be to treat every use of a logical distinction as a new discovery by Ibn Rushd. The Mustaṣfā comparisons show why the exact transformation of an argument matters more than a general contrast between “philosopher” and “jurist.”

### 2.7 Māzarī supplies an earlier legal comparison

In the chapter on matters taken from language in **Īḍāḥ al-maḥṣūl**, Māzarī describes universal qawānīn governing inquiry into the Lawgiver's speech. Their universality is relative to the particular legal questions they govern. He distinguishes such rules from isolated lexical questions useful for one or two cases and comments critically on inherited organization. The relevant chapter was read in the Arabic e-text, which contains lacunae. A second e-text now supplies the printed-page marker **158** in the al-Ṭālibī edition; the original page image remains to be checked. [M1]

This is a close legal antecedent to compare with the Ḍarūrī's opening. The shared conception does not on its own prove that Ibn Rushd borrowed the wording or arrangement from Māzarī.

## 3. Ibn Rushd's early Qawānīn: a productive and self-examining part of logic

### 3.1 The text and the witnesses inspected

The treatise usually identified by its subject as the **rules by which syllogisms are made, called loci** survives in the early logical corpus. The relevant Munich witness is **BSB Cod. arab. 964, fols. 30a–41b**, in Judeo-Arabic. The corresponding historical Latin translation, by **Abraham de Balmes**, appears in the 1562 Venice logical volume at **fols. 62r–67v**, under a Topics heading. [R2]

All twelve Latin pages were read and checked visually; all twenty-four relevant manuscript pages were inspected against the sequence, with selected central Arabic sentences independently checked. This is a substantive whole-treatise reading through a historical translation, accompanied by targeted manuscript collation. It is not a diplomatic edition of the Arabic witness.

The Munich text ends immediately before **Kitāb al-Burhān**. This location corresponds to the announced progression from the account of syllogism to productive inquiry and then demonstration. The Latin printed order differs; the Topics label alone can obscure the text's distinctive function.

### 3.2 Identifying the instrument from what it does

At **31a / Latin 62vG**, Ibn Rushd investigates what loci are through their characteristic operations. The criterion is demanding: a locus must help locate suitable premises and a form from which the required conclusion follows. An arrangement that does not conclude cannot qualify merely because its premises concern the relevant terms.

This functional account is then used to criticize the definition attributed to Abū Naṣr al-Fārābī. Describing loci simply as universal premises does not adequately articulate their role in producing particular syllogisms and their forms. [R2, 31a–32b / 62v–63r]

### 3.3 The explicit name: taḥlīl

At **33a / Latin 63vG–H**, the author describes resolving a proposed problem into subject and predicate, searching under loci for establishing or refuting it, and obtaining the corresponding syllogism. He then names this operation **taḥlīl**, explicitly a part of logic. [R2]

Thus there is a precise historical answer to one aspect of the nomenclature question. It remains necessary to distinguish this productive analysis of a problem from other uses of taḥlīl, including the analysis of an argument already supplied.

### 3.4 A rule is proved, not merely exemplified

The clearest warrant occurs at **36a / Latin 65rA–B**. The inferential pattern is validated through conversion of a premise and conversion of the conclusion. Its modern reconstruction is:

- No S is M.
- Every P is M.
- Therefore no S is P.

To verify it, convert the first premise to “No M is S.” Combined with “Every P is M,” this gives “No P is S” in the first figure. Convert that conclusion. The result is the original target. [R2]

This shows how a less evident rule can receive its warrant from a more evident valid construction. The truth of the domain premises remains separate. Proving this form does not establish the metaphysical claims used to illustrate it.

### 3.5 What the complete inventory contributes

The treatise proceeds through division, composition, definition, entailment, opposition, and selected further loci. It rejects invalid variants, tracks quantifiers, and distinguishes merely inductive support from demonstrative use. The same general pattern can function differently according to whether its materials are true in the required way, accepted opinion, or an initial appearance. [R2, 33a–41b / 63v–67v]

The right generalization is that Ibn Rushd develops **a productive logic whose rules themselves receive definitions, proofs, and criticism**. The account does not supply every science's substantive premises from logic alone.

## 4. Al-Fārābī: the closest logical antecedent now directly compared

The relevant source is **Kitāb al-Taḥlīl**, in al-Manṭiq ʿinda al-Fārābī, ed. Rafīq al-ʿAjam, II, pp. 95–130. Pages95–110 were read continuously. The comparison covers the opening, several topical families, and a sustained examination of scientific discovery rules. [F1]

The connection is unusually specific: Ibn Rushd names Abū Naṣr; the criticized definition matches the opening definition in the Taḥlīl; the productive sequence closely corresponds; and division, composition, definition, entailment, and opposition follow in comparable order. This is stronger evidence than a shared vocabulary of “rules.”

An essential qualification follows from reading beyond the opening. **Al-Fārābī already supplies syllogistic forms in the ensuing exposition.** Ibn Rushd's objection concerns what the definition expresses. It would be inaccurate to say that Fārābī ignored form until Ibn Rushd introduced it.

### A particularly revealing set of methodological criticisms

At pp. 103–107 Fārābī tests purported discovery rules. Rephrasing the desired universal supplies no independent route to it. Co-presence and co-absence do not, by themselves, distinguish a cause from an accompanying correlate. Half and double provide a control. Coextensivity also fails to establish essence or primary predication. Conversely, mutual predication is stronger than the condition needed for an ordinary first-figure major. The discussion explicitly engages a qānūn associated with Aristotle's treatment of relatives and primary predication. [F1]

The interpretive significance is methodological: establish the proposed rule's exact target, determine what follows from its premises, preserve the premises in a counterexample, and see whether the proposed result fails. A useful rule must be strong enough for its task and need not impose irrelevant additional conditions.

These Farabian passages should be read beside Ibn Rushd's functional definition and rule proofs. The extended causal discussion is not reproduced wholesale in the short Qawānīn, so the comparison does not establish sentence-by-sentence transmission of every criticism.

## 5. Aristotle: three connected textual locations

The original Topics-versus-Posterior alternative omits a crucial third source: **Prior Analytics I.27–30**.

| Text | Exact target | Relevance to this investigation |
|---|---|---|
| Prior Analytics I.27–30, 43a20–46a30 | Discovering syllogisms for proposed problems; acquiring suitable premises. | Closest structural antecedent for constructive proof search. |
| Topics I.1–2, 100a18–101b4 | Topical method and dialectic's contribution to inquiry and principles. | Locates the investigative use of dialectic. |
| Topics I.8, 103b1ff. | Justification of the classification of predicables. | A focused example of warranting a logical classification itself. |
| Topics I.13–18, 105a20ff. | Acquiring premises, distinguishing senses, differences, and similarities. | Supplies organized resources for investigation. |
| Posterior Analytics I.2 and I.7–10 | Demonstrative conditions and principles proper to sciences. | Distinguishes a valid argument from a scientific demonstration. |
| Posterior Analytics II.1–2, II.13, II.19 | Questions, causes, definitions, and acquisition of principles. | Supplies accounts of scientific discovery and starting points. |
| Rhetoric I.1, 1354a1–11 | Investigating why successful practice succeeds. | A structural analogue for constructing an art from its operations; direct borrowing is unproved. |

The research should trace how these resources interact, rather than assign the whole problem to a single Aristotelian title. [AR1–AR5]

## 6. Ghazālī: logic books and explicit arguments about the rules of inquiry

### 6.1 Miʿyār al-ʿilm

The main text of **pp. 160–175** was read in full. Ghazālī treats incomplete induction, concealed universal premises, analogical extension, and several grounds for identifying a relevant legal common feature. On p. 174 he appeals to the Companions' pursuit of the strongest probability while denying that their practice establishes an additional rigid restriction governing every detailed source of probability. Page175 turns this into a procedure: identify the proposed ground of an analogy and compare it against a relevant difference between the cases. [G2]

This directly answers the question about Ghazālī's logic books. They contain investigation of legal methodology, including the grounds for accepting or rejecting constraints on it. A classification of books by title would miss this material.

Elsewhere, **pp. 276–277** gives a constructive procedure for definition with a close verbal parallel in Avicenna's Najāt. **Pp.188–191** treats experience and implicit reasoning; **pp. 256–257** distinguishes probable case knowledge from certain practical obligation. The latter passage states that authorization is certain; it does not itself supply its entire proof. [G2; A3]

### 6.2 Shifāʾ al-ghalīl: the ground of the ground

**Pp.189–203 were read consecutively.** In the debate on admissible suitable causes, p. 190 asks about *mustanad al-mustanad*: why the Companions themselves understood analogy as authorized. The reconstruction invokes patterns of prophetic legislation and instruction. Ghazālī's reply on p. 194 separates analogy's general authorization from the disputed admissibility of a subtype. On p. 195 he challenges an opponent's claim that Companion reasoning was confined to one narrow method. [G3]

This is a second-order inquiry into a methodological warrant. It also distinguishes levels of certainty within that inquiry.

### 6.3 Shifāʾ al-ghalīl: warranting a rule of investigation by its function

**Pp.382–395 were also read consecutively.** Ghazālī distinguishes the complete investigation needed for a jurist's own judgment from the sequence appropriate to debate. Requiring a complete preliminary survey in every debate invites endless allegations of undisclosed alternatives. A concrete rival candidate or counterexample provides a tractable objection. At p. 392 he treats this procedural judgment as certain with respect to what serves inquiry, grounded in extensive practice across comparable debates. [G3]

This is a particularly strong antecedent for the original question: the passage argues about which **rule for conducting inquiry** should be accepted and why. Its warrant appeals to the practice's purpose and to the consequences of competing procedures. It should be compared with the function-based arguments in Ibn Rushd without assuming direct borrowing before a verbal and chronological comparison.

### 6.4 Asās al-qiyās: a further verified bridge and a vocabulary guide

The author's **pp. 4–28 and 101–109** were read in the al-Sadḥān 1993 edition, separately from the editor's introduction and footnotes. Three results deserve inclusion:

- **Pp.11–12:** a universal legal connection requires evidence of the lawgiver's intention, accessible through language, indication, context, silence, and patterns of action. One original instance does not alone license unrestricted generalization.
- **Pp.20–24:** rationally conclusive division must be exhaustive; hidden alternatives, combined causes, or concealed subtypes can defeat a proposed enumeration.
- **Pp.104–107:** *istinbāṭ* is broader than *qiyās*. Extracting an implicit meaning or identifying a cause confined to its original case need not be analogy.

At **p. 26**, Ghazālī himself directs readers to the Qisṭās, Miḥakk, and Miʿyār for rational inferential forms. This provides an internal textual reason for including those works in a complete corpus. [G4]

The cited works have now been pursued directly. **Qisṭās 16–27** examines the warrant of a balance and abstracts an inferential relation from its particular example; **75–79** tests induction, disputed cases, and purportedly exhaustive causal alternatives. **Miḥakk**, under its fourth law on obtaining a definition, uses a counterexample to remove an unjustified condition. Section 17 gives the arguments and their exact limits. [G5–G6]

### What the Ghazālī comparison establishes

Ghazālī already works at three levels: discovering a result, analyzing a general inferential procedure, and justifying the authority or appropriateness of a legal or disputational rule. The Ḍarūrī's relation to him must therefore be reconstructed through specific revisions and retained arguments. A simple opposition between technical philosophy and an unreflective legal tradition is contradicted by the passages examined.

## 7. Avicenna: explicit traffic between Burhān and Jadal

### 7.1 Productive procedures in the Burhān

The examined Burhān material includes **I.9, pp. 45–48; II.7, pp. 104–111; IV.6–7, pp. 233–243; IV.10, pp. 254–257**. These are direct scan readings in Badawī's 1954 edition. [A1]

The central definition chapters organize collection, composition, and division. Particular procedural instructions are explicitly called qānūn at **pp. 237 and 239**. At **p. 241**, suitable topical tests can help establish whether a predicate is a genus or difference. At **p. 257**, the closing discussion expressly recommends appropriate Topics loci for demonstration. Avicenna therefore makes the connection the research question proposed; it is not merely a modern inference from the organization of the Organon.

The experience discussion at **pp. 45–48** restricts generalization to the tested nature and relevant conditions. **II.7** also explains how a conclusion established in one science can function as a principle in another. These are different aspects of scientific methodology: acquisition of a universal, warrant of its scope, and distribution of proof between fields.

### 7.2 Jadal distinguishes making a definition from assessing one

**Jadal V.1, printed p. 241**, distinguishes producing a definition from examining a definition already available, referring the former to the preceding Burhān. The verified source is an edited Arabic e-text with explicit page metadata; its printed scan has not been independently checked. [A2]

### 7.3 The Najāt–Miʿyār comparison

The Najāt section on acquiring a definition and Miʿyār pp. 276–277 share a specific sequence: collect constitutive predicates within the appropriate genus, distinguish mere coextensivity from completeness of essence, and test for a missing constituent. The shared animal example makes the verbal parallel stronger than a generic similarity of doctrines. Exact Najāt printed pagination remains unverified. [A3; G2]

These findings give a strong Avicennan antecedent and a clear Ghazālian comparison. They do not establish that Ibn Rushd's early Qawānīn passage derives directly from Avicenna. The named Farabian engagement supplies stronger immediate evidence for that particular text.

## 8. Later treatments: actual proof search, rule criticism, and reception

### 8.1 Āmidī's Daqāʾiq: general proof discovery before Burhān

In **II 209–211**, Āmidī explains how to acquire premises and construct a proof for a proposed conclusion. The procedure examines the terms and their relevant predicates, looks for a common middle, and chooses an appropriate valid construction. He presents it at the level of general syllogism, before the separate demonstrative and other arts.

**II 212–221** then treats *taḥlīl* of discourse already given: restore missing premises, remove irrelevant additions, recover order, and clarify terms and qualifications. The productive and reconstructive procedures are adjacent but distinct. [L1]

The examined **Burhān**, **II 231–247**, supplies restrictions on experience and universality, and distinguishes a person's acquired certainty from evidence compelling another. The earlier induction section, **II 180–181**, prevents reading this as approval of arbitrary incomplete samples. [L1]

The accessed volume announces twenty-two Burhān chapters but contains only the first four. Its colophon sends the fifth to the next manuscript volume. This is a material limit of the witness and edition; unaccessed chapters cannot be summarized from their titles.

### 8.2 Āmidī's Iḥkām: authorization arguments can themselves be probable

The final assessment of the solitary-report discussion, **II 70–71**, does not promote its preferred consensus argument into a certain proof. The qiyās authorization discussion, **IV 24–55**, likewise explicitly permits a probable warrant for an issue judged probable. **IV 63** rejects an objection premised on the issue's necessary certainty merely because it belongs to uṣūl. [L2]

This changes a tempting thesis: it is insufficient to say that case-rulings are probable while all methodological authorizations are certain. One must distinguish the case-ruling, the authority of an evidence-kind, and the reasoner's obligation to act upon the probability acquired. The passages do not supply a completely worked universal meta-foundation removing every difficulty among those levels.

**IV 56–61** supplies another concrete case: a cause stated for one ruling does not by its wording alone license unrestricted transfer. Additional linguistic or contextual grounds may be required. Āmidī distinguishes application of an independently authorized universal from an analogy whose evidential structure depends on the original case. [L2]

No examined passage attributes these positions to Ibn Rushd. They are substantive comparisons within the later eastern tradition, not established Rushdian transmission.

### 8.3 The Shamsiyya retains demonstrative-science material

Al-Kātibī's Shamsiyya **§§ 116–120**, in Tony Street's 2024 edition, **pp. 78–85**, treats premise materials, demonstration, other argumentative arts, and the parts of sciences. Its last section explicitly concerns **subjects, principles, and questions**. [L3]

The inference for the research design is straightforward: a search restricted to titles containing “Posterior Analytics” will miss material preserved in the conclusions of general manuals and developed in their commentaries. A shorter or relocated treatment is not evidence of absence, although its contents and omissions must still be compared in detail.

### 8.4 Quṭb and Jurjānī discuss the incomplete reach and revision of logical rules

A directly read passage in Quṭb al-Dīn al-Rāzī's Taḥrīr al-qawāʿid, **pp. 258–260**, addresses propositions that do not fit the two classes under discussion. Quṭb explains that logicians studied classes commonly used in the sciences and extracted their rules; other rules are not yet known, and generalization extends as far as human capacity allows. [L4]

The Jurjānī note at **p. 258**, signed **Sharīf**, proposes a revised threefold account, illustrated through mathematics, natural philosophy, and logic. This is an actual attempt to repair the domain of the classification. It does not itself constitute a complete alternative calculus.

These passages answer the suspicion about the shurūḥ particularly well: commentators can explicitly debate the construction and coverage of the logical rules themselves. Their commentaries are a primary source for the history of methodology, not only a means of reading earlier authors.

### 8.5 Logic's own warrants and the regress question

The Maṭāliʿ/Lawāmiʿ discussion at **1885 pp. 15–22**, now read continuously in the physical scan, distinguishes immediate logical principles from theoretical rules established through evident inferential routes. At **17–18**, it explains why access to primitive rules does not make acquired intermediate knowledge cognitively dispensable. At **21–22**, it restricts logic's inquiry to the relevant general conditions of acquisition, avoiding the inclusion of every particular result in every science. The matn, commentary, and marginal material must be attributed separately. [L5]

Ṭūsī **I 119–120, 127–138, 321–333** and Quṭb/Jurjānī **1948 pp. 125–132** supply further explicit discussions of discovery, primitive warrant, universal proof, counterexamples, and qualifications. These are developed in section 18, including the distinction between logic's questions (*masāʾil*) and its technical subject (*mawḍūʿ*). [L4b; L6]

### 8.6 One actual line of Ibn Rushd reception

The disconnected-exception discussion in Zarkashī's Baḥr al-muḥīṭ explicitly reports Ibn al-Ḥājj's attribution of a solution to Abū al-Walīd Ibn Rushd. It matches the core of Ḍarūrī §§ 185–186. The online edition places it at **IV 375**; the Kuwait 1988 pagination reported in the comparison is **III 280** and should be checked in that edition before citation. [Z1; R1]

This is stronger evidence for reception than shared methodological vocabulary. It warrants a specific transmission claim about an intervention, not a general claim that Āmidī or the entire later logical tradition follows Ibn Rushd.

Ibn Ṭumlūs's separate **Kitāb al-Taḥlīl**, ed. Fouad Ben Ahmed, **pp. 227–248**, has now been acquired and read in full. The comparison identifies a refined definition of the instrument, proofs and restrictions of individual rules, and an explicitly unfinished dispute about whether a rule merely restates its target. His **Jadal 308** closely matches Ibn Rushd's **Middle Topics 12** in wording and sequence. This supplies a substantial Andalusian comparison; full source discrimination still requires the Farabian control. Section 16 records the evidence. [T1; R4]

## 9. Nomenclature: what “methodology” can mean here

| Historical term or location | Function supported by the examined evidence | What must not be assumed |
|---|---|---|
| Qawānīn / qawāʿid | Rules of an art or of particular operations. | The word alone names a single autonomous discipline. |
| Taḥlīl | In early Ibn Rushd, productive analysis of a problem; in Āmidī's adjacent chapter, reconstruction of given arguments. | Every occurrence has exactly the same operation as its referent. |
| Iktisāb al-muqaddimāt / taḥṣīl aqyisat al-maṭlūbāt | Acquiring premises and producing proofs; Āmidī's explicit chapter title. | The discussion belongs only to Burhān. |
| Burhān | Conditions of scientific knowledge, causal demonstration, principles, definitions, and related acquisition problems. | Valid form alone supplies the truth or scientific specificity of premises. |
| Uṣūl al-fiqh | General rules and evidentiary procedures governing legal derivation; its contents and boundaries remain disputed. | All its propositions are certain, or all are merely propositions of formal logic. |
| Aṣl / uṣūl, without a disciplinary qualifier | A principle, starting point, source, or basis, according to context. | Every occurrence denotes the autonomous discipline of legal theory or a universal methodology. |
| Masāʾil manṭiqiyya; qawānīn of logic | Ṭūsī explicitly places the exposition of universal transition-types among logic's questions. | Questions (*masāʾil*) and the technical subject (*mawḍūʿ*) of a science are interchangeable. |
| Limmiyya | In Ṭūsī's conversion discussion, explanatory understanding that discloses the precise consequence to be proved. | A newly independent discipline called “limmiyya,” or rejection of reductio. |
| Naẓar / baḥth / jadal | Inquiry, examination, and disputation in contexts whose precise operations vary. | All are exact synonyms of one universal “methodology.” |
| Ādāb al-baḥth | A later organized discipline of regulated inquiry and disputation, including scrutiny of causal indicators and objections. | All methodological investigation moved here, or its eastern history derives from Ibn Rushd. |
| Istinbāṭ / qiyās / istidlāl | Different ways of obtaining or extending a result; Ghazālī and Āmidī expressly distinguish some of them. | The modern translation “inference” captures all historically relevant distinctions. |
| Ajzāʾ al-ʿulūm, and discussions of subjects, principles, and questions | Constitution and organization of scientific knowledge in later logic. | A separate title “Posterior Analytics” is necessary for this material to survive. |

The best working description is **inquiry into the production, warrant, scope, and use of rules**, distributed across general logic and particular arts. This is an analytical description; it should not be projected back as a single native discipline-name.

## 10. Chronology and strength of historical claims

The Ḍarūrī is dated **1157**. The early logical project belongs to approximately the same early period; the present evidence does not establish whether this particular Qawānīn text preceded the legal abridgment. The **Middle Commentary on the Topics, 1168**, is later. It can illuminate development but cannot chronologically prefigure the 1157 work. [R1; R3–R4]

The conventional **1170 date for the Middle Burhān should not be treated as secure**. Lamprakis's 2025 chapter, note 14, traces it to Renan’s use of a catalogue entry subsequently identified as a Physics commentary; the manuscript is now lost. His proposed relative placement of the Middle Burhān before the Middle Topics is an inference to assess, not a dated colophon independently inspected here. The arguments in this dossier do not require that disputed absolute date or a demonstrated early-Qawānīn-before-Ḍarūrī sequence. [S11]

The Middle Topics' account of scientific utility, **ed. Sālim, pp. 10–14**, treats the examination of opposed arguments and distinguishes scientific benefit from mere exercise. It is a necessary control on any claim that Ibn Rushd simply denied dialectic a role in inquiry. Long editorial quotations from Fārābī in the notes must not be attributed to Ibn Rushd's main text. [R4]

### Textual problems identified

- **Ḍarūrī § 163:** the e-text has a missing negative in one contrasted position, and the edition marks a disturbed phrase. The final criticism of incomplete induction remains clear. Do not quote a silently repaired text.
- **Miʿyār p. 163:** the scan distinguishes complete induction yielding knowledge from incomplete induction yielding opinion; the electronic text is corrupted around this contrast.
- **Daqāʾiq II 247:** the printed “enduring universal opinion” was checked against Princeton239a–b, which reads “enduring universal certainty.” A claim about Āmidī's epistemology should not be built on that printed error. The clearer earlier passages and induction chapter independently constrain the interpretation.
- **Numbering:** Bou Akl's division of the Ḍarūrī differs from al-ʿAlawī's. Partial wording matches were made, but a complete concordance requires the 2015 edition.
- **Speaker attribution:** Quṭb's commentary, Jurjānī's signed marginal notes, later glosses, and modern editorial explanations must remain distinguishable.
- **Ibn Ṭumlūs 238:** the animal/human removal example appears reversed relative to Fārābī 104. It is excluded as an independently valid inference until manuscript collation resolves the reading.
- **Edition metadata:** the inspected Ṭūsī volume is Dunyā's third Cairo edition; some catalogue metadata contradict its imprint. Ibn Ṭumlūs's references to Butterworth's early Qawānīn pagination refer to a then-forthcoming edition, not a separately acquired published text.
- **Chronology:** the customary 1170 Middle Burhān date has a specific evidential challenge; see section 10. It is not used as a secure premise.

## 12. Research program from the present evidence to a publishable account

### Stage A: establish the core textual argument

**1. Prepare a critical passage set from the early Qawānīn.**

Transcribe Munich 31a–33a, 34b, 36a, 37b–38b, and 41a; collate them against the Latin and the second Judeo-Arabic witness, Paris BnF hébreu 1008, 32v–45v. Keep Arabic written in Hebrew characters distinct from a Hebrew translation. The object is the definition of locus, the naming of taḥlīl, one fully worked rule proof, and the conditions of demonstrative use. Consult the existing DARE transcription if it becomes available. Output: a short Arabic text with a literal translation and a genuine variant apparatus, clearly distinguished from the explanatory reconstructions in this dossier.

**2. Collate the Farabian predecessor at the same level of detail.**

Use Taḥlīl 95–107 against the corresponding Qawānīn passages. Record exact repeated words, changes in definitions, reordered examples, and omitted criticisms. Test the hypothesis that Ibn Rushd sharpens the definition of a locus rather than introducing formal analysis into an otherwise formless predecessor. Output: an aligned comparison supporting a precisely delimited influence claim.

**3. Construct a Ḍarūrī–Mustaṣfā case dossier.**

Use four principal cases: delayed clarification, apparent meaning, report authority, and preference. For every case record the proposed rule, its domain, evidence, objections, final qualification, and Ibn Rushd's actual change to Ghazālī. Obtain Bou Akl 2015 to establish text and numbering. Output: four argument maps and a verified edition concordance.

**4. Test the account against Ghazālī's own second-order procedures.**

The principal readings are complete. Collate Miʿyār 174–175, Shifāʾ 190–195 and 382–395, and Asās 11–12, 20–24, 104–107 against Qisṭās 16–27, 75–79 and Miḥakk's fourth definition law. Distinguish authority, probability, exhaustiveness, instrument warrant, and the purpose of debate. Obtain a stable Miḥakk print concordance and test specific shared formulations against the Ḍarūrī. Output: a controlled account of antecedents that identifies both continuity and the points of disagreement.

### Stage B: clarify the scientific-method context

**5. Collate the now-read early and middle Burhān against the instrumental legal program.**

The entire early Latin Burhān and Middle Burhān 161–183 have been read. Begin the matched collation with early 60rB–C and 61vM, Middle §§ 136–140 and 152–156, and Ḍarūrī §§ 2–6. Examine the instrumental classification, reception of principles, definition discovery, and practical universals. Extend manuscript verification only where a substantive reading requires it. If adding the long commentary, respect its Arabic survival limits and use the appropriate Hebrew/Latin witnesses for gaps. Output: an account of the productive inquiry's demonstrative and disciplinary presuppositions, with no reliance on the disputed 1170 date.

**6. Compare development in the Middle Topics.**

The scientific-preparation account at pp. 12–14 is read and matched with Ibn Ṭumlūs 308–309. The complete locus-definition debate, Sālim 73–78, is now also read in Arabic and two Latin translations: the requirement that loci supply premises and form is expressly retained. Extend the comparison to matching topical families in the early Qawānīn and Middle Topics. Ask how their precise rules, exceptions, and standards of warrant change. Use exact printed pages, verifying any paragraph-number concordance separately. Output: a developmental argument with explicit limits on the relative chronology of the early legal and logical works.

**7. Complete the Avicennan bridge with textual controls.**

Obtain the Jadal V.1 scan and establish Najāt pagination. Align Najāt with Miʿyār; compare Burhān IV.6–7 andIV.10 with the relevant Topics passages. For experience, compare Burhān I.9 with Āmidī and the Shamsiyya commentators. Output: a differentiated account of definition production, principle acquisition, and rules governing the scope of a universal.

**8. Establish the earlier legal counterpart in Māzarī.**

Obtain the critical print of Īḍāḥ al-maḥṣūl and verify the now-located page marker158 for the language chapter. Locate the original passage about probable uṣūl propositions reported by Shāṭibī. Compare the functional definition and organization with the Ḍarūrī. Output: an earlier legal control independent of the philosophical corpus.

### Stage C: trace later forms without presupposing a single lineage

**9. Complete a three-way collation of the now-read Ibn Ṭumlūs Taḥlīl.**

The entire chapter and the specified surrounding Qiyās, Burhān, and Jadal units are now read. Compare Fārābī 95–107, Ibn Ṭumlūs 228–239, and the corresponding early Rushdian loci. Prioritize the self-refinement at 233–234, the restatement objection at 238, and the animal/human textual problem. The Middle Topics–Jadal parallel is already matched; determine the contribution of common Farabian sources. Output: an Andalusian transmission study with graded dependencies and a small critical apparatus.

**10. Extend Āmidī only where the present evidence leaves a specific question.**

Locate the next Daqāʾiq manuscript volume, if extant and accessible, or parallel discussions in another securely attributed work. Prioritize the announced chapters on first principles and relations among sciences. Compare the examined legal authorization arguments against his explicit logical standards. Output: an explanation of how far certainty is claimed for rule warrants and practical obligations, without silently resolving tensions the text leaves open.

**11. Follow the eastern commentary chain through controlled passages.**

Substantial Ṭūsī, Lawāmiʿ, and Quṭb/Jurjānī units are now read. The remaining collation should concentrate on two movements of thought (Ṭūsī 119–120, 127–138), discovery and proof of conversion (321–333; Quṭb/Jurjānī 1948 pp. 125–132), and the constitution of sciences. Obtain the Takmila of the Jalāliyya gloss and ʿAbd al-Ḥakīm's gloss on Quṭb at logical ʿaks, since Tahānawī names these exact sources. Add Rāzī or Khūnajī only to resolve the antecedent of a specific argument or rule. Output: a passage-based history of discovery, warrant, and revision.

**12. Trace legal reception through named citations.**

Start with the established Ibn al-Ḥājj–Zarkashī exception case. Search the immediate neighboring discussions and cited works for further Rushdian interventions, always matching the argument to a primary Rushd text. Output: a register of explicit reception, with conjectural parallels kept separate.

**13. Collate the identified juristic-dialectic and ādāb rule-warrant debate.**

Young 205–282 and the primary material he presents have been read completely; Rāzī's Maḥṣūl chapter has been independently checked. Verify Nasafī's fourteen lemmata, Samarqandī's universal-principle objection (Esad 3034, 18a / BL Or. 11183, 17b), and Kīlānī's repetition argument (Damad Ibrahim 1043, 14b–15b) against the actual witnesses. Compare these with Ghazālī's debate procedures and distinguish tanbīh from universal entailment proof. Separately, read Shāṭibī's relevant inductive demonstrations against Āmidī's probable warrants. No Rushdian transmission is presupposed.

**14. Pursue the named Ibn Bājja dispute and one exact later Rushd commentary.**

The early Burhān 55vK–M / Munich 48b supplies a newly verified Ibn Bājja attribution. Locate its independent counterpart in Ibn Bājja's logical writings and keep it distinct from the potential/actual-locus controversy in the scholarship. For actual later Rushd commentary, investigate Moses Narboni, BodleianMich. 355, 165r–170r, reported as commenting on the opening of this precise Taḥlīl. The Themistian tradition should be added only where Hasnawi's comparison explains a concrete shared feature. Output: a bounded predecessor-and-reception apparatus rather than an unrestricted word history.

### Common evidence form for all stages

Each passage should be entered with: author/work; witness and date; edition/page/folio; exact Arabic term; object being sought; proposed procedure; evidence for the procedure; objections and final reply; claimed certainty/probability; attribution to predecessors; and later citation. A dependency claim should state whether it rests on an explicit name, close wording, a distinctive shared example, sequence, or only conceptual resemblance.

The proposed central thesis to test is:

> Ibn Rushd develops a productive and self-examining conception of logical and legal arts. Its procedures for discovering results and warranting rules draw upon different kinds of evidence, and later treatments distribute these problems across general logic, demonstration theory, legal theory, and regulated inquiry.

The thesis must be narrowed if the critical witnesses or matched legal passages show that a claimed innovation is already present in the predecessor; it must be divided into separate lineages if later texts lack evidence of transmission from Ibn Rushd.

## 15. The central research question

The most productive question is now more precise than “where did Ibn Rushd get methodology?” It is:

**How does a thinker turn a capacity for successful inquiry into an articulated art, and how are the resulting rules defined, warranted, restricted, and corrected?**

The present evidence supports studying that question through the Farabian and Rushdian Taḥlīl tradition, the actual proofs and revisions of uṣūl rules in Ghazālī and the Ḍarūrī, and later discussions of proof acquisition and the constitution of sciences. A full genealogy will require the bounded critical and reception work specified above; the existence of those concrete procedures is already established by the passages inspected.

## 16. New primary evidence: the coordinated Rushdian and Andalusian account

### 16.1 The early Burhān contains an instrumental classification and actual rule criticism

The complete early **Burhān**, in the 1562 Latin translation, **fols. 52r–61v**, has now been read continuously, with all twenty pages visually checked and selected Judeo-Arabic manuscript controls. Its relevance is not confined to a generic account of certainty. It supplies both a classification of methodological arts and actual disputes about the adequacy of demonstrative rules. [R5]

At **60rB–C**, Ibn Rushd distinguishes arts directed to operation, arts directed to knowledge, and an art directing the intellect's inquiry into the preceding two: **logic, or an art analogous to it**. The comparison with the Ḍarūrī's opening is now supported by a passage in his own early logical corpus. There is a general category for an instrument that guides inquiry into another art. The passage neither calls every such instrument *uṣūl* nor proves that jurisprudential uṣūl is formally a curricular subsection of logic.

Its last paragraph, **61vM**, supplies an essential qualification. A practitioner, **insofar as practicing the particular art**, cannot establish that art's non-evident first principle except incidentally. Principles can be received from another science or art. This is a restriction on what the discipline proves from within its own resources; it is not a prohibition on the same person examining a principle through a different inquiry. It also means that a methodology of inquiry must include relations between disciplines and the reception of principles, not merely an inventory of valid forms.

There are direct examples of evaluating the rules themselves. At **55vK–M**, Ibn Rushd discusses a class of demonstrations apparently absent from Fārābī's account. **Ibn Bājja is expressly named** and reported as defending the omission. Ibn Rushd distinguishes different orders of definition and differentia to show why the objection does not exclude all cases of the class. The issue is whether the proposed arrangement satisfies the conditions of demonstration and whether a classification has omitted a legitimate case. Munich **48b** independently controls the named exchange.

At **56rE–F**, he examines a second apparently omitted form, constructs an appropriate example, and tests it against Fārābī's own conditions. He finds it admissible, but offers copying loss or deliberate brevity as possible explanations of the omission. The finding is a reasoned test of the transmitted classification, not proof that Fārābī's original work contained the omission or that Ibn Rushd invented the form.

This is a concrete answer to “how does he figure out the qawānīn?” One procedure is to identify what a successful demonstration must accomplish, distinguish relations that an objection conflates, and test whether a proposed form satisfies those requirements. The warrant of the classification becomes an object of argument. This Ibn Bājja dispute is separate from the actual-versus-potential-syllogism controversy reported in the scholarship on loci; their attributions must remain distinct.

### 16.2 The Middle Burhān explicitly sends definition discovery to the Topics

The Arabic **1982 Qāsim–Butterworth–al-Harīdī edition** has now been acquired. Its final **pp. 161–183, §§ 130–157**, were read continuously and compared with the corresponding Latin. The decisive passage is **§ 136, p. 167**. It begins **qultu**, distinguishing Ibn Rushd's intervention from the paraphrased Aristotelian lemma:

> فإنه لابد في استنباط الحدود من المواضع المذكورة في كتاب طوبيقى

Working translation: **Deriving definitions necessarily requires the loci mentioned in the Topics.** [R6]

The context makes the point stronger than an isolated reference. Ibn Rushd rejects the inference that composition of essential predicates is sufficient by itself to discover definitions. One needs the loci of establishment/refutation, genus/differentia, and related relations. Their presentation as reputable in the Topics permits selection of the demonstrative counterparts. Dialectical acceptability does not automatically confer scientific certainty; the relations and materials must be selected and employed under the relevant demonstrative conditions.

At **§ 138, pp. 169–171**, syllogistic argument can help determine whether a proposed predicate belongs essentially, as genus or difference, or accidentally. The Topics is again named. At **§ 140, pp. 172–173**, an investigator compares individuals called by one name before assuming they have one nature and one definition. If the magnanimity examples do not yield a common nature, the name may be shared across different meanings. Language's grouping is something to examine, not an automatic guarantee of an essence.

The final acquisition discussion, **§§ 152–156, pp. 180–183**, distinguishes the sensory and memorial route to universals from their status as principles known by intellect. **§ 154, p. 181**, expressly includes universals acquired from **voluntary matters**, which supply practical principles, alongside universals supplying theoretical sciences. This provides a textual basis for comparing principle acquisition across practical and theoretical contexts. It does not settle the warrant of every particular legal universal.

The early **53r** also distinguishes the extent of experience required according to the diversity of subject matters; it does not offer one sample size for all sciences. Both early **57r–v** and middle **§ 133, p. 163**, discuss matters holding for the most part. These passages prevent excluding all non-invariable subject matter from the discussion. They do not identify every kind of legal probability with frequency, or make every probable inference a demonstration.

### 16.3 The Middle Topics supplies a matched account of scientific preparation

The directly reread Arabic **Middle Topics, Sālim p. 12**, says that theoretical arts contain **premises, questions, and syllogisms**. Collecting and delimiting them first as reputable items is easier. Once available, the investigator tests whether they really are as posited through **al-qawānīn al-burhāniyya**, demonstrative rules. The next **pp. 12–14** distinguish contributions involving induction, the beginner's understanding, provisional acceptance of principles proved in another discipline, and refutation of denials of scientific principles. [R4]

This is an explicit account of a sequence of inquiry: prepare and articulate a field's candidate materials, investigate their difficulties, then evaluate them under the conditions of demonstration. It does not describe merely a technique for defeating an opponent. It also supplies the immediate comparison for the newly read Ibn Ṭumlūs passage below.

### 16.4 Ibn Ṭumlūs: the entire Taḥlīl is now read

The access gap in the first version of this dossier is closed. The complete Ben Ahmed edition has been obtained, and **all twenty-two pages of the Taḥlīl, top pagination 227–248**, were read and visually checked. Its bottom Arabic pagination is **147–168**; a reference beginning at 148 omits the opening page. [T1]

The chapter begins from the difference between knowing what syllogisms are and knowing how to **produce** them. Premises are the material, arrangement the form. A builder obtains suitable materials and arranges them into a house. In reasoning, the desired conclusion supplies the two extremes; inquiry must obtain an appropriate middle.

The initial definition of a locus as a principle for finding a middle is then reconsidered. At **233–234**, Ibn Ṭumlūs distinguishes finding a middle and only afterward recognizing the resulting figure from constructing the argument with its determinate form already in view. Proper productive knowledge makes the figure and mood guide the operation. The full locus is a **potential syllogism**, not merely a topic that happens to suggest useful material.

His treatment of Fārābī is discriminating. At **228–232**, some general statements contain the whole arrangement of a syllogism and therefore have complete particular syllogisms as their instances. Other loci really do provide only the general connection used as a major premise. He criticizes extending the major-premise definition to all loci while preserving its application to the second class. This is a refinement of the instrument's definition, not an indiscriminate rejection of Fārābī.

He also warrants and restricts individual rules:

| Passage | What is examined | Methodological contribution |
|---|---|---|
| 234–235 | An inference through a definition initially yields two affirmative premises in the second figure. | Coextension of a genuine definition licenses conversion into a valid first-figure arrangement. Ordinary one-way predication would not suffice. |
| 235–237 | Division and conditional inference. | Exhaustiveness and the direction of entailment must be established; induction is distinguished from demonstration. |
| 237–239 | Generalizing from a concomitant and identifying a cause. | The association may be accidental, narrower than supposed, or due to a common cause. |
| 239–242 | Correspondence of opposites and practical conduct. | Some rules depend on accepted ethical commitments; examining examples can expose overgeneralization. |
| 243–247 | Analogy, greater/less, addition, and subtraction. | A relevant similarity or qualification cannot be silently removed; particular counterexamples restrict proposed rules. |

Especially important is **p. 238**, where he explicitly engages Fārābī's objection that a purported rule merely restates the universal conclusion sought. Ibn Ṭumlūs considers whether the potential-syllogism account saves its value, but calls for further reflection. The question is not fully resolved in the passage. A historian should preserve that hesitation: it is evidence that the independent value of a methodological rule was debatable, not evidence of a completed solution.

The ending, **247–248**, differentiates generic form from the materials proper to demonstrative, dialectical, and rhetorical use. It bounds the generic relations without pretending that the introductory inventory exhausts everything. Particular analogy/transfer loci retain dialectical or rhetorical restrictions.

### 16.5 Legal cause finding and the remaining logical books

The adjacent **Qiyās 213–220**, also read, supplies a direct juridical comparison. At **217–219**, the task is to identify which universal a legal utterance intends. Methods include ordinary linguistic understanding, explicit indication, **sabr wa-taqsīm**, **munāsaba**, and comparison of competing suitable grounds. Before employing them, one must know that the ruling was not confined to the original particular. Once identified, the universal becomes the middle of the inference to another case.

At **218**, the ribā example weighs food, staple nourishment, and measure/weight as competing grounds. The text prefers the Hanafi measurement rationale because it fits equality in exchange. This is a substantive judgment about appropriateness, not a result selected by formal validity alone. The apparatus identifies exact Mustaṣfā, Ḍarūrī, and Bidāya parallels; their individual contributions require further collation.

Targeted readings of **Burhān 249–254, 256–260, 275–282** and **Jadal 305–311** establish the coordination of the curriculum. Experience occasions acquisition of a universal but the observed items remain particulars, requiring an additional act of the soul. Principles cannot all be demonstrated internally without regress; some belong to a higher science. Definition procedures have prerequisites that division alone cannot supply. At **Burhān 281**, the author expressly adds the Book of Dialectic to complete the account of discovering definitions.

At **Jadal 308–309**, premises, questions, and syllogisms are assembled as reputable and tested by demonstrative qawānīn. The wording and sequence closely match the independently read **Ibn Rushd, Middle Topics 12–14**. This is now a matched primary-text parallel, beyond an editor's suggested reference. A complete source analysis must still compare the underlying Farabian tradition before assigning every common sentence exclusively to Rushd.

Two textual controls remain important. The printed animal/human removal example at **Taḥlīl 238** appears reversed relative to Fārābī 104 and needs manuscript collation. Also, the edition's references to pages in Butterworth's early **Qawānīn** edition concern a work described in its introduction as **forthcoming**; those numbers must not be presented as a separately acquired published edition.

### 16.6 The Middle Topics now gives a direct account of the nature of a qānūn

A final acquisition closed the remaining gap at the central definition: the actual **Sālim 1980 Arabic scan**, not a catalogue substitute, has now been acquired. **Printed 73–80** were read continuously, including the complete locus-definition debate through 78 and its transition to the organization of the Topics. The exact same debate was also read in the two parallel Latin translations, printed 27r–29r. The Arabic title page is verified; physical PDF = printed page + 9. [R4]

The opening of the second part, **p. 73**, asks what a locus is, whether loci are finite and enumerable, and which organization best teaches them. It reports Alexander and Theophrastus as treating a locus as a principle from which premises for particular arguments are obtained. It then brings Fārābī into the comparison. These are authorial attributions in the main text, distinct from the extensive modern footnotes.

At **p. 75**, Ibn Rushd argues that a syllogism has both matter, its premises, and form, its figure. A locus that serves its production must therefore supply the premises and the inferential form. He expressly calls this account correct. The paragraph adds a useful qualification: loci may supply both operations together, or different loci may supply one or the other. This closely preserves the early Qawānīn's twofold functional requirement; it does not establish that every individual locus must separately state an entire completed syllogism.

The following debate is about how generality serves an art. Themistius's competing account permits a locus to function as an actual premise in a particular argument. At **p. 76**, Ibn Rushd presents Alexander's reason for requiring the more general account: particular premises are unlimited, whereas a finite stock of general principles can contain indefinitely many specific premises potentially. Knowing the general principles makes the corresponding particular constructions available. He explicitly connects this with **the nature of a rule as a rule** and judges Alexander's position closer to the truth.

This is an unusually direct passage for the user's question. The argument moves from the operation an art must perform to the form its knowledge must take: a teachable rule directs indefinitely many constructions through something general and graspable. It therefore explains why generality and potential application belong to the instrument. It does **not** establish the truth of every particular rule simply by saying that it has this function. Those warrants require the further proofs, conditions, and counterexamples documented elsewhere in this dossier.

At **p. 77**, the defended account also relates the technical name *mawḍiʿ* to question-and-answer procedure. Universal principles used to obtain premises in demonstrations are said not to receive that Aristotelian name, since demonstration is not arranged for question and answer. This statement occurs within the account supporting the Alexander/Fārābī position; the pronouns should not be flattened into a securely identified verbatim quotation from one predecessor. Its relevance is terminological and contextual: related productive functions need not always carry the same name in every argumentative art. It must be read together with Middle Burhān167, where appropriate topical resources are expressly required for definition discovery.

Finally, **p. 78** separates loci proper from other preparatory directions, including clarifying how a term is used or changing its wording to expose its meaning. Such directions help one find premises, but the commentators are reported as agreeing that they are preparations rather than loci. Consequently, “method of investigation,” “locus,” and “rule” should not be treated as three perfectly coextensive expressions. The larger art can contain preparatory operations, general productive instruments, and tests of the products obtained.

The pages immediately following discuss possible organizations of the material: by sought predication, by relations shared across types of question, or toward the central task of definition. Only the beginning of this organizational discussion has been read here. A claim about the whole ensuing inventory still requires the further matched topical-family study described in section 12.

## 17. Ghazālī: the balance's warrant, abstraction, and limits

### 17.1 Qisṭās now supplies a direct answer about establishing the instrument

The Bījū **1993** scan has now been read visually at **11–27, 38–47, 72–81**. These spans include the sustained arguments on the instrument’s warrant, its generalized use, the naming of balances, and the limits of proposed proofs. Other balances have not all been read completely. [G6]

At **16–18**, the dialogue asks how one knows that a balance is accurate. A physical balance can be checked through its observed equilibrium, experiential knowledge of weight and descent, and a necessary consequence. Once its correctness and use are known, the user need not know its original inventor or consult him at every weighing. Ghazālī transfers the issue to the intellectual balances. His theological account of divine and prophetic teaching remains part of the work, but it does not eliminate the demand to recognize why the instrument works.

At **21–24**, a Qurʾānic argument is expanded into the premises left implicit by its concise expression. The interlocutor asks how the balance demonstrated in one instance can guide other questions. Ghazālī abstracts the relation that makes the conclusion follow from the particular example. Sense, experience, and innate intellect supply familiar standards through which that relation is recognized. At **25**, he also distinguishes a familiar derived conclusion from an immediately evident cognition: fluency can conceal the premises on which one depends.

At **41**, he distinguishes three historical claims which should not be collapsed: he has invented the **names** he is using; he presents an **extraction from the Qurʾān**; and the underlying balances were already known to earlier people under other names. His prophetic genealogy is his own account, not independent historical proof about the origin of Greek logic. At **42–43**, his changed terminology also serves a rhetorical and pedagogical purpose.

The passages are especially valuable beside the Ḍarūrī's measuring-instrument analogy. They show that the analogy can introduce a question about the instrument's **warrant**, as well as about its use. A direct textual dependence between these particular passages and Ibn Rushd's opening is still to be demonstrated.

### 17.2 The final chapter tests arguments instead of equating conviction with proof

At **38**, purity and valid prayer supply a counterexample distinguishing valid conditional inferences from affirming the consequent or denying the antecedent. At **39–40**, division must be exhaustive. At **44–45**, defects can concern premises, the shared middle, or the arrangement.

The final chapter, **75–79**, is more searching. Rendering an analogy as a syllogism exposes its universal major as a further claim requiring evidence. Surveying many observed makers does not establish an unrestricted claim about every possible maker; treating the disputed case as already surveyed assumes the question. The text distinguishes the correctness of a conclusion from the validity of an argument offered for it: at **77**, Ghazālī accepts the world's origination while rejecting the proposed route to that result.

The same discussion explains why eliminating rival causal descriptions can fail. The list may omit an alternative; the cause may be specific to the original case; several features may operate jointly; or the surviving description may be true only with an additional qualification. “I do not know another possibility” is not identical to “there is no other possibility.” At **79**, he explicitly differentiates uses suited to probable legal reasoning or persuasion from what supplies certainty.

These are methodological arguments in a logical work, despite its distinctive dialogical and scriptural presentation. They belong alongside the previously read Miʿyār and Shifāʾ passages, not outside the corpus because the title is different.

### 17.3 Miḥakk: a named procedure for obtaining a definition

The newly read Arabic e-text includes the formal-rule conditions, the certainty discussion, and the definition laws. The especially useful heading is **al-qānūn al-rābiʿ fī ṭarīq iqtināṣ al-ḥadd**, the fourth law concerning the way of capturing a definition. [G5]

In disputation, Ghazālī tells the investigator to obtain the rival definition, isolate the additional condition, and find an instance the opponent accepts without that condition. The example concerns usurpation. A second usurper takes from the first: there is usurpation without a new removal of the rightful owner's hand, since that removal has already occurred. The counterexample defeats the proposed extra requirement. It does **not** by itself establish every remaining constituent of a complete definition.

The denial that an ordinary syllogism straightforwardly demonstrates a sought definition should therefore not be paraphrased as an exclusion of reasoning from definition discovery. The procedure itself employs comparison, argument, and counterexample. The following law diagnoses substitutions of material, part, capacity, or an equally obscure correlate for an appropriate defining constituent.

Miḥakk also expressly distinguishes genuine certainty, firm belief, and probability, and differentiates the premises required for rational demonstration from probable premises in legal reasoning. This supports a narrow but important correction to inflated claims about Ghazālī: a valid form alone does not make its substantive content certain.

### 17.4 How to use the modern certainty debate

Rudolph's and Opwis's chapters in Adamson 2019 have both been read in full. Opwis makes a strong interpretation of the role of syllogistic logic in securing certainty for legal analogy. That claim needs to be broken into three questions: certainty of a report or substantive premise; certainty of the resulting legal judgment; and certainty that one should act on a qualified probability. [S13–S14]

The direct logical passages rule out treating those as automatically identical. They do not rule out every account on which cumulative evidence produces certainty, and they do not by themselves refute Opwis's entire interpretation. A controlled discussion should juxtapose her reconstruction with Miḥakk's distinctions, Miʿyār 256–257, the relevant tawātur passages, and the separate arguments for legal authorization.

## 18. Later logic commentaries establish and revise the rules themselves

### 18.1 Ṭūsī's express disciplinary answer: qawānīn and logical masāʾil

The actual Dunyā **third-edition Cairo scan** of Ṭūsī's *Sharḥ al-Ishārāt* has now been read at **I 118–139 and 320–337**, with a sampled continuation at 340. Its printed pages agree with the relevant references in the 1971 second edition, but the downloaded book must not be mislabeled as that edition. [L6]

At **I 119–120**, Ṭūsī explains thought's two movements: from a sought result through available meanings to suitable principles, and from those principles back to the result. Logic concerns both. He explicitly refers to Aristotle's **chapter on acquiring premises in the Book of Syllogism**, *bāb iktisāb al-muqaddimāt min Kitāb al-Qiyās*. The precise ancestral passage must be collated, but the reference already rules out locating this entire concern exclusively in the Posterior Analytics.

At **I 127–128**, commenting on the first nahj § 9, universal kinds of transition are called **qawānīn**, and their exposition constitutes **logical questions, masāʾil manṭiqiyya**. At **I 138**, he again assigns logic both the search for suitable starting points and their arrangement. This is an explicit later answer to the nomenclature question: the general rules and their examination belong among logic's own questions. That does not identify the *masāʾil* with the technical subject, *mawḍūʿ*, of the discipline.

That placement does not put every substantive conclusion inside logic. Quṭb's *Lawāmiʿ*, **1885 pp. 21–22**, criticizes an unrestricted definition of logic's subject that would include the individual definitions and judgments of all sciences. General conditions of acquisition are not identical with all the acquired content. [L5]

### 18.2 Ṭūsī distinguishes discovering the exact rule from proving a candidate

The strongest passage is **I 332–333**, fifth nahj, third chapter, commentary on § 8. The subject is the conversion of qualified propositions. Ṭūsī observes that reductio begins with the contradictory of an already specified target. Consequently, that procedure alone does not tell one **which precise modal consequence should have been proposed**. A proof can establish a weaker consequence without discovering the strongest result the original supports. [L6]

He develops an explanatory account of the predication and qualification involved, then says that the insight is supplied by **limmiyya**, the explanatory why; it was not initially noticed by those relying on reductio. Once brought to attention, however, it may be established by reductio. This is an explicit distinction between finding the exact rule and confirming it afterward. His word here is *limmiyya*, not *taḥlīl*, and he is not rejecting reductio as a proof method.

The neighboring pages enact the same standards. At **I 321–322**, he distinguishes a stipulated feature of conversion from the necessary preservation of truth and questions an additional condition found in some copies. At **I 325–328**, he tests inherited arguments against temporal qualifications and constructs a restricted result where an unqualified one fails. This is a methodological investigation within an ordinary technical chapter on logical conversion.

### 18.3 Why logic's own proof need not lead to regress

At **I 323–324**, Ṭūsī separates the order of exposition from the order of warrant. Reductio can be an immediately evident inference even when its general type is taught later. A later position in the book does not mean the operation initially had no justification.

He also rejects interpreting ecthesis as a disguised third-figure syllogism. The operation takes one individual already bearing the relevant descriptions. Naming the same individual differently does not create an independent middle term. This is an actual account of the primitive operation, not merely a declaration that some propositions are axioms.

Quṭb and Jurjānī's **Bīdār 64–67** distinguish possession of an evident rule, learning it, and codifying it. Jurjānī argues that conceiving premises and a conclusion in specified primitive inferential forms suffices for immediate assent to their entailment. This does not require that the material premises already be known true. Codification can nevertheless remove obscurity through **tanbīh** and make the rule available for acquired results. Quṭb's **Lawāmiʿ 17–18** then answers the stronger objection that primitive rules should render every intermediate theoretical rule dispensable: the ability to acquire an intermediate body of knowledge does not mean one already possesses what that knowledge supplies. [L4–L5]

These arguments should be compared with the external critic Ibn Taymiyya, who explicitly discusses Ghazālī's *Qisṭās* and challenges a measure that itself requires a further measure. His *Radd* also argues that general truths cannot all await further general syllogistic proof. That is a direct reception of Ghazālī, not an established reception of Ibn Rushd. The critique contests the exclusivity and necessity of the scholastic instrument; it should not be equated with rejecting every valid inference. [D3]

### 18.4 Jurjānī and Quṭb give a two-part procedure for establishing a conversion rule

The **1948 Ḥalabī edition** has been checked physically. Its title identifies the lower gloss as Jurjānī's. The complete **pp. 125–132** were read, keeping Kātibī's matn, Quṭb's commentary, and the lower gloss separate. [L4b]

At **125–126**, Jurjānī treats the converse as the strongest uniformly entailed proposition under the specified term exchange and preservation of affirmative or negative quality. He requires two arguments:

1. Establish that the proposed consequence follows **in every relevant material instance**.
2. Show why a stronger proposed consequence is not universally necessary, by an instance in which that stronger claim fails.

Quṭb's main commentary at **127** independently explains the asymmetry. One instance in which the reversed proposition happens to be true does not prove a universal conversion rule; a proof must apply across all materials. One counterinstance is enough to refute the proposed universal entailment. This is a direct statement of a method for warranting a logical qānūn.

At **130**, Quṭb notices that simply transferring an argument from the universal-affirmative case to a particular case would yield two particular premises and fail. He supplies another route. At **131**, Kātibī's matn states that the conversion of two possibility propositions remains unsettled. Quṭb diagnoses the failed proofs, including their dependence on unestablished rules, and at **132** shows how interpreting the subject as actual or possible changes the result. The matn's suspension and the commentary's further intervention must remain distinct. A textbook's rule system is explicitly open to correction, dependence checks, and suspension of judgment.

Tahānawī's **Kashshāf II1203–1205**, under logical **ʿaks**, preserves the same two obligations in connected prose: proof or prompting for entailment in all materials, and counterinstances against stronger candidates. He expressly names the **Takmila of the Jalāliyya gloss** and **ʿAbd al-Ḥakīm's gloss on Quṭb** as sources of his summary. These are exact later acquisition targets. His attribution does not let us assign every sentence individually to either named source without collation. [L7]

## 19. A different but connected later home: juristic dialectic and ādāb al-baḥth

### 19.1 Distinguish the two Āmidīs and the two histories

|---|---|
| **Sayf al-Dīn al-Āmidī**, d.631/1233 | *Daqāʾiq al-ḥaqāʾiq* and *al-Iḥkām*: proof discovery, demonstrative premises, and the warrant of legal evidence examined earlier in this dossier. |
| **Rukn al-Dīn al-ʿAmīdī**, d.615/1218 | *Irshād*: a major source in the development of streamlined juristic dialectic leading to Nasafī and Samarqandī. |

Walter Edward Young's **“Concomitance to Causation,” pp. 205–282**, has now been read completely. It supplies edited primary lemmata, translations, and detailed comparisons of manuscripts and later glosses. The chapter's presented primary material has been read; the manuscripts it cites have not all been independently inspected in this project. [S12]

This source establishes a historical line through **Burhān al-Dīn al-Nasafī's Fuṣūl**, his self-commentary, and his student **Shams al-Dīn al-Samarqandī's Sharḥ Fuṣūl, ʿAyn al-naẓar, and Risāla fī ādāb al-baḥth**. The developing discipline is named *ādāb al-baḥth*. “Proto-ādāb al-baḥth” is Young's modern descriptive label. This is a documented later inquiry tradition; no textual dependence on Ibn Rushd has been established for it.

### 19.2 The literal question: how can examples establish a universal principle?

The central debate concerns **dawarān**, the repeated concomitance of a ruling or putative effect with a candidate cause. Does it provide grounds for attributing the ruling or occurrence to that cause? What sort of grounds? Which conditions and exceptions matter?

The passage nearest the user's wording is **Young 239–240**, presenting Samarqandī's *Sharḥ Fuṣūl*, **Esad Efendi 3034, fol. 18a / BL Or. 11183, fol. 17b**. It asks how a **qāʿida kulliyya**, a universal principle, can be confirmed through **amthila**, examples.

The offered answer treats the examples as **tanbīh**. They prompt a sound understanding to recognize the general evidential operation rather than supply an enumeration of every case. The discussion invokes the philosophers' classification of experiential cognitions among necessary cognitions. The surrounding examples include medicines, food, and repeated anger at an offensive form of address.

Two qualifications are decisive. First, recognizing a general rule about the production of belief or probability is different from knowing the inferred **cause itself with certainty**. Second, Samarqandī immediately poses a difficulty about missing conditions and impediments. Scammony's historical purgative example does not hold under every climatic and bodily condition. Likewise, a legal effect associated with violation of the fast might require an additional condition such as sexual intercourse. The passage does not supply a completed answer to that objection.

This is a direct discussion of the warrant of a methodological principle. It is also an example of the standard under dispute: one writer presents an operation as evident through prompting, while the same debate exposes qualifications that may block its unrestricted application. It should not be merged with Quṭb's proof of logical entailment as if both authors were giving one uniform method for one kind of rule.

### 19.3 What the extended debate adds

The surrounding commentaries repeatedly separate the use of a rule from establishing it. The most informative criticisms are these:

- **Shared agreement on a case does not establish a shared universal explanation.** A known expiation for one act can be explained by competing causes. If the proposed generalization is supported by already controversial branch cases, it risks assuming the disputed rule. Young 237, 244–249.
- **Multiplying correlated properties is not automatically multiplying independent evidence.** More candidate *madārāt* are not necessarily more *adilla*. Young 248–249.
- **A rival cause must be excluded by evidence.** The critic's donor example involves a recipient who is poor, learned, and a relative. Repeated gifts do not identify which feature motivates the donor. Further information, such as asking the donor, can discriminate. Young 255–257.
- **The target may be evidential rather than an unconditional causal production.** A connection can produce dominant probability or knowledge of a ruling without establishing every aspect of an efficient cause. Young 252–254.

The critic called **al-Muntaqid** is associated with the work printed as *Tanbīh al-rajul al-ʿāqil*. Its attribution to Ibn Taymiyya is disputed; this dossier follows Young's neutral designation for that text. It keeps the separately read *Radd ʿalā al-manṭiqiyyīn* evidence distinct.

### 19.4 Rāzī provides an independently checked precursor

The entire Arabic **Maḥṣūl** chapter headed **al-faṣl al-sādis fī al-dawarān** was independently read in the public e-text. Young's print concordance is **V 207–216**; the e-text itself does not provide a scan-verified page map. [D2]

Rāzī supports dawarān's production of probability, gives arguments including ordinary responses to name-calling and equal treatment, then considers many counterexamples. The final reply qualifies the evidential claim by the absence of defeating evidence. Presence and absence in combination may supply a force not available from either in isolation.

This direct check matters because a summary that gives only the opening endorsement would miss the objections and final restriction. It is the exact procedure's conditional warrant, not the bare existence of the term *dawarān*, that belongs in the history of methodology.

### 19.5 The later shurūḥ and ḥawāshī are concrete research objects

Young **272–275** follows the definition into commentaries by **Quṭb al-Dīn al-Kīlānī** and **Masʿūd al-Shirwānī al-Rūmī**. Kīlānī restores the phrase “time after time” and defends repetition's epistemic role. The glosses distinguish universal and particular connections, necessary implication and dawarān, and propositions from their component terms. These are changes to how an investigative operation is defined and justified.

The principal manuscript targets are Kīlānī, **Damad Ibrahim 1043, 14b–15b / Bodleian Laud Or. 192, 31b–32b**, and Masʿūd's **2010 edition 157–158**. For Samarqandī's extended treatment, consult *ʿAyn al-naẓar* **§§ 43–56**, BL Or. 3730, 75a–76a / Or. 3908, 4b–5a.

There are also precise connections to general logical manuals. Young 264–267 discusses the Shamsiyya treatment of analogy, Taftāzānī's commentary, Yazdī's *Sharḥ Tahdhīb*, and a later gloss quoting Masʿūd. Yazdī refers methods of causal validation to the books of **uṣūl al-fiqh**. This is evidence for interaction and relocation across fields. It does not establish that all methodology became a separate science of ādāb, or that legal methods lost their distinct disciplinary warrants.

## 20. Revised historical model and exact priorities

### 20.1 The strongest conclusion supported by the combined evidence

There is no need to infer the existence of methodological reflection merely from general words such as *qānūn* or *aṣl*. The project has now identified passages that directly ask how a rule works, which rule is the right one, whether it is universal, how to establish it, and what to do when available arguments fail.

The evidence supports three connected histories:

1. **The Farabian–Rushdian productive tradition:** acquiring premises and a middle, controlling inferential form, evaluating loci, and coordinating Taḥlīl with Burhān and Jadal. Ibn Ṭumlūs is now a directly read later case, with a matched Middle Topics parallel.
2. **The Avicennan and later logical-commentary tradition:** general transitions as logic's questions, the two movements of thought, primitive warrant, conversion-rule discovery, universal proof, counterexamples, and unsettled cases. Its immediate textual links run through Ibn Sīnā, Fārābī, Rāzī, Ṭūsī, Kātibī, Quṭb, and the glossators; a Rushdian transmission is not established.
3. **The legal and regulated-inquiry tradition:** authority for using kinds of evidence, discovering intended universals or causes, managing objections, distinguishing certainty from probability, and debating general causal indicators. Ghazālī, the two distinct Āmidīs, Rāzī, Nasafī, Samarqandī, and later ādāb glosses supply different portions of this history.

These histories share problems and sometimes exchange procedures. Their differences matter: proving a formal entailment, establishing permission to act on a kind of report, and explaining why covariance supplies probability of causation are not the same warrant.

### 20.2 Five propositions for a publishable argument

**First:** a productive account of logic is a central source for Ibn Rushd's methodological vocabulary and practices. The appropriate Aristotelian frame includes Prior AnalyticsI.27–30 as well as Topics and Posterior Analytics.

**Second:** the Topics–Burhān relation is explicit within Ibn Rushd's own commentaries. Scientific definition discovery requires topical resources; dialectical collection can prepare material for evaluation by demonstrative rules.

**Third:** constructing an instrumental legal art involves more than importing valid forms. The Ḍarūrī's actual rules require linguistic, historical, evidential, and practical warrants. Ghazālī already develops important antecedents to this second-order inquiry.

**Fourth:** later logic commentaries are primary sources for methodological development. Ṭūsī and Quṭb/Jurjānī explicitly discover, justify, correct, and sometimes suspend judgment on rules. Their subject is frequently a technical chapter other than Burhān.

**Fifth:** a later field called ādāb al-baḥth supplies another locus for such reflection, but it neither absorbs all logic nor replaces uṣūl al-fiqh. Samarqandī's universal-principle question illustrates the overlap while preserving different standards and objects of inquiry.
