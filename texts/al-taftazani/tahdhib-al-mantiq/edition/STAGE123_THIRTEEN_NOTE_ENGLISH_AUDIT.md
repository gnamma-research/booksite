## HWN-112 (Q157–158): two distinct historical analyses of Islam and faith

**ʿAbd al-Raḥīm:** If Islam is taken as the verbal profession under discussion, a report attributed to al-Riḍā may suggest synonymy with faith. Yet the Shaykh of the school indicates that faith is more extensive, for it essentially concerns **assent in the heart**. The expression *īmān* in ordinary Arabic means accepting a statement as true, as in Joseph's brothers' “You will not believe us.” One should not presume a transfer to a novel technical meaning without evidence. A person who cannot speak, or one who remains silent, may nevertheless assent inwardly. Verbal and bodily expression is therefore required for faith **to become manifest**, not to constitute its essence.

**Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī — distinct omitted continuation:** In the reports, Islam can consist of **outward profession** that God is one and Muḥammad His messenger, whether or not inner conviction and corresponding deeds accompany it. In this respect Islam is broader than faith. A report from al-Ṣādiq distinguishes the legal outward profession, upon which protection of life, marriages and inheritance proceed, from faith established inwardly and manifested through action. Q 49:14 likewise distinguishes verbal submission from faith that has entered the heart. Q 3:19 does not erase the distinction: the general word *Islam* may be employed for a more complete instance that includes confession, conviction and deeds, as in Q 2:131. The glossator's alternatives concern **which instance of a general sense is intended**, not uncertainty over the lexical meaning of Islam. To restrict Islam exclusively to conviction would be a figurative extension requiring separate justification.

Sources: [Q157](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/157?lang=ar) and [Q158](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/158?lang=ar). The old English omitted Mīrzā's historical continuation entirely; this distinction cannot be erased.

## HWN-INTRO-018 (Q168–169): perception of particulars and divine knowledge

**Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī, first historical comment:** The definition of knowledge as a form becoming present has been understood in several ways: as the soul's reception of a form or as a thing's form becoming present to the intellect. On different analyses knowledge is considered under quality, reception, or relation. Extended works of philosophy must settle that question.

**Modern Muḥammad al-Karmī, separately attributed:** A later quotation discusses knowledge as reality's disclosure and the possibility of misleading mental images. This is a **modern insert**, not an uninterrupted historical Mīrzā text.

**Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī, resumed historical argument formerly absent:** One may object that **particular perceptions** occur in particular organs and faculties, whereas the intellect apprehends universals. The answer is that the intellect does not perceive particulars **by itself without mediation**, but can perceive them **through the outward and inward particular faculties**. Some objects become present directly; others are represented by those intermediate powers. A second objection says that this definition omits **God's knowledge**, for the name “intellect” is not applied to Him. The answer is that the definition concerns **acquired or acquirable knowledge** in the present discipline. God's knowledge is neither; this does not undermine logical generality, which is general **relative to the discipline's intended objects**, not absolutely every being called knowledge.

Sources: [Q168](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/168?lang=ar), [Q169](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/169?lang=ar). This note's old digital-source SHA discrepancy is not yet resolved; its English is a *working* reconstruction.
