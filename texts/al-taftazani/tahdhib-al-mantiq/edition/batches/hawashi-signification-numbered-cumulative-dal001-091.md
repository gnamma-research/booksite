## Signification note 1 — PARTIAL English / unresolved

**ID:** `HWN-DALALA-001` · **Qum source page(s):** [188](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/188?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** al-Taqrīb, printed p.20 (quoted) · **Arabic:** SHORT locator ONLY · **Photo/manuscript verification:** NO

### Arabic locator (NOT full source text)

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

حواشي التصورات بحث الدلالات قوله دلالة اللفظ

</div>

### English — PARTIAL argument rendering

Logic is primarily concerned with known concepts and judgments as means of acquiring further concepts and judgments. That purpose does not itself require studying the sounds of words. Nevertheless, traditional logic textbooks begin by discussing simple and compound expressions, univocal and gradated terms, and related distinctions because they assist the presentation of conceptual inquiry.

Signification in general occurs when cognition of one thing leads to cognition of another. Conventional verbal signification depends on the designation of an expression for a meaning; a sound never conventionally assigned to a meaning does not possess that particular signification. Within verbal conventional signification, *muṭābaqa* indicates the whole meaning assigned, *taḍammun* indicates a constituent part, and *iltizām* indicates an accompanying implication outside the meaning itself.

### Source/translation caution

Long source note abbreviated, not a full literal English translation. The al-Taqrīb author should not be conflated with Yazdī.

## Signification note 6 — first-pass English complete

**ID:** `HWN-DALALA-006` · **Qum source page(s):** [189](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/189?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** Shaykh ʿAbd al-Raḥīm · **Arabic:** SHORT locator ONLY · **Photo/manuscript verification:** NO

### Arabic locator (NOT full source text)

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

الدلالة الالتزامية مهجورة في الحدود التامة

</div>

### English — working first pass

Logic needs an account of signification because technical rules concerning definitions distinguish whole, partial and implicative meanings. In a complete essential definition, an external implication cannot replace the defining content; the use of constituent meanings likewise has restrictions. The five predicables belong to the study of simple meanings, requiring a prior distinction between simple and compound expressions. Figurative and equivocal expressions are normally avoided in definitions unless a clarifying indicator is present. Univocal and gradated terms also matter: some allow a gradated expression to function as a genus while others deny it. These controversies explain the conventional introductory study of language.

## Signification note 21 — PARTIAL English / unresolved

**ID:** `HWN-DALALA-021` · **Qum source page(s):** [191](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/191?lang=ar) · [192](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/192?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī (signature before 22) · **Arabic:** SHORT locator ONLY · **Photo/manuscript verification:** NO

### Arabic locator (NOT full source text)

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله أو على ما هو خارج عنه لازم له

</div>

### English — PARTIAL argument rendering

The third signification is a word's indicating something outside its assigned meaning but necessarily associated with it. The word “human,” for example, may indicate the capacity to laugh. The three species are called *muṭābaqa* because the word agrees with its full assigned meaning, *taḍammun* because the signified part lies *within* that meaning, and *iltizām* because the external item accompanies it. In each case the name transfers from the explanatory relation to the resulting indication. The word *iltizām* is chosen over *luzūm*, according to the glossator, to suggest an especially strong connection.

The commentator warns that these descriptions require the qualification “under that aspect” (*min ḥaythu huwa kadhālik*). Otherwise the same indication could be assigned to several species whenever one ambiguous expression is independently assigned to a whole, to one of its parts, and to an external correlate. Suppose the word is assigned independently to both the whole and the part. Its indicating the part **as an assigned meaning in its own right** is by correspondence, but indicating that identical part **as a constituent of the wider assigned meaning** is by inclusion. Similarly, a word separately assigned to a consequent can indicate it by correspondence, whereas the same item considered as the consequent of another assigned meaning is indicated by implication. The bare object indicated does not determine the kind; the *respect in which it is indicated* does.

The intended passage is a division pointing toward definitions, not a fully developed independent definition of each kind. The source therefore allows the accustomed contextual qualifications to remain implicit.

### Source/translation caution

Long note p191–192 with multiple complex counterexamples; not fully word-for-word, source translation marked PARTIAL pending clause alignment.

## Signification note 22 — PARTIAL English / unresolved

**ID:** `HWN-DALALA-022` · **Qum source page(s):** [192](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/192?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī · **Arabic:** SHORT locator ONLY · **Photo/manuscript verification:** NO

### Arabic locator (NOT full source text)

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله في الدلالة الالتزامية

</div>

### English — PARTIAL argument rendering

A word cannot indicate *every* thing external to its assigned meaning; otherwise one finite expression would signify infinitely many unrelated objects. Implicative signification must therefore have an additional condition, namely an association of the right kind between what the word primarily signifies and the external item.

Whole-meaning signification and contained-part signification require no corresponding further condition beyond knowing the lexical assignment. Someone who knows that a word was assigned to several meanings can understand the several meanings when the word is uttered, although the speaker may intend only one. Likewise a complex assigned meaning gives access to its constituents. Conventional indication is not identical with the speaker's current intention: the hearer may understand meanings which the speaker did not intend. Nor does this produce an infinity of significations, since no finite word is assigned independently to infinitely many meanings, and no finite assigned meaning has infinitely many constituents in the relevant sense.

Implicative signification differs because there may be indefinitely many external objects. The requirement of a sufficiently clear concomitance prevents taking every causal, accidental or merely possible association as something actually implied by the word. The discussion turns on the difference between *an association which can be discovered with further thought* and one that is immediately available through the understanding of the assigned meaning.

**ID:** `HWN-DALALA-068` · **Qum source page(s):** [206](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/206?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** Shaykh ʿAbd al-Raḥīm with separate Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī continuation · **Arabic:** SHORT locator ONLY · **Photo/manuscript verification:** NO

### English — PARTIAL argument rendering

For a conventional assignment to be made, the assigner must have some conception of the item for which an expression is selected. If the item conceived is particular and the word is assigned to that particular, the act of assignment is specific and the meaning assigned specific. If a universal is conceived and a word assigned to that universal, both the act of assignment and what is assigned may be general. The source uses this distinction to debate pronouns, demonstratives, relative pronouns and expressions made definite through the article.
