## Qum printed page 67 · L.12

### Truth-exclusion versus falsity-exclusion; intrinsic versus material opposition

**Arabic source:** https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/67?lang=ar  
**Attribution:** al-Yazdī main ḥāshiya; separately signed ḥawāshī excluded. **Certification:** searchable printed transcription, photographic/manuscript collation pending.  
**Page seam:** continues-to-68

**العربية — Original**

نحو «هذا الشيء إما أن يكون حجرا وإما أن يكون شجرا». والمنفصلة المانعة الخلو ما حكم فيها بتنافي النسبتين أو لا تنافيهما في الكذب فقط، كقولك «إما أن يكون زيد في البحر وإما أن لا يغرق». قوله «أو صدقا فقط»: أي لا في الكذب أو مع قطع النظر عن الكذب حتى جاز أن تجتمع النسبتان في الكذب وأن لا تجتمعا؛ ويقال للمعنى الأول مانعة الجمع بالمعنى الأخص وللثاني مانعة الجمع بالمعنى الأعم. قوله «أو كذبا فقط»: أي لا في الصدق أو مع قطع النظر عن الصدق؛ والأول مانعة الخلو بالمعنى الأخص والثاني بالمعنى الأعم. قوله «لذاتي الجزءين»: أي إن كان المنافاة بين الطرفين، أي المقدم والتالي، منافاة ناشئة عن ذاتيهما في أي مادة تحققا، كالمنافاة بين الزوجية والفردية، لا عن خصوص المادة، كالمنافاة بين السواد والكتابة في إنسان يكون أسود و

**English — Translation**

... as in ‘This thing is either a stone or a tree’. A **falsity-excluding** disjunction (*māniʿat al-khulū*) affirms or denies incompatibility of the two components with respect to being **false together only**, as in ‘Zayd is either in the sea or does not drown’. On ‘in truth only’: this may mean ‘and not in falsity’, or ‘considered without deciding what happens regarding falsity’. In the first interpretation the two components may **both be false**, but not true together; in the second the issue of their jointly being false is left open. These are called the **narrower** and the **broader** senses of truth-exclusion, respectively. Similarly, ‘in falsity only’ may mean exclusion of joint falsity together with allowance of joint truth, or simply exclude joint falsity without deciding the truth case. These are the narrower and broader senses of falsity-exclusion. On ‘due to the natures of the two components’: the incompatibility arises from the two components themselves, irrespective of the particular subject matter in which they are instantiated, as with even and odd. This is distinguished from incompatibility arising from the **particular matter** of the proposition, as in the example of blackness and writing applied to a human who is black and ...

**Editorial notes**

1. The source distinguishes **narrow** and **broad** interpretations of the formulas ‘in truth only’ and ‘in falsity only’. Do not reduce all forms to a single exclusive-or operator.

2. The initial comparison is of **negative conditions on joint truth/joint falsity**; it does not assert a real metaphysical incompatibility of blackness and writing in all subjects.

3. The final source sentence continues from Q67 to Q68, with the example of a person black and nonwriting versus writing and nonblack.

4. Qum 67's extended text after «هامش» is a different writer's criticism of applying conditional quantification over every possible circumstance and is deliberately excluded.

## Qum printed page 68 · L.12

### Conditional propositions quantified over antecedent suppositions

**Arabic source:** https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/68?lang=ar  
**Attribution:** al-Yazdī main ḥāshiya; separately signed ḥawāshī excluded. **Certification:** searchable printed transcription, photographic/manuscript collation pending.  
**Page seam:** complete-at-page

**العربية — Original**

غير كاتب أو يكون كاتبا وغير أسود، فالمنافاة بين طرفي هذه القضية المنفصلة واقعة لا لذاتيهما بل بحسب خصوص المادة؛ إذ قد يجتمع السواد والكتابة في الصدق أو في الكذب في مادة أخرى، فهذه منفصلة حقيقية اتفاقية. قوله «ثم الحكم» إلخ: كما أن الحملية تنقسم إلى محصورة ومهملة وشخصية وطبيعية، كذلك الشرطية أيضا، سواء كانت متصلة أو منفصلة، تنقسم إلى المحصورة الكلية والجزئية والمهملة والشخصية، ولا يتعقل الطبيعية هاهنا. قوله «على جميع تقادير المقدم»: كقولنا «كلما كانت الشمس طالعة فالنهار موجود». قوله «فكلية»: وسورها في المتصلة الموجبة «كلما» و«متى» و«مهما» وما في معناها، وفي المنفصلة «دائما» و«أبدا» ونحوهما؛ هذا في الموجبة وأما في السالبة مطلقا فسورها «ليس البتة». قوله «أو بعضها مطلقا»: أي على بعض غير معين، كقولك «قد يكون إذا كان الشيء حيوانا كان إنسانا». قوله «فجزئية»: وسورها في الموجبة، متصلة كانت أو منفصلة، «قد يكون»، وفي السالبة كذلك «قد لا يكون». قوله «فشخصية»: كقولك «إن جئتني اليوم أكرمك». قوله «وإلا»: أي وإن لم يكن الحكم على جميع تقادير المقدم ولا على بعضها بأن يسكت عن بيان الكلية والبعضية مطلقا. قوله «فمهملة»: نحو «إذا كان الشيء إنسانا كان حيوانا». قوله «في الأصل»: أي قبل دخول أداة الاتصال والانفصال عليهما.

**English — Translation**

... not writing, or is writing and not black. The opposition between the components in this particular disjunctive proposition is due not to the two natures of blackness and writing, but to the **special matter** under discussion: in another context blackness and writing may both hold or both fail. This is therefore called an **accidental genuine disjunction**. On ‘then the judgment’: just as categorical propositions can be quantified, indefinite, singular, or natural, so a conditional—connected or disjunctive—can be universal or particular among the quantified kinds, indefinite, or singular. A natural proposition does not arise in this division. On ‘under all suppositions of the antecedent’: for example, ‘Whenever the sun has risen, daylight exists’. On ‘universal’: the universal connected affirmative uses operators such as *kullamā* (‘whenever’), *matā* (‘whenever’) and *mahmā* (‘whatever the circumstance’); the disjunctive uses expressions such as ‘always’ and ‘ever’. The universal negative uses ‘not at all’ (*laysa al-batta*). On ‘or in some suppositions without designation’: for example, ‘It can happen that, if something is an animal, it is a human’. On ‘particular’: *qad yakūn* (‘it may happen’) expresses the affirmative, whether connected or disjunctive; *qad lā yakūn* expresses the negative. On ‘singular’: ‘If you come to me today, I shall honour you’. On ‘otherwise’: no commitment is made whether the connection holds under all or some possible antecedent suppositions; the scope is left unspecified. On ‘indefinite’: ‘If something is human, it is an animal’. On ‘originally’: before an operator of connection or disjunction is introduced between the component propositions.

**Editorial notes**

1. The distinction between a genuine disjunction as contrary by **its terms** and one that happens to be contrary in a specific matter is not merely verbal: it controls which inferential substitutions are licensed.

2. The conditional ‘quantity’ is over *taqādīr*—suppositions or circumstances under which the antecedent is considered—not directly over individuals of a simple subject term.

3. The source explicitly excludes a separately defined ‘natural’ conditional at this point. This is a feature of the historical classification, not a general denial that natures can enter conditional judgments.

4. The Q68 «هامش» is a long continuation of the objection by Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī that begins on p67 and does not belong to the source al-Yazdī commentary.

## Qum printed page 69 · L.12

### A conditional's components are independently complete only before connection

**Arabic source:** https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/69?lang=ar  
**Attribution:** al-Yazdī main ḥāshiya; separately signed ḥawāshī excluded. **Certification:** searchable printed transcription, photographic/manuscript collation pending.  
**Page seam:** end-of-L12

**العربية — Original**

قوله «حمليتان»: كقولنا «إن كانت الشمس طالعة فالنهار موجود»، فإن طرفيها وهما «الشمس طالعة» و«النهار موجود» قضيتان حمليتان. قوله «أو متصلتان»: كقولنا «كلما إن كانت الشمس طالعة فالنهار موجود فكلما لم يكن النهار موجودا لم تكن الشمس طالعة»، فإن طرفيها وهما قولنا «إن كانت الشمس طالعة فالنهار موجود» وقولنا «كلما لم يكن النهار موجودا لم تكن الشمس طالعة» قضيتان متصلتان. قوله «أو منفصلتان»: كقولنا «كلما كان دائما إما أن يكون العدد زوجا أو فردا فدائما إما أن يكون العدد منقسما بمتساويين أو غير منقسم بهما». قوله «أو مختلفتان»: بأن يكون أحد الطرفين حملية والآخر متصلة، أو أحدهما حملية والآخر منفصلة، أو أحدهما متصلة والآخر منفصلة؛ فالأقسام ستة وعليك باستخراج ما تركناه من الأمثلة. قوله «عن التمام»: أي عن أن يصح السكوت عليهما ويحتملا الصدق والكذب. مثلا قولنا «الشمس طالعة» مركب تام خبري يحتمل الصدق والكذب ولا نعني بالقضية إلا هذا، فإذا أدخلت عليه أداة الاتصال مثلا وقلت «إن كانت الشمس طالعة» لم يصح حينئذ أن تسكت عليه ولم يحتمل الصدق والكذب، بل احتجت إلى أن تضم إليه قولك مثلا «فالنهار موجود».

**English — Translation**

On ‘two categoricals’: in ‘If the sun is up, daylight exists’, both components—‘the sun is up’ and ‘daylight exists’—are categorical propositions. On ‘or two connected conditionals’: in the nested argument ‘Whenever, if the sun is up, daylight exists, then, whenever daylight does not exist, the sun is not up’, the antecedent and consequent are themselves connected conditional propositions. On ‘or two disjunctions’: one may likewise connect a disjunction between even and odd numbers to a disjunction between being divisible into two equal parts and not being so divisible. On ‘or two different kinds’: one component may be categorical and the other connected, categorical and disjunctive, or connected and disjunctive. There are thus six classes, and the reader is invited to construct examples of those not explicitly furnished. On ‘ceasing to be complete’: the components cease, under the introduction of the connecting or separating operator, to be complete utterances that can stand alone and be true or false in that unconnected grammatical form. Thus ‘The sun is up’ is a complete report to which truth or falsity applies. But after introducing the connective and saying only ‘If the sun is up ...’, one cannot properly stop; that fragment cannot yet stand as an independent assertion until a consequent such as ‘... daylight exists’ is added.

**Editorial notes**

1. Al-Yazdī treats the two original components as propositions **before** being governed by the connective. Their subsequent grammatical incompleteness does not imply that the subordinate propositions are metaphysically annihilated.

2. The source's nested conditional and disjunctive examples are retained in their historical formulation rather than silently converted to symbolic notation; semantic formalization needs separate checking.

3. The p69 main شرح ends before its long separately attributed هامش, which discusses cases where a consequent is imperatival or otherwise nonassertoric. Do not attribute that later author's critique to al-Yazdī.

4. Q70 opens the new section «التناقض» (L.13), so p69 is a natural end of this continuous commentary unit.

The description-conditioned actual proposition stands to customary permanence as the general actual proposition stands to unrestricted permanence. The first asserts that the relation is actual **while the subject bears its named description**. The explicit negation of customary permanence is denial that the relation persists for that entire descriptive interval; its counterpart therefore actually occurs while that description obtains. Thus ‘Every writer keeps the fingers moving throughout writing’ is contradicted by ‘Some writer has fingers not moving at some time while writing’. Al-Taftāzānī leaves aside the contradictories of the unrestricted time-specific and dispersed-time necessary forms, not because they cannot be considered, but because the subsequent rules for conversion and syllogism do not require them here. On ‘and for the compound proposition’: a compound assertion is negated by rejecting **at least one** constituent. Which one is rejected is not predetermined, and **both may be false**. Its contradictory accordingly has the character of an *inclusive*, falsity-excluding disjunction between the constituents' contradictories. For the special conditioned proposition asserting that every writer necessarily moves the fingers while writing but not permanently throughout life, the opposing judgment is a disjunction: either some writer can fail to move the fingers while writing, or some writer does move them permanently. Once the constituent meanings and their simple contradictories are understood, the remaining cases can be derived. On ‘but in a particular proposition, separately for every individual’: it is **not enough** to disjoin the two universal negations of a particular compound assertion. The original particular compound can be false while both those supposed universal opposites are false. Consider ‘Some animal is human actually, but not permanently’. Its alleged pair of universal opposites, ‘No animal is ever human’ and ‘Every ...’, may both fail. What matters is testing the alternatives with respect to **each individual** falling under the subject, because the contradictory of a particular has universal scope ...

1. The first sentence completes Q71's discussion of description-conditioned necessity, not a fresh chapter.

2. The main formal innovation is the **inclusive disjunction** of negated components, not an exclusive-or. The `مانعة الخلو` character is explicit.

3. The particular-compound case requires quantifying over **each subject instance**, not just taking global negations of the two component formulas. This prevents a quantifier/disjunction scope error.

**English — Translation**

On ‘exchanging the contradictories of the two terms’: the contradictory of the original first term becomes the converted proposition's second term, and the contradictory of the second term becomes the first. On ‘with truth preserved’: when the original is true, so must its conversion be. On ‘with quality preserved’: an affirmative remains affirmative, and a negative remains negative. **English — Translation**

‘Composed of propositions’ excludes incomplete combinations, and also a single proposition that merely entails its simple conversion or contraposition. A composite proposition does not qualify merely because it has implicit multiple aspects: the ordinary meaning of *propositions* here refers to explicitly distinct propositions recognized as several. The word ‘entails’ excludes induction and analogy, which, in this classification, do not **necessitate** their conclusions; at most they lead to probable judgment. The phrase **‘from itself’** excludes a putative proof requiring an **additional external premise**. Consider: ‘A equals B, and B equals C.’ The conclusion ‘A equals C’ follows only with the principle ‘things equal to the same thing are equal to one another’. With that extraneous principle, this so-called syllogism of equality can be reduced to two proper syllogisms; without it, it is not an argument whose conclusion follows by its own organization. The conclusion of a syllogism is called its **result** (*natīja*) and what is sought (*maṭlūb*). On ‘if it is mentioned’—the conclusion may appear within the premises in both its **matter**, the two terms being related, and its **form**, the arrangement by which they are affirmed or denied. In an **exceptive** syllogism, what is explicitly mentioned may be the contradictory of the conclusion: ‘If this is human, it is animal; but it is not animal’, so ‘it is not human’. Or a component equal to the conclusion may be mentioned: ‘... but it is human’, so ‘it is animal’. The exceptive argument takes its name from the added term *lākinn(a)*, ‘but’. Otherwise, in a **conjunctive** syllogism, the terms occur but not the concluded propositional form, since the arrangement cannot be present independently of those terms. The commentator even suggests the author's wording would be preferable without the extra phrase ‘in its matter’, whose inclusion is superfluous in this context. ‘Conjunctive’ names the linking of the **minor, major, and middle** terms. Such a syllogism can be **categorical** or **conditional**. The categorical consists exclusively of categorical premises, as in ‘The world changes; everything changing has originated; therefore the world ...’

**English — Translation**

The first method is **reductio**: add the contradictory of the desired negative conclusion as an affirmative minor to the original universal major; a first-figure syllogism then derives a judgment incompatible with the original minor. This works for **all four** productive second-figure moods. The second method **converts the major premise** and reduces the arrangement to the first figure, which then produces the required conclusion. It works only for the first and third moods, where the major is universal negative and converts as a universal negative; it fails for the two moods with universal affirmative majors, which convert only to particulars unsuitable as first-figure majors. Their original minors are also negative, unsuitable for the first figure's minor condition. The third method **converts the minor premise**, then exchanges the order of the two premises, obtaining a first-figure arrangement whose result is convertible into the target conclusion. This requires the converted minor to be universal, so it works only for the second mood with a universal negative minor. In the other cases the original affirmative minor converts only to a particular, or a particular negative does not convert at all. On ‘the minor must affirm actual predication’: in the **third figure**, the major predicate is asserted of a middle term actually instantiated; the minor term must also connect to that middle actually. If the minor is negative or merely affirms a possibility of the middle rather than actual instantiation, the necessary link is not made. On ‘one of the premises must be universal’: if both premises are particular, the portion of the middle to which the minor term applies may differ from the portion to which the major term applies. Thus ‘Some animals are human; some animals are horses’ does not establish ‘Some humans are horses’.

**Editorial notes**

1. The passage begins with the three methods introduced by Q93, then transitions to **third-figure** conditions in the same printed page. This is *not* an authorial new chapter within Q94; the editorial title reflects both source topics.

2. The distinction between **proof by reductio**, **conversion of the major**, and **conversion of the minor followed by reordering** belongs to al-Yazdī’s main text.

3. A third-figure syllogism with two particular premises lacks a guaranteed shared witness; some animal human and some animal horse need not be the **same** animal.

4. The final line under هامش, «موضوع الصغرى هو موضوع النتيجة (التقريب ص96)», belongs to another annotated source and is excluded.

**English — Translation**

On "the two affirmatives produce": the third figure has six productive moods under the stated conditions. Four arise by pairing a universal affirmative minor with each of the four major forms; two more arise by pairing a particular affirmative minor with a universal affirmative or universal negative major. None of the six yields a universal conclusion: all their results are particular. Three are affirmative, three negative. The first affirmative mood has two universal affirmative premises: "Every C is B; every C is A; therefore some B is A." The second has a particular affirmative minor and universal affirmative major. The third reverses the relative quantities of those two premises. When the author writes "or conversely", he means the reversal of the second configuration, not of both previous configurations; reversing the first, whose premises are both universal, reproduces it. Of the negative moods, the first combines a universal affirmative minor with universal negative major, the second a particular affirmative minor with universal negative major, and the third a universal affirmative minor with particular negative major. On "by reductio": these six results can be established by three methods. Third, convert the major to arrive at the fourth figure, reverse the premise order to obtain the first figure, derive a result, and convert that result to the desired conclusion. This last procedure applies to the first and third moods, where the original major is affirmative and the original minor universal. These restrictions belong to the argument, not additional moods.

Then the middle's modal relation to the major description is, for example, necessary or permanent affirmative, while its relation to the minor's actual subject is denied actually or under a stronger condition; these are incompatible. **English — Translation**

On "exceptive": an exceptive syllogism includes the matter and arrangement of its conclusion in one of its components. It combines a conditional premise with a categorical exceptive premise that asserts or denies one constituent of the conditional so as to infer the other constituent or its denial. Four formal moves can be envisaged: asserting either component or denying either component. Which moves are productive depends on the kind of conditional. With a connected, relationally necessary conditional, asserting the antecedent establishes the consequent, and denying the consequent excludes the antecedent. Asserting the consequent does not establish the antecedent, nor does denying the antecedent establish the consequent's denial: the consequent may apply more widely than the antecedent. The connected form here must therefore be luzūmiyya, not a merely accidental conjunction. For disjunctive premises, the intended kind is grounded in opposition. A truth-excluding disjunction allows asserting one alternative to deny the other, but not denying one to assert the other, because both could be false. For a falsity-excluding disjunction the productive moves are reversed. A fully exclusive disjunction excludes both joint truth and joint falsity, so all four moves are productive. For example, "If this is human it is animal; but it is human; therefore animal"; or "but not animal; therefore not human." For the even/odd disjunction, "this number is either even or odd; but even ..."

**English — Translation**

... it is not odd; but if odd, then not even; if it is not odd, it is even; and if not even it is odd. On "as with truth-exclusion": "This is either a tree or a stone; but it is a tree, so not a stone; but if a stone, not a tree." On "as with falsity-exclusion": "This is either nonstone or nontree; but it is not nontree, so it is nonstone; but if not nonstone, then nontree." On "it may be called a proof by contradiction": to establish a claim, assume it false. Its contradictory would then hold, since contradictories cannot both fail. But the contradictory is shown impossible, and so the claim must hold. This argument is called khalf because the assumption leads to an impossibility or because the desired claim is reached from behind, through its contradictory. Strictly it is not one syllogism. **English — Translation**

... so that the ruling can be established in the first particular. Analogy means comparing one particular case to another through a meaning common to both, then establishing for the compared case the ruling of its model because that meaning explains the model's ruling. A conventional example is the prohibition of a fermented drink by reference to wine: if wine is forbidden on account of intoxication and the same intoxicating property occurs in the other drink, the ruling is extended. But the formulation is imprecise if presented as the definition of the **argument itself**. Just as 'conversion' can name either the act of exchanging terms or the proposition produced by exchange, 'analogy' can name either the act of comparing or the argument within which the comparison occurs. The author has changed the familiar definitions of induction and analogy to avoid this ambiguity; has he not, ironically, returned to the problem from which he sought to escape? On 'the principal methods are concomitance and elimination': an analogical inference needs three premises. (1) The ruling obtains in the **original case** (*aṣl*). (2) A specified **property is the cause** (*ʿilla*) of that ruling in the original. (3) The property exists in the **new case** (*farʿ*). Once these are established, the thinker passes to the ruling for the new case. The first and third premises are relatively clear; the difficult one is the **causal premise**. Legal theory explains several methods for proving it, of which two are especially important. First is **concomitance** (*dawarān*): the ruling appears and disappears as the candidate causal property appears and disappears, such as a prohibition associated with intoxicating quality. The theorists take covariation as evidence that the characteristic being tracked explains the ruling. The source presents this as a method used by theorists, not a modern universal proof that any observed correlation establishes causation.

الثاني: الترديد ويسمى بالسبر والتقسيم أيضا، وهو أن يتفحص أولا أوصاف الأصل، ويردد أن علة الحكم هل هي هذه الصفة أو تلك، ثم يبطل ثانيا حكم عليه كل كل حتى يستقر على وصف واحد، ويستفاد من ذلك كون هذا الوصف علة. كما يقال: علة حرمة الخمر إما الاتخاذ من العنب أو الميعان أو اللون المخصوص أو الطعم المخصوص أو الرائحة المخصوصة أو الإسكار، لكن الأول ليس بعلة لوجوده في الدبس بدون الحرمة، وكذا البواقي ما سوى الإسكار بمثل ما ذكر، فتعين الإسكار للعلية.

**English — Translation**

The second method is **eliminative enumeration** (*tardīd*), also called *sabr wa-taqsīm*. One first examines the features of the original case and asks which one explains the ruling. One then considers the rival candidate characteristics and defeats each candidate in turn, leaving a single proposed cause. For example, the cause of wine's prohibition might be that it comes from grapes, is liquid, has a particular colour, taste or smell, or is intoxicating. This is presented as a jurisprudential argument for identifying an operative cause, not as a formal deductive guarantee without substantive evidence that the list of candidates is exhaustive.

**English — Translation**

... the lower class: a discourse composed of one commonly accepted premise and one merely imaginative premise is **poetic**, not dialectical. On "from certain premises": certainty is **firm assent corresponding to reality and remaining stable**. As assent it excludes mere imagination, doubt, estimating appearance and other conceptions. Firmness excludes probable opinion; correspondence excludes confident false belief (compound ignorance); stability excludes conviction resting merely on imitation of another. Certain premises are either self-evident or acquired by demonstration, the latter ultimately terminating in self-evident foundations if circularity and infinite regress are to be avoided. The commentator classifies the basic immediate sources of certainty into **six kinds**, deriving them by further divisions. If grasping the terms and their relation suffices for assent, the proposition is a **first principle** (*awwaliyya*). Otherwise it may rest on **observation**, either by outward senses (*ḥissiyyāt*) or inward apprehension (*wijdāniyyāt*). If the mediating reasoning is so inseparable from the initial concepts that it is never absent when they are grasped, it is an **innate or concomitant syllogistic truth** (*fiṭriyya*, a proposition whose syllogism accompanies it). If not, immediate insight (*ḥads*) may carry the mind swiftly from principles to the sought claim, producing an **intuited proposition** (*ḥadsiyya*). Alternatively the judgment may arise from widespread independent testimony whose deliberate collusion is rationally impossible (*mutawātirāt*), or from repeated experience (*mujarrabāt*). Each kind has its own source. The source examples include the whole exceeding its part for first principles, sight of daylight or fire for external observation, awareness of hunger and thirst for inward observation, a traditional claim concerning a purgative medicinal substance for experience, the moon's light coming from the sun for intuition, and the existence of Mecca for widespread testimony. These are illustrations of epistemic kinds, not independently verified medical or astronomical findings.

1. The four clauses in the definition of certainty—assent, firmness, correspondence, stability—each exclude a different epistemic deficiency; this is particularly relevant to later kalām epistemology.

2. The six foundational categories include both external and internal observation under the general heading *mushāhadāt*; count them as six major types (not seven unrelated sources).

3. The printed line has bracketed «المطابق» and «المطلوب» alternatives, without independently identified manuscript sigla; retain the running text pending photo verification.

4. No recommendation is made concerning the example herb; it is a historical doctrinal illustration.

### Burhān limmī and innī: cause of knowing versus cause of being

**Attribution:** al-Yazdī main ḥāshiya; separately signed ḥawāshī excluded. **Certification:** searchable printed transcription, photographic/manuscript collation pending.

**العربية — Original**

قوله «والفطريات»: كقولنا الأربعة زوج، فإن الحكم فيه بواسطة لا تغيب عن ذهنك عند ملاحظة أطراف هذا الحكم، وهو الانقسام بمتساويين. قوله «ثم إن كان»: الحد الأوسط في البرهان بل في كل قياس لا بد أن يكون علة لحصول العلم بالنسبة الإيجابية أو السلبية المطلوبة في النتيجة، ولهذا يقال له الواسطة في الإثبات والواسطة في التصديق. فإن كان مع ذلك واسطة في الثبوت أيضا، أي علة لتلك النسبة الإيجابية أو السلبية في الواقع وفي نفس الأمر، كتعفن الأخلاط في قولك هذا متعفن الأخلاط وكل متعفن الأخلاط محموم فهذا محموم، فالبرهان حينئذ يسمى البرهان اللمي لدلالته على ما هو لم الحكم وعلته في الواقع. وإن لم يكن واسطة في الثبوت أيضا، يعني لم يكن علة لتلك النسبة الإيجابية أو السلبية في الواقع وفي نفس الأمر، فالبرهان حينئذ يسمى البرهان الإني، حيث لم يدل إلا على إنية الحكم وتحققه في الذهن دون عليته للحكم في الواقع. سواء كانت الواسطة حينئذ معلولا للحكم كالحمى في قولنا زيد محموم وكل محموم متعفن الأخلاط فزيد متعفن الأخلاط، وقد يخص هذا باسم الدليل، أو لم تكن معلولا للحكم كما أنها ليست علة له بل يكونان معلولين لثالث، وهذا لم يخص باسم، كما يقال هذه الحمى تشتد غبا وكل حمى تشتد غبا محرقة فهذه الحمى محرقة، فإن الاشتداد غبا ليس معلولا للإحراق ولا العكس بل كلاهما معلولان للصفراء المتعفنة الخارجة من العروق. قوله «من المشهورات»: هي القضايا التي تطابق فيها آراء الكل كحسن الإحسان وقبح العدوان، أو آراء طائفة كقبح ذبح الحيوانات عند أهل الهند. قوله «والمسلمات»: هي القضايا التي سلمت من الخصم في المناظرة أو برهن عليها في علم وأخذت في آخر على سبيل التسليم. قوله «من المقبولات»: هي القضايا التي تؤخذ عمن يعتقد فيه كالأولياء والحكماء.

**English — Translation**

On "the innate propositions": "Four is even" is known through an intermediate notion that cannot be absent when one considers the two terms—divisibility into equal parts. On "if the middle also is": the **middle term** of a demonstration, indeed of any syllogism, must be the cause of acquiring knowledge of the affirmative or negative relation sought in the conclusion. It is therefore called the **medium of proof** or **medium of assent** (*wāsiṭa fī al-ithbāt / fī al-taṣdīq*). But the middle may additionally be the **cause of the very relation in reality**, the medium in being (*wāsiṭa fī al-thubūt*). If it is, the demonstration is *limmī*, a demonstration **of why** the fact obtains. The traditional medical example reasons from a putative corruption of bodily humours to fever: that physical condition is supposed to cause the fever and also explains why the judgment is known. If the middle is only a means of knowing and not a cause of the relation in extramental reality, the demonstration is *innī*, an account **that the fact is**, not why it is. In one kind the middle is an **effect** of the causal condition: reason from fever back to the corruption thought to produce it. This is sometimes called *dalīl*. In another, middle and predicate are neither cause nor effect of each other, but two effects of a **third cause**. The traditional example appeals to periodic exacerbation and a severe fever as joint effects of corrupted bile. These examples illustrate the causal pattern, not medically accepted mechanisms. On "commonly admitted premises": these are propositions acknowledged by everyone, such as the praiseworthiness of beneficence and blameworthiness of aggression, or acknowledged by a particular group. "Conceded premises" are those granted by an interlocutor during debate or established in one science and accepted for the purpose of another. "Accepted-on-authority premises" (*maqbūlāt*) are those taken from a person in whom the audience places confidence, such as a revered spiritual teacher or philosopher.

**Editorial notes**

1. The **cause of knowing** can differ from the **cause in reality**. This distinction between *wāsiṭa fī al-ithbāt* and *wāsiṭa fī al-thubūt* must be preserved in the English critical edition.

2. The example of the body's humours reflects historical humoral medicine; the logical account does not depend on its modern medical accuracy.

3. The distinction between *burhān innī* and *limmī* concerns the explanatory status of the middle term, not simply deductive validity.

4. Q112's printed main discussion includes examples of *mashhūrāt*, *musallamāt* and *maqbūlāt*; none is a later marginal addition.

Al-Yazdī objects: this seems contrary to the matn's explicit statement that questions are propositions with subjects and predicates. Moreover, if the questions are merely predicates ascribed to subjects, why are other particular subject-terms of the science not also independent components? Returning to the four possible senses of "subject": its **definition** may be counted separately despite belonging to conceptual principles, because of the subject's importance. The **assertion of its existence** may similarly receive special treatment. Indeed the standard account that the subject's existence belongs among propositional principles is loose: strictly, those principles are propositions used to compose the proofs of the science. The statement that syllogisms "are built upon" them is too broad as a definition. Some add the discussion of **words and expressions** as a tenth part. In the second sense one describes the organization of this particular book: it has two broad parts, Logic and Kalām. The Logic portion contains an introduction on its definition, purpose and subject; a first main section on conceptions; a second on assents; and a conclusion on the parts of the sciences. Kalām has its own divisions. A similar practice of arranging a book into introduction, chapters and conclusion appears in the *Shamsiyya*. On "methods of teaching": these are methods useful across the disciplines, on which commentators have offered divergent interpretations. Al-Yazdī proposes an account drawn from a reading of the classical texts and the *Sharḥ al-Maṭāliʿ*.

**Editorial notes**

1. The note about giving priority to Arabic grammar and literature is a **historically situated pedagogical recommendation**, not a universal prescribed syllabus.

2. The 9/10 divisions are the logical-arts taxonomy, distinct from the 19 editorial headings of the modern website.

3. The Q121 main paragraph precedes the separately signed Muḥammad ʿAlī هامش listing four methods; his gloss is not copied as Yazdī's authorial commentary.

### Constructing a syllogism backwards from a sought conclusion, then analyzing one already given

**العربية — Original**

قوله «وهي التقسيم»: كأن المراد به ما يسمى «تركيب القياس» أيضا. وذلك بأن يقال: إذا أردت تحصيل مطلب من المطالب التصديقية فضع طرفي المطلوب واطلب جميع موضوعات كل واحد منهما وجميع محمولات كل واحد منهما، سواء كان حمل الطرفين عليها وحملها على الطرفين بواسطة أو بغير واسطة، وكذلك اطلب جميع ما سلب عنه أحد الطرفين أو سلب هو عن أحدهما. ثم انظر إلى نسبة الطرفين إلى الموضوعات والمحمولات، فإن وجدت من محمولات موضوع المطلوب ما هو موضوع لمحموله فقد حصل المطلوب من الشكل الأول، أو ما هو محمول على محموله فمن الشكل الثاني، أو من موضوعات موضوعه ما هو موضوع لمحموله فمن الشكل الثالث، أو محمول لمحموله فمن الرابع، كل ذلك بعد اعتبار الشرائط بحسب الكمية والكيفية كما في شرح المطالع. وقد عبر المصنف عن هذا المعنى بقوله «أعني التكثير»، أي تكثير المقدمات أخذا «من فوق»، أي من النتيجة، لأنها المقصد الأقصى بالنسبة إلى الدليل. قوله «والتحليل»: في شرح المطالع كثيرا ما يورد في العلوم قياسات منتجة للمطالب لا على الهيئات المنطقية، لتساهل المركب اعتمادا على الفطن العالم بالقواعد. فإن أردت أن تعرف أنه على أي شكل من الأشكال فعليك بالتحليل وهو عكس التركيب. حصل المطلوب وانظر إلى القياس المنتج له، فإن كان فيه مقدمة تشارك المطلوب بكلا جزأيه فالقياس استثنائي، وإن كانت مشاركة للمطلوب بأحد جزأيه فالقياس اقتراني. ثم انظر إلى طرفي المطلوب ليتميز عندك الصغرى عن الكبرى، لأن ذلك الجزء إن كان محكوما عليه في النتيجة فهي الصغرى، أو محكوما به فيها فهي الكبرى. ثم ضم الجزء الآخر من المطلوب إلى الجزء الآخر من تلك المقدمة، فإن تألفا على أحد التأليفات الأربع فما انضم إلى جزئي المطلوب هو الحد الأوسط، ويتميز الشكل المنتج، وإن لم يتألفا كان القياس مركبا فاعمل بكل واحد منهما العمل المذكور، أي ضع الجزء الآخر من المطلوب والجزء الآخر من المقدمة كما وضعت طرفي المطلوب في التقسيم، فلا بد أن يكون لكل واحد منهما نسبة إلى شيء مما في القياس وإلا لم يكن القياس منتجا للمطلوب. فإن وجدت حدا

**English — Translation**

On "division": here it appears to mean **constructing a syllogism** (*tarkīb al-qiyās*). To establish a sought propositional conclusion, place its two extreme terms before you. Search for subjects and predicates connected with each extreme, including relations through intermediaries and negative predications. Compare the candidate terms and their relations. If a predicate of the intended conclusion's subject can be made the subject of the predicate-term, you have a first-figure construction; if the corresponding candidate term stands as a predicate of that predicate-term, a second-figure pattern; analogous arrangements give the third and fourth figures. In all cases the quantity and quality conditions for a productive syllogism must be checked. Al-Taftāzānī calls this "multiplication from above": one **expands backwards from the desired conclusion**, the uppermost end or aim of proof, seeking premises that will establish it. On "analysis": the reverse method is used when a proof has already been presented but without explicit syllogistic form, perhaps because its author trusted the competent reader to supply the arrangement. Identify its conclusion and examine the premises. A premise which reproduces the matter of the conclusion in both its terms suggests an exceptive argument; one sharing only one term suggests a conjunctive argument. Determine which component of the conclusion is its subject and which its predicate, so the corresponding premises can be classified as minor and major. Link the remaining terms and see whether a valid arrangement from one of the four figures emerges: the connecting item is the **middle term**. If not, the argument may be **compound**, requiring repeated decomposition until all its premises and intermediate conclusions are laid out. The printed page ends with "if you find a term ...", continued on page 123.

**Editorial notes**

1. The source describes a heuristic **backward search** for proof and a forward/reverse analysis of an existing argument.

2. The phrase «التكثير من فوق» is interpreted from the conclusion as the highest end or aim, not from a spatial diagram.

3. The layout's printed editorial alternatives around «حصل/حصلت» and some clause numbers are not proven manuscript variants.

4. The Q122 source runs into Q123 at «فإن وجدت حدا»; source seam must be preserved.

### Defining, demonstrating and avoiding imitation: the commentator's closing counsel

**Arabic source:** https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/123?lang=ar  
**Attribution:** al-Yazdī main ḥāshiya; separately signed ḥawāshī excluded. **Certification:** searchable printed transcription, photographic/manuscript collation pending.  
**Page seam:** end-main-ḥāshiya

**العربية — Original**

مشتركا بينهما فقد تم القياس وتبين لك المقدمات والأشكال والنتيجة. فقوله «وهو عكسه»: أي تكثير المقدمات إلى فوق، وهو النتيجة، كما مر وجهه. قوله «والتحديد أي فعل الحد»: يعني أن المراد بالتحديد بيان أخذ الحد، وكأن المراد المعرف مطلقا للأشياء. وذلك بأن يقال: إذا أردت تعريف شيء فلا بد أن تضع ذلك الشيء وتطلب جميع ما هو أعم منه وتحمل عليه بواسطة أو بغيرها، وتميز الذاتيات عن العرضيات بأن تعد ما هو بين الثبوت له وما يلزم من مجرد ارتفاعه ارتفاع نفس الماهية ذاتيا، وما ليس كذلك عرضيا عاما، وتطلب جميع ما هو مساو له فيتميز عندك الجنس من العرض العام والفصل من الخاصة، ثم تركب أي قسم شئت من أقسام المعرف بعد اعتبار الشرائط المذكورة في باب المعرف. قوله «أي الطريق إلى الوقوف على الحق»: أي اليقين إن كان المطلوب علما نظريا، وإلى الوقوف عليه والعمل به إن كان علما عمليا. كما يقال إذا أردت الوصول إلى اليقين فلا بد أن تستعمل في الدليل بعد ملاحظة شرائط صحة الصورة إما الضروريات الست أو ما يحصل منها بصورة صحيحة وهيئة منتجة، وتبالغ في التفحص عن ذلك حتى لا تشتبه بالمشهورات أو المسلمات أو المشبهات، ولا تذعن لشيء بمجرد حسن الظن به أو بمن تسمع منه حتى لا تقع في مضيق الخطابة ولا ترتبط بربقة التقليد. قوله «وهذا بالمقاصد أشبه»: أي الأمر الثامن أشبه بمقاصد الفن منه بالمقدمات، ولذا ترى المتأخرين كصاحب المطالع يوردون ما سوى التحديد في مباحث الحجة ولواحق القياس، وأما التحديد فشأنه أن يذكر في مباحث المعرف. وقيل هذا إشارة إلى العمل وكونه أشبه بالمقصود ظاهر، بل المقصود من العلم العمل. جعلنا الله وإياكم من الراسخين في الأمرين، ورزقنا بفضله وجوده سعادة الدارين بحق نبيه محمد خير البرية أجمعين وعترته الطاهرين، إنه موفق ومعين.

**English — Translation**

... shared between them, the proof is complete and its premises, form and result have become clear. On "its reverse": analysis runs in the opposite direction from constructing premises upward toward a target conclusion, as just explained. On "definition, that is, composing the defining formula": one should find what is more general than the item to be defined and what is predicable of it, directly or indirectly. Distinguish **essential** features from **accidental** ones: an essential feature belongs through what the thing is, and removing it would destroy the essence; a general accident does not have that role. Identify the concepts coextensive with the item, separating **genus from common accident** and **differentia from property**, and then construct the appropriate type of defining account according to the rules given earlier. On "the way to reach truth": for a theoretical science this means knowledge of truth with certainty; for a practical science it means knowing what is true and **acting upon it**. To attain certainty, ensure first that a demonstration has the proper form; then employ the six sorts of necessary foundational premises, or what follows soundly from them. Investigate carefully so that they are not confused with mere common opinions, concessions or deceptive semblances. Do **not** assent simply because you have a favourable opinion of a proposition or admire the person from whom you heard it: that would trap you in rhetoric or bind you to imitation. On "this is more like the aims of the discipline": the eighth introductory heading, concerned with educational procedures, belongs more naturally among the discipline's proper contents than among its preliminaries. Later commentators therefore locate most such procedures in the study of proof and its subordinate topics, and defining procedures in the study of definition. Another interpretation takes the phrase to refer to **practice**: the point of knowledge is action. Al-Yazdī closes by asking God to establish reader and author alike in knowledge and practice and grant them happiness in both worlds.
