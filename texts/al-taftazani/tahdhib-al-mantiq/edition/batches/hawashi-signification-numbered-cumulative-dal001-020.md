## Signification note 1 — PARTIAL — incomplete source translation

**ID:** `HWN-DALALA-001` · **Reported voice:** al-Taqrīb, printed p.20 (quoted) · **Original printed source:** [p.188](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/188?lang=ar) · **Arabic:** short locator ONLY. **Independent witness/photo certification:** NO.

### Short Arabic locator (NOT entire note)

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

حواشي التصورات بحث الدلالات قوله دلالة اللفظ

</div>

### Working English (partial)

Logic is primarily concerned with known concepts and judgments as means of acquiring further concepts and judgments. That purpose does not itself require studying the sounds of words. Nevertheless, traditional logic textbooks begin by discussing simple and compound expressions, univocal and gradated terms, and related distinctions because they assist the presentation of conceptual inquiry.

Signification in general occurs when cognition of one thing leads to cognition of another. Conventional verbal signification depends on the designation of an expression for a meaning; a sound never conventionally assigned to a meaning does not possess that particular signification. Within verbal conventional signification, *muṭābaqa* indicates the whole meaning assigned, *taḍammun* indicates a constituent part, and *iltizām* indicates an accompanying implication outside the meaning itself.

### Editorial qualifications

Long source note abbreviated, not a full literal English translation. The al-Taqrīb author should not be conflated with Yazdī.

**ID:** `HWN-DALALA-002` · **Reported voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī · **Original printed source:** [p.188](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/188?lang=ar) · **Arabic:** short locator ONLY. **Independent witness/photo certification:** NO.

### Working English (first pass)

What defines a human as a rational animal is the *meaning* of that expression, not merely the pronunciation. Otherwise someone ignorant of the language's word assignments would grasp the definition simply on hearing the words. Similarly, what demonstrates the world's origination is the understood content of the premises “the world changes” and “whatever changes is originated,” not their sounds.

### Editorial qualifications

Conceptual content versus linguistic vehicle.

## Signification note 6 — COMPLETE FIRST-PASS English

**ID:** `HWN-DALALA-006` · **Reported voice:** Shaykh ʿAbd al-Raḥīm · **Original printed source:** [p.189](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/189?lang=ar) · **Arabic:** short locator ONLY. **Independent witness/photo certification:** NO.

### Short Arabic locator (NOT entire note)

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

الدلالة الالتزامية مهجورة في الحدود التامة

</div>

### Working English (first pass)

Logic needs an account of signification because technical rules concerning definitions distinguish whole, partial and implicative meanings. In a complete essential definition, an external implication cannot replace the defining content; the use of constituent meanings likewise has restrictions. The five predicables belong to the study of simple meanings, requiring a prior distinction between simple and compound expressions. Figurative and equivocal expressions are normally avoided in definitions unless a clarifying indicator is present. Univocal and gradated terms also matter: some allow a gradated expression to function as a genus while others deny it. These controversies explain the conventional introductory study of language.

### Editorial qualifications

First-pass paraphrase of the note's instructional dependency chain, not certification of every reported technical opinion.

## Signification note 12 — PARTIAL — incomplete source translation

**ID:** `HWN-DALALA-012` · **Reported voice:** Shaykh ʿAbd al-Raḥīm, quoting al-Sayyid al-Sharīf · **Original printed source:** [p.189](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/189?lang=ar) · **Arabic:** short locator ONLY. **Independent witness/photo certification:** NO.

### Short Arabic locator (NOT entire note)

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

وإن كان بسبب اقتضاء الطبع

</div>

### Working English (partial)

Natural verbal signification may be attributed to the speaker's disposition to produce a sound when a state occurs, to a state that naturally calls forth that sound, or to the hearer's instinctive understanding of it without knowing a convention. Al-Sayyid al-Sharīf objects that the hearer-focused account does not cleanly distinguish natural from rational indication, because neither requires knowledge of a word assignment. A defensible definition therefore needs an account of the nature producing or prompting the sign, not merely the listener's arrival at a meaning.

### Editorial qualifications

Argument includes disputed alternatives; the passage's wording and attribution need a second Arabic translation pass.

## Signification note 14 — PARTIAL — incomplete source translation

**ID:** `HWN-DALALA-014` · **Reported voice:** Shaykh ʿAbd al-Raḥīm · **Original printed source:** [p.190](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/190?lang=ar) · **Arabic:** short locator ONLY. **Independent witness/photo certification:** NO.

### Short Arabic locator (NOT entire note)

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله ودلالة سرعة النبض على الحمى

</div>

### Working English (partial)

A rapid pulse indicating fever is proposed as *nonverbal natural signification*. Some earlier classifications allowed only conventional and rational forms of nonverbal indication. The writer defends an additional natural class through facial flushing, paling from fear, bodily movement in response to music, and characteristic movements of animals. The same sign can also support a rational inference if a necessary causal link is known. But a person can recognise the sign through habituated experience of nature without knowing such a link. Thus the classification depends in part on *why the observer moves from sign to signified*, not solely on the sign's physical shape.

### Editorial qualifications

A complex overlap of causal and habituated indication; source expressly challenges a five-type-only taxonomy.

## Signification note 15 — PARTIAL — incomplete source translation

**ID:** `HWN-DALALA-015` · **Reported voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī, reporting al-Sayyid al-Sharīf · **Original printed source:** [p.190](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/190?lang=ar) · [p.191](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/191?lang=ar) · **Arabic:** short locator ONLY. **Independent witness/photo certification:** NO.

### Short Arabic locator (NOT entire note)

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

كدلالة لفظ ديز المسموع من وراء الجدار

</div>

### Working English (partial)

The meaningless sound *dīz* heard from behind a wall indicates the existence of a speaker by rational inference, not through a convention that assigns it a lexical meaning. A meaningful utterance could likewise indicate the speaker, but a beginner might confuse that rational inference with the word's conventional signification. The wall makes the point vivid: the speaker is not directly seen. Hearing a visible speaker also gives rational evidence of that person, but it is overshadowed by simultaneous visual perception. The presence of direct sight does not logically remove the sound's evidential function.

### Editorial qualifications

Historical example includes a cross-page discussion of visibility, sound and rational inference; English rendered as an analysis, not a fully collation-certified source note.

## Signification note 18 — PARTIAL — incomplete source translation

**ID:** `HWN-DALALA-018` · **Reported voice:** Shaykh ʿAbd al-Raḥīm, quoting Quṭb al-Dīn · **Original printed source:** [p.191](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/191?lang=ar) · **Arabic:** short locator ONLY. **Independent witness/photo certification:** NO.

### Short Arabic locator (NOT entire note)

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله وهي تنقسم

</div>

### Working English (partial)

A definition of conventional verbal signification is proposed: when a sound is uttered, its assigned meaning is understood *because the listener knows the assignment*. The last condition excludes natural signs, whose comprehension does not depend on learned convention, and rational signs such as hearing a person behind a wall, which indicates a speaker even to someone who does not know the word's meaning. The author treats the relevant logical signification as a general relation, not a meaning occasionally inferred from a special context. Grammarians and jurists, by contrast, may also attend to context-dependent ordinary usage. The gloss explains that saying “knowledge of its assignment” avoids limiting the definition to whole-meaning signification alone.

### Editorial qualifications

Quṭb al-Dīn's quoted explanation crosses several technical definitions; a compact rendering, not full independently checked translation.
