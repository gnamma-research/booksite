## Introduction note 1 — first-pass English complete

**Stable ID:** `HWN-INTRO-001` · **Qum source:** [p.165](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/165?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī · **Status:** Arabic *locator only*, source photograph NOT independently checked.

### Short Arabic locator — NOT full transcription

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله أي هذه مقدمة

</div>

### Working English — first pass

On the explanation “that is, this is an introduction”: the word *muqaddima* is a predicate of an omitted demonstrative subject, “this.” This follows the basic order that the subject is definite and the predicate indefinite. Others instead make “an introduction” the subject, with an omitted predicate: “an introduction [is concerned] with defining logic, explaining the need for it, and stating its subject.” An objection arises that a completely indefinite noun cannot ordinarily serve as the subject of a statement. Several replies are given. One can treat the indefiniteness as specially qualified by the tanwīn of magnification—signalling the many benefits of this introduction—or of diminution—signalling the brevity of its expressions. Alternatively place the omitted predicate before it: “In the definition of logic, the need for it, and its subject, there is an introduction,” like “In the house is a man.” A more general response judges the admissibility of an indefinite subject by whether the sentence conveys information, not by a fixed list of formal qualifications. Thus “A tree prostrated” and “A star fell just now” can inform the hearer, whereas a bare “A man is standing” may lack informative force in the relevant context.

A further objection targets the original analysis “this is an introduction.” Does the demonstrative not designate the three matters mentioned—the definition, need, and subject—whereas the introduction is not identical with those matters but is an exposition *about* them? Even granting this distinction, one need not take “this” to point to the three items themselves; it can point instead to the specifically arranged words and meanings that introduce them. The same response is available to those who understand the suppressed subject as “these matters.”

### Editorial note

Entire p165 numbered note1 in first pass; the objection concerns reference and identity, not just definite-vs-indefinite forms.

## Introduction note 2 — PARTIAL; source/argument incomplete

**Stable ID:** `HWN-INTRO-002` · **Qum source:** [p.165](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/165?lang=ar) · [p.166](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/166?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī, with separate reported ʿAbd al-Raḥīm comment · **Status:** Arabic *locator only*, source photograph NOT independently checked.

### Short Arabic locator — NOT full transcription

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله يتبين فيها أمور ثلاثة

</div>

### Working English — partial

The words “three matters are explained in it” raise a problem about literal containment. The treatment is comparable to the prior discussion of “the first division is in logic”: a thing and what is contained in it must not be identical in precisely the same respect. One proposal, connected with al-Taftāzānī's commentary on the *Talkhīṣ*, distinguishes the *introduction to a science* from the *introduction to a book*. The former comprises what must be known in order to embark intelligently on the discipline—its definition, end and subject. The latter is the portion of a writer's discourse placed ahead of the principal subject because it is connected to and useful for that subject, whether or not the science strictly depends on it. The former could then be said to contain the latter, avoiding literal self-containment. Another proposal considers the three preliminary matters themselves as parts of the introduction, as one says that a particular inquiry occurs “in” a particular chapter.

A reported comment from ʿAbd al-Raḥīm challenges the supposition that “introduction to a book” is necessarily a new technical expression unsupported by usage. Writers regularly name their leading portions introductions, chapters, sections, *namṭ*, *tanbīh* or *ishāra*, and such designations cannot be confined to one prescribed vocabulary. The source compares an introduction with the advance detachment of an army, from which “what goes before” can be extended to a beginning. A further response by Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī distinguishes two aspects: the referent of “introduction” in the two analyses (science vs book), and whether containment applies separately to each of the three topics or to the topics taken collectively.

The whole p165–166 dossier combines signed prose by different voices; this note is an analytic first pass, NOT full literal transcription of each quote. Keep incomplete pending clause/signature collation.

## Introduction note 7 — PARTIAL; source/argument incomplete

**Stable ID:** `HWN-INTRO-007` · **Qum source:** [p.166](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/166?lang=ar) · [p.167](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/167?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** al-Taqrīb p.14, quoting al-Khaṭṭāʾī; separate ʿAbd al-Raḥīm and Muḥammad ʿAlī comments · **Status:** Arabic *locator only*, source photograph NOT independently checked.

### Short Arabic locator — NOT full transcription

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

وهي مأخوذة من مقدمة الجيش

</div>

### Working English — partial

On “it is derived from the vanguard of an army”: *muqaddima* is the group that proceeds before the rest of the army. Transferring the term to an introductory section of a book may be a new technical assignment, making it literally applicable in that convention, or a metaphor, like calling a brave man a lion. The likeness is that both are portions of a larger whole placed before the remainder.

Al-Khaṭṭāʾī suggests another possibility: the term was originally an adjective whose qualified noun was omitted, then used for a group of meanings or words which precedes the science or the rest of the book. The feminine ending may signal the transfer from adjectival to nominal use, or correspond to an implicitly feminine noun such as *ṭāʾifa* (“group”). Whether the resulting use is literal or figurative depends on whether the adjective is considered a general property of being advanced or a noun conventionally assigned to a specific kind of advance detachment.

The following, separately introduced ʿAbd al-Raḥīm passage compares the meanings of *muqaddima* with kasra or fatḥa on the dāl. The common pronunciation with kasra looks like an active participle; however the author's act of putting a section first might suggest a passive participle. A response takes the base verb in an intransitive sense, “to go forward,” making the preceding material properly active in advancing; another considers the active form as indicating that the section, by enabling insight, effectively puts itself forward. That answer faces an objection from the usual rule that the intensive causative form is transitive, though the rule may be predominant rather than without exceptions.

### Editorial note

Substantial cross-page translation selection; the source includes multiple separately introduced voices and examples requiring full line-by-line collation, so remain partial.

## Introduction note 8 — first-pass English complete

**Stable ID:** `HWN-INTRO-008` · **Qum source:** [p.167](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/167?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** Shaykh ʿAbd al-Raḥīm · **Status:** Arabic *locator only*, source photograph NOT independently checked.

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

إشارة إلى أن المقدمة يطلق على معنيين آخرين

### Working English — first pass

The commentator signals that *muqaddima* also has two other uses. First, it is a proposition made a component of a syllogism or other proof. Second, it is something on which the validity of an argument depends—for example, in the first syllogistic figure, the minor premise's being affirmative and the major premise's being universal. The second use is broader than the first, since it includes conditions of an inference as well as its constituent propositions.

### Editorial note

The two logical senses of premise are distinct from the earlier book/science introduction distinction.

**Stable ID:** `HWN-INTRO-010` · **Qum source:** [p.167](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/167?lang=ar) · [p.168](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/168?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī, with separately cited al-Taqrīb · **Status:** Arabic *locator only*, source photograph NOT independently checked.

### Working English — partial

If “book” means its expressions, its “introduction” is a portion of the book's words placed before the principal discourse. If “book” refers to the meanings signified, the introduction may denote knowledge on which the science depends. The source criticizes proliferating word–meaning distinctions which obscure the genuine difference between the preliminary material necessary for a science and a literary introduction which assists comprehension without constituting that discipline.

The glossator rejects making the introduction to a science depend entirely on the author's act of ordering words. Some preliminary investigations, including the definition of a science, its subject and its need or purpose, are intellectual foundations of the discipline; introductory discussion of technical terminology can instead be indispensable merely to understanding a particular book's expressions. The long continuation is concerned with whether a philosophical introduction must itself be a constituent of the science and whether distinguishing the two introductions produces a useful explanation.

## Introduction note 11 — first-pass English complete

**Stable ID:** `HWN-INTRO-011` · **Qum source:** [p.168](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/168?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī (after al-Taqrīb citation) · **Status:** Arabic *locator only*, source photograph NOT independently checked.

### Short Arabic locator — NOT full transcription

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

أي من الكلام اللفظي كما هو ظاهر

</div>

### Working English — first pass

This means “from verbal discourse,” as is clear from the context. A *ṭāʾifa* is a group or portion. Some authorities put its minimum at three or four, others at two or three. Its form is among adjectives that have come to be used substantively, as though the original expression were “a group which separates itself from something.”

It is reported from Mujāhid and Ibn ʿAbbās, however, that a *ṭāʾifa* may denote even one person or more. This accords with al-Jawharī's general explanation of it as a “piece of something.” Qurʾān 9:122, which speaks of a party going forth from every group to acquire religious understanding, is interpreted as allowing a single person, or a pair, to comprise the party. The plural pronoun in the following words does not require each individual *ṭāʾifa* to contain a plurality; it can refer to the multiple parties collectively conveyed by the context.

## Introduction note 12 — first-pass English complete

**Stable ID:** `HWN-INTRO-012` · **Qum source:** [p.168](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/168?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī · **Status:** Arabic *locator only*, source photograph NOT independently checked.

### Short Arabic locator — NOT full transcription

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

لا يخفى أن ارتباط المقصود إنما هو بمعاني تلك الطائفة

</div>

### Working English — first pass

It is obvious that the principal topic is connected to the *meanings* conveyed by that group of expressions, not to the expressions considered merely as sounds or letters. Likewise, what is beneficial for the subject is the meanings, not the words simply as words. Hence one may supply an omitted genitive: “because the purpose is connected with *their meanings*, and *their meanings* are beneficial to it.”

Alternatively no omission is needed. Since expressions are the means through which knowledge is communicated and received, the connection and benefit are attributed to them figuratively. On the first analysis there is figurative omission (*majāz al-ḥadhf*), and on the second there is figurative attribution (*majāz fī al-isnād*).

### Editorial note

Clearly distinguishes ellipsis from attribution to instruments of teaching.

The author says “An introduction concerning the definition of logic, the need for it and its subject.” Yet if the *introduction* is itself simply those three matters, how can one say the three matters are *in* it without making container and contained identical?

## Introduction note 19 — PARTIAL; source/argument incomplete

**Stable ID:** `HWN-INTRO-019` · **Qum source:** [p.169](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/169?lang=ar) · [p.170](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/170?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī, following separate quoted al-Taqrīb discussion · **Status:** Arabic *locator only*, source photograph NOT independently checked.

### Short Arabic locator — NOT full transcription

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله والمصنف لم يتعرض بتعريفه

</div>

### Working English — partial

Why did Taftāzānī divide knowledge into conception and assent before defining knowledge itself? Division is a judgment about a thing; one might expect the thing to be understood before judgments are made about it. Three explanations are offered. First, a preliminary conception “in some respect” suffices for division: one need only recognise the familiar use of “knowledge” in the discipline. Grammarians similarly divide an exception into connected and disconnected kinds without first supplying an exhaustive definition of the exceptional term. Second, knowledge's definition is already familiar, so the author omits it for brevity. Third, the concept of knowledge is self-evident through experience, as al-Rāzī is reported to hold. Knowledge is among the qualities apprehended by introspection, like hunger, thirst and satiety.

A further argument appeals to ordinary speakers: even people untrained in logical definition, when asked “Do you know this?”, readily answer yes or no. They must therefore grasp the concept of knowledge well enough to understand the question. The glossator objects that neither introspection nor such answers proves that the *word* ʿilm has a self-evident lexical signification. One may immediately encounter an instance or grasp the general concept while still requiring an explanation that this word has been assigned to it.

An attempted defence distinguishes real definition from verbal explanation. The cited arguments might establish that one does not require a real definition of knowledge, while leaving lexical explanation open. The objection to the common-speaker argument also turns on whether the listener already knows the word's conventional assignment. If he does, he may answer without knowing the full real nature; if he does not, he cannot grasp the question merely by hearing it. The author later recognises that the arguments were perhaps directed only toward the impossibility or unnecessary character of a *real* definition, not a verbal gloss.

A further objection says knowledge cannot be defined at all: we know everything by knowledge, so knowing knowledge through something else would be circular. One answer is that knowing an object does not require explicit knowledge of the linguistic assignment of ʿilm. ### Working English — partial

The assertion that Taftāzānī chose the philosophers' position requires distinguishing two different questions. The first concerns the *nature of assent* (*taṣdīq*). For the philosophers it is the act of judgment, an intellectual apprehension accompanied by assent toward a complete predicative relation. It differs from simple conception in kind, not merely in having a different object. For al-Rāzī and his followers it is a compound of four constituents—conception of subject, predicate and relation, together with the act of judgment. Conceiving the three is a prerequisite of simple assent on the first theory; it is a *part* of assent on the second.

The distinction is relevant to logic's purpose. Conceived unknowns are acquired through an explanatory statement (*qawl shāriḥ*), whereas the act of assent is acquired through a proof (*ḥujja*). If assent is a simple intellectual act, these two kinds of acquired knowledge can be distinguished according to their acquisition. A composite containing several conceptual elements acquired in one way and an additional judgment acquired in another does not as naturally admit one distinctive procedure of acquisition.

Some attempt to reconcile the views by saying that the same assent is simple when considered as a whole and composite upon analysis into its presupposed contents. But al-Rāzī expressly calls assent the aggregate of conceptions and judgment, and contrasts composite with simple in *al-Mulakhkhaṣ*. Treating his stated composition as merely a different way of viewing a simple act obscures that explicit disagreement.

The second question concerns the *constituents of a proposition*. The older philosophers are described as counting three: subject, predicate and complete affirmative or negative predicative relation. Later philosophers count four by adding the obtaining or nonobtaining of the positive restrictive relation, motivated by cases of doubt. One can conceive a relation without judging whether it obtains; the cognition of obtaining/nonobtaining appears to be an additional element.

An opposing account replies that the same relation can be conceived without assent and apprehended with assent, without multiplying components. Some later writers distinguish the two considerations of the same relation—conceived and judged—and explain the different counts as analytic rather than numerical. The author of *Sharḥ al-Maṭāliʿ* is cited for allowing four elements upon detailed analysis. Taftāzānī's wording, however, describes assent to *the complete predicative relation*, and his later treatment of categorical propositions appears to require three formal parts. The source considers whether a suppressed “occurrence of” could be supplied to restore four parts, but rejects that as an interpretation contrary to his explicit later exposition.

The surface wording could seem to mean that conception and assent divide necessity and acquisition, making the first pair the *dividers* and the second pair what is *divided*. The principal purpose, however, is to show that each of conception and assent is divisible into immediate and acquired kinds. This can be understood implicitly in the stated division. If both conception and assent each take shares from the two classes, each must have an immediate kind and an acquired kind, just as a noun divides into definite/indefinite or other opposing grammatical classifications.

The main assertion is thus conveyed by implication (*iltizām*) and by indirect expression (*kināya*), rather than explicitly saying that conception and assent are each of two kinds. The commentator calls an indirect expression better and more eloquent than a bare statement because the conclusion is approached through something entailing it: in favourable cases, the existence of a necessary concomitant provides evidence for the intended meaning. One must nevertheless preserve the difference between an implication that actually follows and a rhetorical interpretation merely alleged to follow.

### Editorial note

The note crosses Q175–176; this is its first-pass full argumentative summary, not independently scanned.

### Working English — first pass

Two explanations of *kināya* were already contrasted in the book's preface: al-Sakkākī and his followers define it as mentioning what necessarily accompanies something while intending the thing accompanied, with the literal meaning still potentially intended; another group reverses that relation. The present construction can support either explanation, because the two relevant divisions entail one another when the terms are understood correctly.

The glossator criticizes an interpretation which reduces the matter to simply reversing the order of a sentence, as in the proverbial “I presented the she-camel to the pool” instead of “I presented the pool to the she-camel.” Reversal is not in principle incompatible with indirect expression; one can use a reversal *and* speak by kināya. The critic might have intended indirect expression effected by a reversal, but that is not the same as defining kināya as reversal itself.

### Editorial note

A distinction between structural inversion and indirect predication; explicitly preserves possibility of both.

## Introduction note 40 — first-pass English complete

**Stable ID:** `HWN-INTRO-040` · **Qum source:** [p.176](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/176?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī · **Status:** Arabic *locator only*, source photograph NOT independently checked.

### Short Arabic locator — NOT full transcription

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله وهي أبلغ وأحسن من التصريح

</div>

### Working English — first pass

Why is an indirect expression said to be more eloquent and beautiful than explicit speech? One answer likens it to establishing a claim by evidence. From the existence of the thing accompanied (*malzūm*) one may infer its necessary concomitant (*lāzim*), because the one cannot exist without the other. That explanation works readily for the account of kināya in which the thing accompanied is mentioned and the concomitant intended.

For al-Sakkākī's direction of inference—from the concomitant to the thing it accompanies—the explanation is less straightforward. A concomitant may be more general than the thing for which it is a concomitant, and the existence of what is general does not prove a specific instance. The argument can be repaired if the concomitant and its referent are coextensive or otherwise mutually entailing. Al-Sakkākī is cited as recognising the dependence of the inference on that equality. The full theory of rhetorical evidence would require more discussion than this note permits.

### Editorial note

A rigorous scope qualification: a necessary consequence need not be convertible unless coextensive.

## Introduction note 42 — PARTIAL; source/argument incomplete

**Stable ID:** `HWN-INTRO-042` · **Qum source:** [p.176](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/176?lang=ar) · [p.177](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/177?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī · **Status:** Arabic *locator only*, source photograph NOT independently checked.

### Short Arabic locator — NOT full transcription

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله كما ارتكبه القوم

</div>

### Working English — partial

The usual proof that some conceptions and assents are immediate and others theoretical argues by excluding two extremes. They cannot **all** be immediate, because in fact some objects of conception and some matters of judgment require deliberate inquiry. Nor can they **all** be theoretical, because if every cognition required a prior cognition, the acquisition of knowledge would be trapped in circular dependence or endless regress.

If a cognition A requires B, B requires C and C in turn requires A, the supposed acquisition of A would require A itself to precede its own acquisition—an impossibility. The infinite regress also seems to demand a completion of infinitely many prerequisites before the present cognition can occur. The glossator develops the distinctions between circularity and regress and the assumptions behind the impossibility of each.

An objection raises a possible eternal history of intellect: an infinite series of preparatory cognitions might be spread over an infinitely long past, with each preparing the next rather than being jointly present at one time. A response says this is unavailable on the theological premises which reject the eternity of souls and the world; the argument is therefore conditional upon those disputed premises and does not by itself prove that every kind of infinite causal sequence is impossible.

A final logical caution is important. The two types of cognition may not be absolutely isolated. If a conception could be acquired by an earlier assent or an assent by an earlier conception, the claim that **every conception** is theoretical might terminate in a necessary assent, and the claim that **every assent** is theoretical might terminate in a necessary conception. The standard proof therefore requires an additional premise restricting the cross-type acquisition routes. Without it, the proposed dilemma does not independently establish the twofold division *within each type*.

### Editorial note

Extensive source Q176→177 with multiple objections and philosophical premises. Significant English analytic translation, but not fully clause-paired; remains PARTIAL.

## Introduction note 43 — first-pass English complete

**Stable ID:** `HWN-INTRO-043` · **Qum source:** [p.177](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/177?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** al-Taqrīb p.17 (quoted) · **Status:** Arabic *locator only*, source photograph NOT independently checked.

قوله وذلك أي ودليل بداهتها

### Working English — first pass

The point is to establish that immediate cognitions really occur. Reality is often disclosed to us spontaneously through ordinary dealings with the world. We encounter heat without seeking to know it, conceive what heat is, and learn of fire; we then judge that fire is hot, without having first constructed a deliberate proof. Thus a conception and a judgment can arise without paid effort or systematic inquiry. Thousands of similar everyday examples are available.

Theoretical cognition is equally evident: the very need for canons of inference and methods of evaluating proofs is to acquire and establish conclusions that do not simply arrive of themselves.

### Working English — first pass

By the conception of heat and cold is meant the intellect's grasp of the *universal concept*, acquired by means of particular sensations of heat and cold, not those particular sensations themselves. Heat itself can be present in the bodily organ which bears the power of touch. There is no inconsistency between calling these concepts immediate and later giving the properties or characteristics of heat and cold in a scientific discussion. Historical distinction between the causal occasion in sensation and the universal concept abstracted by intellect.

## Introduction note 46 — first-pass English complete

**Stable ID:** `HWN-INTRO-046` · **Qum source:** [p.178](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/178?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī · **Status:** Arabic *locator only*, source photograph NOT independently checked.

اعلم أن ضرورية التصديق ونظريته عند الحكماء

### Working English — first pass

For the philosophers, assent is necessary or theoretical with respect to the act of judgment alone; they do not include the prior conceptions of the two terms and their relation in this classification. For al-Rāzī, by contrast, assent is a compound of four elements. A change in what counts as the *thing classified* changes the extension of its necessary and theoretical species.

## Introduction note 47 — first-pass English complete

**Stable ID:** `HWN-INTRO-047` · **Qum source:** [p.178](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/178?lang=ar) · **Reported voice:** al-Taqrīb p.17 (quoted) · **Status:** Arabic *locator only*, source photograph NOT independently checked.

### Short Arabic locator — NOT full transcription

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

هو ملاحظة المعقول لتحصيل المجهول

</div>

### Working English — first pass

The definition of inquiry (*naẓar*) as “considering an intelligible to obtain an unknown” would, the critic argues, be clearer if it said “considering something known to obtain something unknown.” The latter immediately conveys the thought that an item already disclosed is used to discover something concealed. The phrase “the intelligible” instead suggests what has already been apprehended by the rational faculty, without making the inferential transition as transparent.

The principal commentator gives several reasons for choosing “intelligible.” First, “knowledge” (*ʿilm*) is used in multiple senses, such as a form in the intellect, assent and certainty, and equivocal language in a definition is undesirable. The critic regards the list as verbal proliferation: knowledge properly refers to the disclosure of reality. Second, reasoning proceeds in intelligibles, especially universals, not bare particulars; an isolated particular is too determined to serve as a universal mediating term by which another is inferred. Third, the term maintains balanced rhyme with “the unknown.”

The critic suspects that the third reason was the chief motive. He adds that a particular is neither a suitable general means of acquiring a distinct particular nor usually something acquired by using another particular as a middle, because the individuating conditions separate them. The argument concerns the logical role of middle terms, not the claim that particular events can never be known by signs in ordinary life.

### Editorial note

Rendered substance of the quoted al-Taqrīb note; traditional inference distinction is source-attributed.
