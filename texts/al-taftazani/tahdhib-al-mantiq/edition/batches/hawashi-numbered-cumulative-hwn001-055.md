### Working English — INCOMPLETE

One explanation given for writing the basmala without the alif expected by ordinary orthography is the frequency with which the formula is used; the elongated bāʾ is said to compensate graphically for the omission. A report recounts the gradual development of the introductory formula: an earlier expression attributed to Quraysh; then the formula invoking God's name; then adding al-Raḥmān; and finally the full formula with al-Raḥīm. The note offers alternative syntactic expansions: “I begin in the name of God,” “my beginning is in the name of God,” or an understood imperative “Begin” or “Say.” These alternatives change whether the omitted governing expression is verbal, nominal, or understood as an implied predicate. It also reports rival etymologies of ism, either from elevation (sumuww) or from a mark/sign (sima). The bracketed passages in the Qum digital text explicitly reference later authors, including Muṭahharī and al-Ṣāfī, and require separate text-historical treatment rather than attribution to an early glossator.

## Note 2 — Complete working first pass

**Printed location:** [Qum p.127](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/127) · [Qum p.128](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/128)

**Photo/witness certification:** NO

### Arabic locator lemma

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قول المصنف الحمد لله

</div>

### Working English translation

On the author's words “Praise belongs to God”: he changed from a verbal sentence to a nominal one to signify permanence and continuance, as befits discourse about the knowing Sovereign. Praise has been placed first because of the special attention it receives in the context, even though God is more important in Himself. Priority determined by a particular circumstance outweighs essential priority in constructing this sentence. The Qurʾānic example in which “to God” is placed before “praise” differs: there the principal intention is to affirm the exclusivity of praise to God, not merely to establish that He is praised.

An objection arises: al-Zamakhsharī has stated that something is called “advanced” or “delayed” only when its position has been changed, not when it has remained in its original place. How, then, can one call the opening word ḥamd advanced?

I reply: the author answers this in his commentary on the Talkhīṣ by distinguishing two kinds of preposing. First, there is preposing with the intention that the word has an underlying later position, as when the predicate precedes its subject. Second, there is preposing without any such intended later position, as when an expression is put first as the subject of a nominal sentence. Thus one may take the noun Zayd, place it before the verb and make it a subject—“Zayd rose”—or put it after the verb and make it the agent—“Rose Zayd.” The advancement of ḥamd is of the second kind, whereas the remark by the author of al-Kashshāf concerns the first. There is no contradiction. Al-Zamakhsharī's own discourse repeatedly calls the first kind “preposing.”

One can also propose a different derivation. The original sentence was “I praise God with praise” (aḥmadu llāha ḥamdan). For the purpose already mentioned, the verbal noun was changed from accusative to nominative, and then ḥamd was placed before the prepositional complement. On that analysis it falls under the first kind of preposing, and no difficulty arises in any event. Reflect.

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

الظرف اما لغو متعلق بافتتح

</div>

### Working English translation

The prepositional phrase may be an ordinary adjunct governed by “he opened”: he opened his book with praise of God after first seeking blessing through the basmala. Alternatively, it may be a predicative adjunct depending on an omitted “while accompanied by,” with the complement of “opened” also suppressed: he opened his book with the basmala while accompanying it thereafter with praise of God. On this second account, repeating “the basmala” places an explicit noun where a pronoun could have stood. Under the first construal, “the best of speech” means the best speech *other than* the basmala; under the second, the best without qualification. The two constructions also allow different introductory reports to be invoked: one enjoins praise and the other the naming of God. Under the first, saying that one opened with praise even though praise follows the basmala either focuses on the less obvious fact and omits the obvious, or signals that praise can properly be called an opening despite coming second. Thus the introductory traditions need not contradict one another. The commentator has given a general indication of the distinction that he will subsequently unpack when he asks how the reports concerning the opening can be reconciled. Explication after an initial allusion is more agreeable and effective for the hearer.

## Note 8 — Complete working first pass

**Printed location:** [Qum p.128](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/128) · [Qum p.129](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/129)  
**Source voice:** Muḥammad ʿAlī (signed marginal gloss; sectarian claim is authorial, not editorial)  
**Arabic:** SHORT LOCATOR ONLY — full Arabic still to be transcribed  
**Photo/witness certification:** NO

### Arabic locator lemma

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

عطف على الضمير المجرور في عليه

</div>

### Working English translation

The expression is coordinated with the pronoun governed by the preposition in “upon him” (ʿalayhi), without repeating that preposition. This is permitted by the Kufan grammarians, and also by al-Akhfash, Yūnus and Ibn Mālik: according to them it is not obligatory to repeat the governing preposition. The Basrans, however, require its repetition. They argue that the attachment of a pronoun in the genitive to its preposition is even tighter than the attachment of a nominative pronoun to its verbal governor. Just as coordination with the latter is forbidden, since the pronoun has become virtually part of the word, so they forbid it here.

The marginal commentator then introduces a contentious sectarian explanation of why the author might have omitted the additional word ʿalā (“upon”). He suggests that the omission was meant to affront the ʿāmma, because a report had circulated that the khāṣṣa omit ʿalā when coordinating mention of the Prophet's family with mention of the Prophet. According to the report ascribed to the Prophet, whoever separates him from his family “by ʿAlī/ʿalā” will not obtain his intercession. The commentator says that the opposing party regards the insertion of ʿalā as objectionable.

But, he continues, this would be a false allegation against the khāṣṣa, because ʿalā does occur repeatedly when the family are coordinated with the Prophet in supplications transmitted from their Imams. Whoever examines those prayers can see this.

He therefore offers two possibilities. Either the alleged report is fabricated and must be rejected; or its disputed words are to be vocalised with a kasra under the lām and a doubled yāʾ, giving the proper name ʿAlī rather than the preposition ʿalā. On that second construal the meaning would be: whoever separates the Prophet from members of his family, from al-Ḥasan and al-Ḥusayn through the awaited Ḥujja, *by invoking ʿAlī b. Abī Ṭālib*—that is, by declaring them not to be the Prophet's family because they descend through a daughter—would be denied intercession. The commentator adduces a line of verse to express the genealogical objection: “The sons of our sons are our sons; our daughters' sons belong to men more remote.” If one reasoned that way, one would call those descendants the family of ʿAlī rather than the family of the Prophet. Reflect.

These are the speaker's grammatical, transmitted, and confessional claims. They do not constitute independent authentication of the alleged prophetic report, and the translation does not endorse the polemical inference.

## Note 9 — Complete working first pass

**Printed location:** [Qum p.129](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/129) · [Qum p.130](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/130) · [Qum p.131](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/131)  
**Source voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī (signed at Q131)  
**Arabic:** SHORT LOCATOR ONLY — full Arabic still to be transcribed  
**Photo/witness certification:** NO

### Arabic locator lemma

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

في حديث الابتداء بالتسمية والتحميد اشكالين مشهورين

</div>

### Working English translation

On the words “If you ask, the report concerning the beginning is transmitted ...”: know that two familiar difficulties arise from the traditions commanding that important undertakings begin with the divine name and with praise.

The first difficulty is that the basmala and the praise formula are themselves important matters. The expression “a matter of consequence” (dhū bāl) can mean something of standing or status, as in Qurʾānic questions about former generations and the women who cut their hands; alternatively bāl can be associated with the heart, so that the term means whatever occurs to and occupies one's attention. If the rule includes either introductory formula itself, that formula must begin with another formula of the same kind; the second must begin with a third, and so forth. The procedure must either return to what started it, producing circularity, or continue without end, producing an infinite regress. Requiring one formula to start with the other creates the same pair of alternatives.

The second difficulty is that applying one of the two traditions seems to cancel the other. Either one begins with the basmala, thereby omitting the initial praise commanded by the other report, or begins with praise, omitting the initial naming of God. This is the difficulty to which the author is presently referring.

One answer to the first difficulty is that everything that exists through another ultimately depends on what exists through itself. This is illustrated by the transmitted saying about the divine will—God created the will through itself and other things through the will—and by the psychological illustration that the things one brings about are produced through one's will whereas the will itself arises without another prior willing, according to the view of the upholders of divine justice. Similarly, the other affairs begin with the basmala or praise, while the opening formulas begin through themselves.

Another answer is that the general statement is restricted by a contextual indication: to begin *one thing with another* already implies a distinction between them. The same restriction is invoked when it is said that God creates every thing and that every thing depends on God: the intended meaning is everything other than God. It is common to restrict a general expression, hence the saying that scarcely any general statement escapes qualification. These two answers, the glossator observes, remove only the first of the alleged consequences; the other difficulty still remains.

The preferable answer construes “a matter of consequence” in the two reports not as everything that is objectively important, but as the act or purpose intended for its own sake. Neither introductory formula is the independent undertaking intended for its own sake, although both are important in reality. Thus neither falls under the subject of the command in the problematic sense.

As for the second difficulty, the commentator's answer depends on three kinds of beginning. (1) A **real** beginning precedes without itself being preceded; another definition says that nothing precedes it at all. (2) A **relative** beginning comes before the thing principally intended, even if it comes after some other thing; a broader definition treats it as first in relation to one item though preceded in relation to another. The first definition is narrower. (3) A **customary** beginning is whatever ordinary usage regards as part of the beginning whether or not something precedes it; it can extend from the commencement of composing the book to the moment when the main intended discussion begins. The narrow relative beginning and the customary one may differ only in the standpoint from which we consider them.

Each report admits these three readings, giving nine possible pairings. Three are both sound and counted as relevant; those are the readings invoked by the primary commentator. Three are sound but omitted as not adopted: reading both beginnings relatively; reading the basmala relatively and the praise customarily; and conversely reading the basmala customarily and the praise relatively. The remaining three are unsound: these read the praise as the *real* beginning while interpreting the basmala by any of the three definitions, contrary to the actual custom under consideration.

The grounds of validity and invalidity follow from the definitions, but why should a logically valid pair be counted or left out? The commentator considers two suggested criteria: either one of the beginnings is real, as in the first two recognised possibilities, or both expressions receive a beginning of the same kind, as in the last. The two omitted mixed cases have neither property; however, the excluded relative-relative case plainly satisfies the second. Either it should be counted too or the accepted customary-customary case should also be disallowed. Otherwise the distinction is arbitrary. One might reply only that customary use is common in this discussion whereas specifically relative use is not.

He cautions that being “counted” or “uncounted” presupposes being *valid*. An invalid case cannot become valid just because it happens to share both criteria. Moreover the whole scheme assumes the current convention of placing the basmala before the praise. Reverse the order and both the validity and the relevance of some combinations change. Care is needed not to confuse the cases.

Two further replies to the original difficulty are mentioned. One might say that praise means displaying God's beautiful attributes, whether by the word ḥamd or otherwise, so that the basmala itself has an aspect of praising and satisfies both reports. Or one might take the two reports as jointly prohibiting the absence of *both*: a significant undertaking lacking either the basmala or praise is defective, and a truly initial use of either one suffices. The basmala would still be preferable because it also contains praise and alludes to the assistance of God's supreme name in action.

Both proposals, he objects, strain the apparent wording. The second quite clearly does. The first conflicts with ordinary linguistic usage: when someone utters only the basmala, people do not normally say that he has uttered praise. It is for this reason that the primary commentator did not choose these replies.

### Working English translation

His phrase “for what is beautiful” serves to exclude blame and satire if thanāʾ is understood broadly enough to embrace both commendation and condemnation, as the Qāmūs states. If one instead confines thanāʾ to commendation, the phrase merely makes explicit the intended reality and meaning. An objection may be made: a tyrant might be exalted for unjustly plundering property and killing heroes; is that not praise despite being directed to what is not beautiful? Should “beautiful” not be replaced by “action”? We deny that such commendation is properly called ḥamd. Even if this is conceded, “beautiful” can include what is beautiful in the opinion of the praiser: a person may regard an action as fine even if others—or reality itself—regard it as reprehensible. It can also be beautiful merely in outward appearance.

## Note 12 — Complete working first pass

**Printed location:** [Qum p.131](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/131) · [Qum p.132](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/132)  
**Source voice:** Muḥammad ʿAlī

**Photo/witness certification:** NO

### Arabic locator lemma

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله الاختياري وصف به ليخرج عن الحد المدح

</div>

### Working English translation

Concerning the expression “voluntary”: the qualifier was included so that madḥ (commendation) would fall outside the definition of ḥamd (praise), since commendation extends both to beautiful things that are chosen and to those that are not chosen. One may say, “I commended him for the beauty of his cheek and the elegance of his stature,” whereas one would not normally say, “I praised him [ḥamidtuhu] for that.” This distinction is necessary only if madḥ is broader than ḥamd. If the two are treated as synonymous, as appears from al-Zamakhsharī's formulation “ḥamd and madḥ are two brothers,” the qualifier should instead be abandoned as he abandoned it.

An objection: the author himself leaves out “voluntary” in his commentary on the Talkhīṣ when he says that ḥamd is commendation with the tongue for what is beautiful, whether the excellence be personal virtues (faḍāʾil) or benefits extended to others (fawāḍil). Does he not therefore also regard the terms as synonymous?

I reply: no. He treats “beautiful” as modifying a suppressed noun: the intended expression is “for a beautiful *action*.” That amounts to saying “for a beautiful voluntary thing,” because, as they explicitly explain, an act in the relevant sense is something done by choice.

A further objection comes from kalām. God's eternal attributes are not chosen in the sense of being newly acquired; otherwise their acquisition would entail their origination. Yet those who laud Him for His eternal attributes are, by agreement, said to praise Him. Does the voluntary condition not exclude this?

I reply, assuming that ḥamd in this instance is literal, that the eternal attributes may be treated analogically as voluntary acts insofar as God independently possesses and entails those attributes, as He is independent in His voluntary acts. Alternatively, the praise that appears to be directed to the eternal attributes is in truth directed to the voluntary acts of which those attributes are principles. Thus the grammatical or immediate object of praise and its ultimate object need not be identical. Reflect.

### Working English translation

On the words “Allāh is, according to the sounder view, a proper name”: as human minds have been bewildered by the divine essence and attributes, so they have disputed the word by which He is designated. Is it Arabic, Hebrew, or Syriac? Is it a noun or an adjective? Derived or primitive? A proper name or a general expression? Each group has advanced different proofs. Rather than multiplying reports to the point of weariness, we shall state the preferred account briefly.

The word Allāh originally derives, on this account, from ilāh, on the morphological pattern fiʿāl in the sense of the thing to which an action is done: maʾlūh, the one worshipped, just as kitāb can refer to the thing written. Its original hamza was then dropped, and the definite article was substituted as compensation. On that account it became permissible to address Him by the name directly, without an intervening particle, and often to pronounce its initial hamza as a hamzat qaṭʿ in calling upon Him. This treatment would not ordinarily be permitted for common nouns or relative expressions bearing an ordinary article.

Why, then, is the hamza not pronounced independently in other contexts? The name still retains something of its definite force, for which liaison is suitable. In direct address that aspect recedes, leaving the article as compensatory and permitting the divine name to be uttered with the vocative yā. The special pronunciation also conveys honour from the outset: the word has departed from its former construction, and the article has become almost a constituent part of it.

Al-Jawharī offers an alternative: the hamza was omitted *after* adding the article, simply because of the word's frequency, not because the article compensates for it. People do, after all, say al-ilāh, combining the two; if the article existed solely as compensation, that ought not to be possible. Nor does the independently pronounced hamza in vocative use prove substitution, for it might be a mark of magnification. The present glossator records this argument but says that it calls for examination.

The designation then becomes a proper name of the *particular* necessarily existent being who possesses every attribute of perfection. The author says in his Sharḥ al-Talkhīṣ that those who treat “Allāh” merely as the concept “necessary through itself” or “entitled to worship” miss the point. Both descriptions are universal concepts, even if they happen to have only one actual instance, whereas a proper name has a particular signification.

Consider the confession “There is no god but Allāh.” It is agreed to declare unity without requiring a separately supplied contextual identification. If “Allāh” named merely the concept “rightful object of worship” or “necessary through itself,” rather than the individual being who alone instantiates it, the formulation would not by itself exclude multiple instances: a universal concept, considered in itself, allows plurality. Furthermore ilāh in the confession cannot mean simply “whatever is worshipped,” since many false objects are worshipped and the statement would then be untrue. Nor can “Allāh” simply mean the same universal notion “that which deserves worship,” for the exception would then appear to exclude an item from itself. The proper analysis takes ilāh as “one rightfully deserving worship” and Allāh as the proper name of the actual individual in whom that description is realised. The meaning is that nobody in existence deserves worship except the one who created the world.

A difficulty remains: to assign a proper name to a thing presupposes apprehending that thing, but the divine essence cannot be comprehended. How can the name therefore be a proper name for Him? And if it is a proper name, why is “One” informative when predicated of it in the Qurʾānic words “Say: He is Allāh, One”? A person already knows that an individual denoted by a proper name is one; one does not ordinarily say “Zayd is one.”

The reply to the first difficulty is that God's distinction from all else can certainly be apprehended through His beautiful attributes. What is impossible is grasping the very innermost reality of His essence and attributes. Such exhaustive comprehension is not required when establishing a proper name; it is enough to conceive its bearer as distinct from everything else. Many ordinary proper names are assigned under the same limitation.

Even if one granted that a human being could not institute this proper name, the stronger universal conclusion would not follow. One might hold that God Himself instituted the name and taught it to others through inspiration, revelation, or created sounds, as some investigators maintain concerning the original institutor of language generally or of this name in particular.

Nor is the alleged redundancy of predicating “One” accepted. It depends on interpreting huwa in “Say: He is Allāh, One” as an impersonal pronoun of the affair, with “Allāh is one” forming the explicative subject-predicate sentence. That is not obligatory. The pronoun can refer to the Lord whom the interlocutors had asked the Prophet to describe. The verse then says, in effect: “He—my Lord, to whom I call you—is Allāh, One.” “One” can be taken as an apposition or a second predicate. Even if the impersonal construction be allowed, “One” need not mean merely the numerical count of one rather than two. It can signify that He is not divided into parts, or that He is first, or that He has no equal or counterpart. Under each meaning the predication contributes knowledge.

Al-Taftāzānī says in his commentary on the *Talkhīṣ*: the fact that explanatory *ayy* can explicate a pronoun governed by a preposition without the preposition being repeated, or a directly attached nominative pronoun without reinforcement or separation, supports the majority's position.

The glossator objects: this special grammatical behaviour may indeed support that position, but the very fact that it explains a pronoun supports al-Sakkākī instead. ### Working English translation

The following explains how the verse yields an objection. If *guidance* meant actually bringing someone to the truth, the verse about Thamūd would say, in effect: “We brought them to the truth, but they preferred error to rectitude.” Bringing someone to the goal entails their arrival. The disputed sense of “bringing” is actual attainment, not a mere capacity to bring or some indeterminate relationship to arrival. The first weaker sense would collapse into showing the road; the second does not exclude that sense and is not what the stronger definition intends.

Yet the account of Thamūd's choice presupposes that they had not reached truth in this decisive sense. As the exegetes explain, God made the truth known to them, explained it, and invited them towards it; despite recognising it, they preferred blindness and misguidance to rectitude.

Someone might object that misguidance *can* follow real attainment: we can observe believers later becoming apostates through satanic deception. The glossator responds by distinguishing outwardly recognised belief from a purportedly genuine inner arrival. Although legal rulings of faith applied to the apostate beforehand, the later apostasy is, on his account, evidence that the person had not truly attained the truth inwardly. He invokes the Qurʾānic saying that none can lead astray whom God guides. This is the glossator's theological premise in the argument, not an empirically established law about the psychological impossibility of conversion.

Finally, one cannot simply say that the verse about Thamūd uses “guidance” figuratively in the weaker sense while the literal, originally instituted meaning is actual arrival. The opposite claim is equally available: that the original literal meaning is showing the road, whereas “You do not guide those whom you love” uses the word figuratively in the stronger sense. Without independent evidence, appeal to figurative usage merely shifts the question.

### Working English translation

This means that the Qurʾānic address “You do not guide whom you love” says to Muḥammad: you cannot yourself bring into Islam everyone whose guidance you desire. God, however, can do so, guiding whom He wills and causing that person to enter Islam through His gracious aid—although another view describes this as compulsion. In this passage, then, *guidance* must signify actual conveyance to the goal rather than simply pointing it out. The Prophet's own appointed function included showing the road and summoning people to truth. The Qurʾān says of him, “You surely guide to a straight path”; thus the verse denying that he guides would refute the purely demonstrative definition if the sense were identical.

Yet the glossator adds a caution. Perhaps the verse is expressed in the manner of “You did not throw when you threw, but God threw.” Although the Prophet did perform the outward act of throwing, the effect surpassed human capacity and was therefore attributed decisively to God. ### Working English translation

One may distinguish them by saying that the first means taking someone to the destination and successfully bringing him there, hence is applied particularly to God. The second and third signify indication or showing the road, and can accordingly be applied to the Prophet—as in “You guide to a straight path”—and to the Qurʾān, as in “This Qurʾān guides to what is most upright.” End of his quotation.

I say: this contrast is rejected by “We guided him the road, whether grateful or ungrateful” (76:3). It has been reported from al-Ṣādiq that this means “We made the road known to him, whether he accepted it or left it.” One could not be ungrateful and astray after having already been brought to truth in the relevant stronger sense.

Some have also used “We guided him the two high roads” (90:10) as an objection: this is in a context of divine favour, whereas taking someone all the way to evil could not be a benefit; the two high roads have been interpreted as the paths of good and evil. But this second objection is not conclusive, since the two high roads could instead mean the two breasts, as suggested by the preceding reference to the person's eyes, tongue and lips; a report also gives that interpretation.

The contention that the first grammatical construction is reserved to God is also challenged by reported speech from Abraham, “Follow me; I shall guide you a straight path” (19:43), from the believer who says “Follow me; I shall guide you the way of right direction” (40:38), and even from Pharaoh, “I guide you only to the way of right direction” (40:29). To force all of these into exceptional constructions like “Moses chose his people” would be contrary to the usual reading. Even if such reanalysis were allowed, one could apply it to every example, as al-Zamakhsharī did, leaving no actual use of guidance as successful delivery.

The sounder account, I say, is that guidance means indicating something with kindness, whether the indication actually brings someone to the goal or directs him to what leads there, and whether its second object follows without a preposition or through a preposition. Both preceding objections and the earlier gloss support this broader sense, as do several investigators.

On “it leads its traveller to the desired goal”: that is, it keeps him safe from being misdirected by someone else. As for the expression al-batta (“absolutely,” “decisively”), it is a verbal noun which, some say, the Arabs use only with the definite article; omitting al- would on that account be erroneous. Yet it also occurs without the article. Al-Yazīdī recited in his disputation with al-Kisāʾī: “Among them the best and noblest—or simply the best of them, decidedly—is Abū Bakr.”

The wording of al-Jawharī implies that the definite article is not obligatory. He says that one can say “I shall not do it batta” or “I shall not do it al-batta,” both signifying something to which there is no return. The accusative is that of a verbal noun; others say the article can be omitted only by poetic necessity.

There is also disagreement whether the form admits regular declension or is diptotic by virtue of femininity and proper-noun status. On one analysis it names a special kind of severance wherever it occurs. Hence the absence of tanwīn may be due either to the article or to restriction from ordinary declension.

The author said: Kināya in its linguistic sense is the verbal noun of “I referred indirectly to this by that,” meaning that I refrained from naming it openly. Technically it has two uses. This account follows the apparent position of al-Sakkākī. On “The first is preferable”: interpreting “the middle of the way” as the truth of reality in general is preferable, because it allows the opening to foreshadow both parts of the book—logic and theology. ### Working English translation

On “The first is closer in wording”: attaching the prepositional expression to “made” (jaʿala) is closer in grammar than attaching it to “companion” (rafīq), but it is further away in sense.

It is closer grammatically because it avoids advancing the dependent of a genitive complement before the noun to which that complement belongs. Some permit such preposing in constructions like this, but it is against the basic order, since the dependent normally follows its governor. The alternative also requires supplying a suppressed expression and then interpreting it, another departure from the normal order. Attaching the expression to “companion,” however, necessarily entails one or another of these expedients if the wording is to be syntactically sound.

The first attachment is more remote in meaning because it risks making God's act of bestowal dependent on a purpose obtained from His servants. Some theologians insist that divine acts are not explained by purposes, although they encompass innumerable benefits, ends, wisdoms and goods. The present glossator calls that exclusion weak and unsatisfactory and promises to discuss it near the end of the book; nevertheless, an interpretation acceptable to all disputants is preferable to one rejected by some.

Another objection is that the first attachment would mean, “Praise belongs to God, who made divine enabling the best companion *for our benefit*.” Since the lām marks benefit, the wording might appear irreverent. A third explanation adds that the wording would not specify *whom* divine enabling accompanies: God might make it a companion to another friend or beloved, while we benefit indirectly from that person's possession of it, whereas the intended sense is that enabling accompanies us.

The reply is that, although the grammatical construction could encompass both possibilities, in a context of praising God the first and intended one is naturally understood. One can also take the underlying expression to be “the best companion *for us*,” with the prepositional complement omitted because its sense is already indicated and the omission preserves the rhymed cadence.

### Editorial/source caveat

Important theological contrast: the writer explicitly rejects a categorical ban on divine final causation yet gives it interpretive accommodation. Note crosses Q141–142 and precedes (47).

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله « هي بمعنى الدعاء » : قيل هي في اللّه تعالى الرحمة ومن الملائكة الاستغفار ومن الآدميّين التّضرع يجمعها قوله تعالى : « إِنَّ اللَّهَ وَمَلائِكَتَهُ يُصَلُّونَ عَلَى النَّبِيِّ . . . » . فان قيل : لا يجوز عموم المشترك فكيف استعملت الصلاة في معنييه ؟ قلنا : لا نسلم انها استعملت في كلا معنييه معا ، فان تقدير الآية : ان اللّه يصلى وملائكته يصلون . وانما لم يتعرض لهذا ، لان ما اختاره هو الأقوى للزوم هذا المعنى الاشتراك ، والمجاز خير منه . فان قلت : لو كانت الصلاة بمعنى الدعاء لا يجوز تعديتها بعلى ، لان على يدل على الضرر . قلت : هي هنا مسند إلى اللّه فتجرد عن معنى الطلب فلا يكون بمعنى الدعاء ، على انا لو سلمنا انها مسندة إلى الملائكة أو إلى المؤمنين ، يمكن الجواب : بان القول بان الدعاء إذا تعدى بكلمة على فهو للضرر مبنى على الأغلب الأكثر . فان ادعيتم انها للتضرر ابدا بطريق الايجاب الكلى فذلك وان ادعيتم انها للتضرر بحسب الغالب فذلك لا يصح كبرى للشكل الأول . ولئن أغمضنا عن ذلك أيضا فيمكن الجواب بان التضرر في الصورة المذكورة مخصوص بصريح لفظ الدعاء فلا يتعدى إلى الصلاة وان كان المعنى واحدا .

</div>

On the statement that ṣalāt means supplication: some say it means mercy when predicated of God, a request for forgiveness when predicated of angels, and entreaty when predicated of human beings. These usages are gathered in the verse “God and His angels send ṣalāt upon the Prophet.” One may object that a homonymous term cannot be used in several separate senses at once. The response denies that the occurrence must be one indivisible predication: one may understand “God sends ṣalāt” and “the angels send ṣalāt” as distributed statements. Yet a single broader or figurative account may be preferable to forced homonymy. Another objection says that if ṣalāt means duʿāʾ, its use with ʿalā is inappropriate, since that particle commonly accompanies a prayer against someone. The reply distinguishes the divine case (where there is no needy request) and challenges the illicit conversion of a frequent usage into a universal rule. If adverse sense merely occurs *most* often with duʿāʾ ʿalā, it cannot serve as the universally affirmative major premise of a first-figure syllogism. Even the stronger objection can be restricted to the explicit word duʿāʾ, rather than carried over to ṣalāt despite their contextual similarity.

Concerning the words “When [ṣalāt] is attributed to God, it is stripped of the meaning of a request”: the intention is to deny any conflict between interpreting ṣalāt as duʿāʾ and attributing ṣalāt to God. </div>

### Working English translation

The words “the rank of messengerhood is higher than prophethood” may provide the reason for either of the two explanations previously offered for the author's choice of the attribute “messengerhood.”

As a reason for the first explanation, the attribute is chosen because it includes the other perfections. Messengerhood includes prophethood and adds something to it: on this account a messenger is a prophet to whom a law and scripture are entrusted, while a prophet may either bring such a law or follow the religious law of a previous prophet. Its inclusion of other excellences such as courage and protection from sin is, the writer says, even clearer in the case of the Prophet Muḥammad, whose person embodies all perfections. He approvingly cites a Persian saying to the effect: “What all the excellent possess between them, you alone possess.” For that reason the commentator did not dwell on those other perfections individually.

As a reason for the second explanation, the designation explicitly proclaims the Prophet's being sent. Calling him merely a prophet does not entail messengerhood, since the former rank is broader; nor do the other descriptions convey this particular status as directly.

The glossator then examines the distinction between rasūl and nabī. The commentator of al-Madārik, in discussing the Qurʾānic phrase “We sent before you no messenger and no prophet ...” (22:52), takes the co-ordination as clear evidence that the two are not identical, contrary to some views. But the precise boundary between them is disputed. A report attributed to Abū Dharr gives a total of 124,000 prophets, of whom 313 are messengers, and speaks of 104 revealed books. The glossator instead prefers an account reported from the Imams: a messenger directly sees and converses with the angel, whereas prophecy can also be realised through inspiration, dreams or hearing a voice. This is a source-specific theological preference, not an independently authenticated account of the distinctions or numbers.
