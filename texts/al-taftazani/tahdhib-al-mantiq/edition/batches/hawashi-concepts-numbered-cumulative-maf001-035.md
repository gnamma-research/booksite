( شيخ عبد الرحيم )

</div>

### Working English

On the words ‘Know that what is understood from an expression …’: the apparent implication is that a concept (mafhūm) and a meaning (maʿnā) are identical in themselves. A meaning, according to the definition given, is the mental form considered insofar as an expression has been assigned to it. A concept would therefore also be a mental form. It would follow that universality and particularity are attributes of the form, although they are attributes of what the form represents. It would also follow that, in distinguishing universal from particular, what matters is whether conceiving the form prevents [its being predicated of many] or does not prevent it, whereas what matters is whether conceiving what it represents prevents this or does not.

This difficulty is not removed by the investigator al-Sharīf’s remark in his marginalia on the commentary on the Maṭāliʿ. After the commentator’s words ‘a concept is what occurs in the intellect,’ he says that ‘what occurs’ means what is of a kind to occur in the intellect, whether or not it actually occurs. For the thing of a kind to be present in the mind is the form, not what the form represents. Moreover, ‘what occurs’ means what actually occurs, since universality and particularity are mental attributes: what has not actually occurred in the mind is neither universal nor particular. Unless, that is, ‘universal’ is taken to mean what need not actually be universal but is of a kind to be universal, whether actually so or not; the same would apply to ‘particular.’

The difficulty can, however, be removed as follows. ‘Mental form’ is used both for a quality occurring in the intellect which serves as a mirror for apprehending what the form represents, and for the known quiddity distinguished in the mind by means of that form, considered with respect to its presence in the mind and its mental existence. It is the latter that is divided into a universal, whose predication of many can be supposed without impossibility, and a particular, whose predication of many cannot be so supposed.

### Editorial qualifications

Supersedes the Stage112 partial English, which remains in its immutable source file. This is full coverage of the digital numbered note, not a photographed witness. The digital wording ‘سواء حصل بالعقل أولا’ and the repeated potential/actual phrasing are retained in Arabic; the English reads the evident contrast ‘whether or not it occurs.’ The distinction is between a particular cognitive quality and the represented quiddity considered in mental existence; it does not establish that every school uses ‘mental form’ identically.

<a id="HWN-MAFAHIM-004"></a>
## HWN-MAFAHIM-004 — local note 4 — PARTIAL English

**Reported voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī, followed by separate ʿAbd al-Raḥīm note

**Source:** [Qum 216](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/216?lang=ar) · [Qum 217](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/217?lang=ar) · [Qum 218](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/218?lang=ar)

### Arabic — SHORT LOCATOR ONLY

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله الفرض هاهنا بمعنى تجويز العقل

</div>

### Working English

The division of conceptions into universal and particular is stated in terms of whether the intellect can **allow** the concept's application to many. If “supposition” merely meant arbitrarily imagining a different arrangement of things, one might imagine even Zayd as distributed among several individuals, making every individual concept universal. Instead *farḍ* here means a possibility the intellect accepts under the content's nature, not whatever words a speaker happens to entertain.

Several familiar objections follow. A distant figure might be thought to be Zayd or ʿAmr or Bakr; does that make the seen individual universal? The answer distinguishes application to many *jointly* from uncertainty over which **one** of many is indicated. The remote figure has one actual intended referent, although the observer has several alternative conjectures. Conversely a general concept with no external instances, such as the supposed partner of God or the phoenix, need not become particular merely through lacking instantiation.

A second objection concerns the phrase “to many” (*kathīrīn*), with its masculine rational plural. If it strictly excluded nonrational referents, concepts of animals or nonliving things could not be universal, an absurd consequence. A semantic rather than school-grammar reading is therefore preferred; some propose “to multiplicity” (*ʿalā al-kathra*) instead. Another objection concerns the minimum number denoted by “many,” but a universal's potential applicability does not become particular because only two or five instances happen to be available in some imagined domain.

### Editorial qualifications

Q216–218 long source, several objections/replies omitted, hence PARTIAL.

## HWN-MAFAHIM-005 — local note 5 — PARTIAL English

**Reported voice:** Shaykh ʿAbd al-Raḥīm with further quoted Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī intervention

**Source:** [Qum 218](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/218?lang=ar) · [Qum 219](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/219?lang=ar)

### Arabic — SHORT LOCATOR ONLY

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله كشريك الباري تعالى

</div>

### Working English

A concept like “a partner of the Creator” can be universal in form while having no real instances. The immediate objection is that rational consideration judges the actual instantiation of such a thing impossible, so how can one claim that the intellect allows many instances? The reply distinguishes **mere initial intelligibility of a general concept** from a subsequent demonstration of impossibility. The mind can combine the descriptive elements before proving that no such instance exists.

The source then tests concepts such as “not-thing,” “nonexistent” and “impossible under general possibility.” It distinguishes supposing an impossible situation from asserting that the supposed situation can in fact occur. However, this approach risks allowing the arbitrary supposition that a personal individual has many instances. To exclude that, the writer says a definite individual cannot be multiplied under precisely the very individuality in question, whereas a universal description may remain repeatable in a merely assumed domain.

### Editorial qualifications

Extended dispute crosses Q218–219, important distinctions of abstract repeatability vs real possibility; not fully translated, PARTIAL.

<a id="HWN-MAFAHIM-006"></a>
## HWN-MAFAHIM-006 — local note 6 — PARTIAL English

**Reported voice:** Mullā ʿAlī Akbar al-Ahrī, independently signed

**Source:** [Qum 219](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/219?lang=ar)

### Arabic — SHORT LOCATOR ONLY

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله أي لم يمتنع

</div>

### Working English

The expression “it does not prevent” may concern general possibility—removing necessity from one side—rather than special possibility—removing necessity from both existence and nonexistence. The text distinguishes the necessary, the impossible and the specially possible through which side's necessity is excluded. An objection says that, if the concept of possible instances uses general possibility in one sense, it may include the impossible; if it uses special possibility, it cannot properly be contrasted with what is necessary.

The response fixes the side under discussion. Removing the necessity of *nonexistence* allows both what is necessary and what is contingent but not what is impossible. Removing the necessity of existence, by contrast, can include the impossible. Since the intended alternative is to what is absolutely impossible, the former interpretation is preferred.

### Editorial qualifications

Separate signed al-Ahrī note, not automatically Y or other later scholar. A complex modal passage, PARTIAL.

## HWN-MAFAHIM-009 — local note 9 — PARTIAL English

**Reported voice:** Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī

**Source:** [Qum 220](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/220?lang=ar)

### Arabic — SHORT LOCATOR ONLY

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله أو امتناعه كمفهوم واجب الوجود

</div>

### Working English

The concept “necessary existent” is universal as a concept even though sound metaphysical reasoning excludes the presence of more than one such existent. The text distinguishes what the mind can provisionally conceive under the words from what can be judged possible after proof. Otherwise the concept would be rendered particular by an external result of metaphysical demonstration rather than by its own abstract representational form.

A further objection says that the class of universals with possible instances should not contain a concept which, at most, could have only one instance. The reply distinguishes impossibility at the first apprehension from impossibility after analysis and considers whether a plural expression of “instances” may stand generically for a single or several individuals. The same argument cannot simply infer that God has more than one real instantiation; it is a dispute over the classification of concepts, not a metaphysical declaration.

### Editorial qualifications

Long Q220 source, several scope replies; PARTIAL.

## HWN-MAFAHIM-010 — local note 10 — PARTIAL English

**Reported voice:** al-Taqrīb pp.25 (quoted)

**Source:** [Qum 220](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/220?lang=ar) · [Qum 221](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/221?lang=ar)

### Arabic — SHORT LOCATOR ONLY

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله كمعلومات الباري

</div>

### Working English

The things known by God can be considered without limit in number, on the cited philosophical account. The source also mentions rational souls under a theory of an eternal succession of events. “Without end” in the latter case does not mean that there must be a presently existing infinite aggregate of souls at a single instant; it means that after any one soul there may be another without reaching a last soul. The distinction between an actual simultaneous infinity and an unending succession matters to the argument.

The philosophical background presupposes doctrines about the eternity of the world and the soul which other theological schools deny. The source's words are descriptive of that position, not the editor's conclusion or a proof of an actual infinity.

## HWN-MAFAHIM-012 — local note 12 — FIRST-PASS English complete

**Source:** [Qum 221](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/221?lang=ar)

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

قوله : « اى كل كليين » : يعنى ان اللام في قوله : « والكليان » للاستغراق ، فيعم جميع الكليات وفيه ان الكلام انما في الكليات التي لها مصداق في الخارج كما سنشير اليه .

وما ذكر ان الجزئيين لا يتحقق بينهما الا التباين ، فيه انه ان أريد به ان التباين بينهما باعتبار التصادق كما هو الحق فلا نسلم انهما متباينان وكيف ، ومرجع التباين كما سيذكره المحشى ، إلى سالبتين كليتين والسالبتان الحاصلتان من الجزئيين شخصيتان وعلى هذا قس الكلى والجزئي ، وان أريد به ان التباين أعم من أن يكونا باعتبار التصادق أو باعتبار الموجود فلا نسلم ان الجزئيين لا يكونان إلا متباينين فان النسب الأربع ح تجرى بينهما وبين الكلى والجزئي . ( عبد الرحيم )

</div>

### Working English

On ‘that is, any two universals’: the definite article in ‘the two universals’ is exhaustive and therefore embraces all universals. There is a difficulty: the discussion concerns only universals having an instance in external reality, as we shall indicate.

The four relations were considered between two universals, rather than between two concepts, because two concepts may both be universal, both particular, or one universal and the other particular. Had the author said ‘the two concepts,’ one might suppose that every kind of relation occurs within each of these three classes. That is not so. All four occur between universals in the sense that there are two particular universals between which disjointness obtains, two others between which equality obtains, and so forth.

Between two particulars, on this explanation, only disjointness occurs. Between a particular and a universal, only disjointness or unrestricted inclusion occurs: if the particular is an instance of this universal, it is absolutely narrower than it; if it is not an instance, it is disjoint. By saying ‘the two universals,’ the author indicates that the other two classes are not in the same position. This is what can be gathered from some writers. The truth, however, is that the restriction indicates that the principal purpose is to learn the relations of universals to one another.

As for the assertion that only disjointness occurs between two particulars: if the disjointness concerns their joint predicability, as it properly should, we do not grant that they are disjoint in this technical sense. How could we? Disjointness, as the glossator will state, resolves into two universal negatives; but the two negatives formed from particulars are singular propositions. Apply the same consideration to a universal and a particular. If, instead, ‘disjointness’ is broad enough to concern either joint predicability or the existing things themselves, we do not grant that particulars can only be disjoint. Under that interpretation, the four relations can occur between them, and between a universal and a particular.

### Editorial qualifications

The note first reports a rationale and then rejects part of it; the English retains both voices. ‘Particular universals’ here means two specifically selected universal concepts, not concepts simultaneously universal and logically particular. The treatment of particulars is source-specific, not a modern set-theoretic claim.

ثم لا يخفى : ان النسب الأربع كما تحقق في المفردات وما في حكمها من المركبات التقييدية ، كذلك تحقق في القضايا ، الا انها تلاحظ في المفردات بحسب الصدق اى : الحمل على شئ كما ستعرف آنفا وفي القضايا بحسب الصدق اى : التحقق والوجود في الواقع ونفس الامر بمعنى مطابقتها له والصدق بمعنى الحمل يستعمل ب « على » فيقال مثلا : الحيوان صادق على الانسان اى : محمول عليه وبمعنى التحقق والوجود يستعمل ب « في » فيقال مثلا : صدقت هذه القضية في الواقع ونفس الامر والصدق بمعنى الحمل على شئ لا يتصور في القضايا ، ضرورة ان قولنا مثلا : زيد ضارب ، من حيث هو هو لا يحمل على مفرد ولا على قضية كما أنه بمعنى التحقق والوجود اى المطابقة لنفس الامر لا يتصور في المفردات وما في حكمها ، اما فيها فظاهر واما فيما هو في حكمها فلما سيأتي في تعريف القضية إن شاء اللّه تعالى لكن لما كان المقصود منها هنا ما يكون بحسب الصدق والحمل لا مطلقا خصّها بالكليات فلا محذور . ( ميرزا محمد على )

</div>

### Working English

On ‘one of the four relations must obtain between them’: this points to an answer to an objection. Someone says that partial disjointness is a kind of relation in general, yet falls under none of the four divisions; the division is therefore not exhaustive. The answer is that the particular relation actually obtaining between two universals can only be one of the four. Partial disjointness, considered merely as such, does not obtain between two concepts without further determination; when specifically determined, it is not a fifth relation set over against the four. Perhaps this is what is meant by the answer that the purpose is to exhaust the species of relation, excluding partial disjointness because it is a genus of total disjointness and overlap. Calling these a species and a genus is nevertheless somewhat strained. Whether partial disjointness is essential to both, and whether their distinction rests on specific differentiae rather than accidents—as in subdivisions of a species—requires consideration.

A difficulty remains. The claim that every two universals must bear one of the four relations is not beyond weakness. ‘Non-thing’ and ‘not-possible in the general sense of possibility’ are two universal concepts, yet none of the four relations obtains between them. They are not totally disjoint: as will be explained, the contradictories of two disjoint terms must be partially disjoint, whereas ‘thing’ and ‘possible’ are certainly equal in extension. Nor are the former two equal in extension, since neither is predicable of anything at all, so that they cannot be jointly predicated. Nor do they stand in unrestricted inclusion or overlap. As will be explained, the positive broader term must be predicable together with the contradictory of the narrower; neither of these two can be predicated together with the other's contradictory, or two contradictories would coincide.

One possible answer restricts the claim: every two universals, each of which is predicable of something in reality itself, must bear one of the four relations. This would not contradict the requirement that the art's rules be general and comprehensive. Their generality is required according to the purposes pursued in the art, and its practitioners have no purpose in merely supposed universals without any instances in reality itself, either externally or mentally. But that answer conflicts with the unrestricted rule that ‘the contradictories of equal terms are equal,’ as will be discussed. ‘Thing’ and ‘possible’ are equal in extension, while their contradictories, ‘non-thing’ and ‘not-possible,’ are not equal according to the account just given.

The preferable answer therefore extends the predicability used in defining the four relations to both real and hypothetical predicability. There is necessarily hypothetical universal predicability in both directions between ‘non-thing’ and ‘not-possible’: whatever is supposed to have ‘non-thing’ predicated of it also has ‘not-possible’ predicated of it, and conversely.

Observe also that the four relations obtain not only among simple terms and expressions treated like them, such as restrictive compounds, but among propositions. In simple terms they are considered with respect to ṣidq in the sense of predication of something, as will shortly be explained. In propositions ṣidq means obtaining in reality and in reality itself, that is, agreement with it. In the predicative sense ṣidq takes ʿalā: ‘animal is predicable of human,’ meaning that it is attributed to it. In the sense of obtaining and existing it takes fī: ‘this proposition is true in reality and in reality itself.’

Predicability of something is not conceivable of propositions as such: ‘Zayd is striking,’ considered just as that proposition, is predicated neither of a simple term nor of a proposition. Conversely, truth in the sense of obtaining—that is, agreement with reality itself—is not conceivable of simple terms or what is treated like them. This is evident for simple terms; for expressions treated like them, the explanation will come in the definition of a proposition, God willing. Since the purpose here concerns ṣidq as predication, rather than every sense of ṣidq, the relations have been restricted to universals. There is thus no difficulty.

### Editorial qualifications

The Q221→222 seam is joined at ‘لا تكون / مقابلة’. This note preserves the rejection of the first proposed restriction and the author's preferred extension to hypothetical predicability. Do not substitute modern vacuous universal truth for the author's explicit account without argument. ‘Partial disjointness’ is his broader condition, not automatically the modern relation ‘overlap’.

### Working English

If you object, ‘Sleeping and waking are equal in extension, yet neither is universally predicable of the other,’ I reply: what is required for two equal terms is that each be predicable of every individual of the other. It does not follow that both must be predicated together at one time. Waking and sleeping are each universally predicable of the other, but not at the same time; that does not prevent their being equal in extension.

Some instead say that the equality is between ‘sleeping at some time’ and ‘waking at some time.’ Of someone asleep, even while sleeping, it is true that he is awake at some time, although not that he is awake while sleeping. Likewise, of someone awake, even while awake, it is true that he sleeps at some time. On this account, each equal term is predicable of every individual of the other at the time when the other is predicated of that individual. Apply the same analysis to the predicability required in unrestricted inclusion and in overlap.

You may object that the author's commentary on the Talkhīṣ apparently takes marjiʿ as a noun of place which nevertheless admits ilā. Explaining the text's words ‘eloquence resolves into avoiding [error],’ he says: ‘It is what must obtain for eloquence to be able to obtain, just as one says that truth and falsehood resolve into the judgment's agreement or disagreement with reality: that through which the two are realised and attained.’ I reply: a noun of place undoubtedly cannot govern such a complement, because it is a purely nonverbal noun without the capacity to govern.

The author's wording may instead employ istikhdām, a shift in the sense resumed by a pronoun, as some investigators have explained. Marjiʿ has two meanings: returning, and the place of return. The first occurrence intends the first meaning, while its pronoun resumes the second. The cited explanation explains that second meaning and thereby clarifies the first.

### Working English

On ‘disjointness resolves into two negatives’: marjiʿ, with kasra on the jīm, is a mīm-prefixed verbal noun meaning returning, not a noun of place meaning the place of return. Its governing ilā is evidence of that. The mīm-prefixed verbal noun generally takes the pattern mafʿal, with fatḥa on its middle radical, in every conjugational class, apart from irregular forms such as marjiʿ, maghfira, mafāza, maʿdhira and maʿṣiya.

Know that the glossator's account of what the four relations resolve into concerns quantity and quality. As regards modality, disjointness resolves into two universal perpetual negatives: disjointness between universals consists in their never being jointly predicated, whether such joint predication is possible or not. Equality resolves into two universal general-absolute affirmatives, because equality between universals consists in their actual joint predicability, whether that predicability is necessary or not. Unrestricted inclusion resolves into one universal general-absolute affirmative and one particular perpetual negative. Overlap resolves into one particular general-absolute affirmative and two particular perpetual negatives.

Thus the affirmatives are absolute, and the negatives perpetual.

### Editorial qualifications

The source's modal qualifiers are translated explicitly. ‘General absolute’ and ‘perpetual’ are technical traditional modalities, not interchangeable with unqualified modern categorical propositions. This record translates the classification; it does not certify the 46 unrelated modal theorem cases.

لأنا نقول : ليس الكلام في خصوص اللاانسان واللاناطق ونحو هما بل في نقيضي المتساويين مطلقا فما تقول في مثل الشئ والممكن العام فإنهما متساويان لصدقهما على جميع المفهومات ولا يصدق نقيضاهما وهما اللاشىء واللاممكن على شئ أصلا كما تقدم فلا يتم البرهان المذكور ح البتة ، فإذا قلت : لو لم يصدق كل لا شئ لا ممكن ، لصدق بعض اللاشىء ليس بلا ممكن فيصدق بعض اللاشىء ممكن ، اتجه المنع المذكور فان الموضوع ليس بموجود قطعا .
وأجيب : بانا نخص البحث بما إذا لم يكن المتساويان شاملين لجميع الأشياء ذهنا وخارجا فلا بد ان يصدق نقيضاهما على موجود اما خارجي أو ذهني فيتم ح الدليل وينسد السبيل وتعميم القواعد انما يجب بحسب الاغراض المقصودة والمقاصد المطلوبة من الفن كما سبق .
ولا يخفى : ان هذا انما يحتاج اليه بناء على ما سبق من تخصيص الصدق بالصدق الحقيقي واما على ما اخترناه من تعميمه إلى الحقيقي والفرضي ، فلا ، لعدم ورود الاعتراض حتى يحتاج إلى الجواب وقولهم : ان السالبة المعدولة المحمول أعم من الموجبة المحصلة ، انما هو على تقدير اختصاص الصدق والوجود بالحقيقي واما على تقدير تعميمهما اليه وإلى الفرضي فالظاهر عدم الفرق بينهما ، فتأمل . ( محمد على )

</div>

### Working English

On ‘that is, everything of which one of the contradictories is predicated’: the point is that once equality between, for example, human and rational has been established, equality must also obtain between their contradictories, non-human and non-rational. Thus every non-human must be non-rational, and every non-rational thing non-human. Were these not true, their contradictories would be true: some non-human is not non-rational, and some non-rational thing is not non-human. It would then be true that some non-human is rational and some non-rational thing is human, because both contradictories cannot be absent. One positive term would therefore be predicable without the other, since contradictories cannot coincide. That contradicts the assumption.

An objection is raised: the truth of ‘Some non-human is not non-rational’ and ‘Some non-rational thing is not non-human’ does not entail the truth of ‘Some non-human is rational’ and ‘Some non-rational thing is human.’ As will be explained, a negative proposition with a negative predicate does not entail the corresponding affirmative proposition with a positive predicate. Do you not see that ‘Zayd is not a non-writer’ does not require ‘Zayd is a writer’ to be true? Zayd might be nonexistent, in which case he is neither a writer nor a non-writer.

Do not reply that the subject here exists, since non-human and non-rational apply to realised existents such as trees and stones, and that the negative with negative predicate and the positive affirmative are mutually entailing when the subject exists.

We answer that the discussion is not specifically about non-human and non-rational and similar cases. It concerns the contradictories of equal terms without restriction. What of ‘thing’ and ‘possible in the general sense’? They are equal through being predicable of all concepts, while their contradictories—‘non-thing’ and ‘not-possible’—are predicable of nothing at all, as explained earlier. The proof then certainly fails. If you say, ‘Were “Every non-thing is not-possible” not true, “Some non-thing is not not-possible” would be true, and hence “Some non-thing is possible”,’ the stated denial applies: the subject is certainly not existent.

One answer restricts the discussion to cases where the two equal terms do not embrace all things, mentally and externally. Their contradictories must then apply to something existent, externally or mentally, completing the proof and blocking the objection. As explained earlier, general rules need be general only according to the intended aims and purposes of the art.

But this restriction is needed only on the earlier assumption that predicability is confined to real predicability. On our preferred extension to real and hypothetical predicability it is unnecessary: the objection does not arise and therefore needs no answer. The assertion that a negative proposition with a negative predicate is broader than the positive affirmative holds when predicability and existence are confined to real predicability and existence. Once both are extended to the real and the hypothetical, apparently no difference remains between the two. Reflect.

### Editorial qualifications

The text's transition from failure of the two universals to the two respective particular negatives is reproduced as stated; its exact dialectical quantification is not silently repaired. ‘Negative predicate’ here renders maʿdūl al-maḥmūl and must be distinguished from a negative copula. The existential-import objection is essential; ordinary modern double-negation simplification would obscure it. Hypothetical existence is the author's proposed repair, not an independent modern proof.

### Working English

When it is false, its falsity can therefore be due only to the truth of the contradictory predicate, which entails the stated result.
