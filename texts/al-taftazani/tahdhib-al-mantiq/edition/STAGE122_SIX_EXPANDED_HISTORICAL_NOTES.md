## HWN-DALALA-067 — Q206 — one meaning, one word, and proper names

**Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī:**

What is intended by the unity of a meaning is not that its number must correspond to the number of expressions, as might first be supposed. If it were, different proper names assigned to one and the same meaning would cease to count as proper names, because the expressions and the meaning would not agree in number. But plainly that is not the case.

**Shaykh ʿAbd al-Raḥīm, investigating the glossator's statement:**

If his explanation of the unity of the meaning of a simple expression is intended to say that it has only one meaning **in reality**, then it would follow that homonymous proper names could not be proper names. If, on the other hand, he intends that its meaning is one either in reality **or by consideration and deliberate regard**, no difficulty results. Yet the former interpretation is what the words most naturally suggest.

**Terminological note:** The second speaker distinguishes a single referent in reality from unity under a mode of consideration; neither speaker makes the crude claim that every proper name must denote a different individual. The original 44-word English field passed over the second speaker's explicit dilemma.

Source: [digital Q206](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/206?lang=ar). Both speakers are visible in the archived source; exact printed-page photographic comparison remains open.

## HWN-CONVERSION-008 — Q325 — why disjunctive conditionals are said not to convert

**ʿAbd al-Raḥīm:**

What is intended here is **connected conditionals**. As for disjunctive conditionals, the logicians say that conversion cannot be conceived in them, because their two parts are not naturally distinguished from one another [as an antecedent is from a consequent].

**Objection.** What is meant by *interchanging* the sides in the definition of conversion is merely interchanging the *designations* of the two sides. There can be no doubt that this is possible with disjunctive conditionals, even though their sides are not distinguished by nature.

**Answer.** Undoubtedly a disjunctive conditional has a converse in that sense. What is understood from our saying “Either the number is even or it is odd” is not precisely what is understood from “Either the number is odd or it is even.” The meaning of the opposition of *this* to *that* is not quite the meaning of the opposition of *that* to *this*. But since nothing useful is gained by the interchange, the logicians did not recognise it as a conversion. **That**, rather than physical impossibility of reversing the words, is the sense in which they say that conversion cannot be conceived in the disjunctive case.

**Argument structure:** Stated exclusion → objection about formal reordering → concession that reordering is conceivable → practical explanation of why it is not admitted as a useful logical conversion. This preserves a distinction flattened by a mere “disjunction has no conversion” summary.

Source: [digital Q325](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/325?lang=ar).

## HWN-CONVERSION-021 — Q328 — why formal logic substitutes letters for examples

**Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī:**

Know that the customary practice of the logicians is to denote the **subject** by *jīm* (ج) and the **predicate** by *bāʾ* (ب), for two reasons.

The first is **brevity**. To say “Every ج is ب” is shorter than to say “Every human being is an animal.”

The second is to avert the mistaken impression that the rule is restricted to the particular example. Were they to make “Every human being is an animal” the example of a universal proposition and then set out the rules governing it, someone might imagine that those rules obtain only for this particular subject matter and not for other universal affirmative propositions. But when they say “Every ج is ب” and establish the rules governing it, it is thereby understood that the rules are not restricted to some instances and withheld from others: they apply to **every instance** belonging to the relevant logical form.

**Argument structure:** Two enumerated reasons, including the epistemic risk of mistaking the material content of an example for the scope of a formal rule. The original first pass conveyed both reasons but omitted the clause contrasting some instances and all instances.

Source: [digital Q328](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/328?lang=ar).

## HWN-CONTRAPOSITION-019 — Q339 — the assumed subject D in the original proposition

**Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī:**

His words “the judgment of the original was that it is not ب for as long as it is ج” mean: the original proposition judged that **د is not ب for as long as it is ج**.

For, as you have already learned, **some ج**—which is the description of the subject in the original proposition—had been judged not to be ب for as long as it bore the description ج. That very instance has now been **supposed to be د**. It follows that **د is not ب while it is ج**, which is just the conclusion sought.

His later words, “the judgment of the original was that it is ب for as long as it is ج,” are to be understood by the same method of substituting the assumed instance.

**Technical note:** The letter د designates the hypothetically selected instance; the scope of “for as long as it is ج” must remain attached to the subject-description. The text gives a substitution proof, not a universal rule that all negative judgments convert.

Source: [digital Q339](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/339?lang=ar).

## HWN-MAFAHIM-011 — Q221 — an indefinitely increasing plurality of rational souls

**Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī:**

Those philosophers say that the world is eternal, without a beginning. They also hold that whatever has no beginning has no end. The world, on their account, therefore has neither a first nor a last limit.

Consequently, rational souls are **unlimited in number** according to them—not in the sense that an actually infinite number of rational souls enter existence **all at one time**, which would invite the objection that what existence has gathered into an actual ordered collection must be finite. Rather, the sense is that the series never reaches a limit **after which no further rational soul comes into existence**. Whenever a rational soul exists, another may come into existence after it, without an ultimate terminus.

The matter is analogous to the numbers. They do not arrive at a final number beyond which there is none; at every rank that is reached, one may go on to the next without end. And something similar is said of the things known to the Creator, exalted is He.

Now know that the glossator's words evidently make **“the rational soul,” in the singular**, an example of the **universal**, since he used a singular expression. By contrast, the wording used by certain others, “and such as the rational souls,” in the plural, expressly makes them an example of the **existing individuals falling under a universal**.

**Textual caution:** The Arabic phrase concerning what existence has actually arranged or collected is syntactically awkward in the recovered digital transcription. The English states the contrast—the difference between simultaneous actual infinity and succession without a final bound—without claiming that this particular noun phrase has been manuscript-collated. The old 60-word field also omitted the final crucial distinction between a universal and its individuals.

Source: [digital Q221](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/221?lang=ar).

## HWN-INTRO-081 — Q187 — the subject of logic: intelligible meanings, not utterances

**Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī:**

This would be an instance of **naming the signifying expression after the thing signified**. Both this statement and the author's words “because they become a cause ...” indicate that the *defining expression* (*muʿarrif*) and the *proof* (*ḥujja*)—which are the subjects of this science—are, in truth, expressions for **their meanings**. It is evident that what explains and makes known the state of something conceptually unknown is the **meaning**, not the uttered words. But since words are pathways to meanings, they are named after what they convey.

This rebuts those who have supposed that the subject of logic is **words insofar as they signify meanings**. Their reason was that logicians call the expression “rational animal” (*ḥayawān nāṭiq*) a “definition”; they call its first part, “animal,” the “genus,” and its last part, “rational,” the “differentia.” Likewise they call the sequence “Every ج is ب; every ب is ا” a “syllogism”; they name its first proposition the “minor premise” and the second its “major premise.”

From this usage, they imagined that all these names refer to the utterances in virtue of their signification of meanings. Yet the truth is that the **subject of logic is the meanings themselves**, and these terms properly refer to those meanings. Attention to the verbal side is incidental and instrumental, as the glossator will explicitly say.

**Technical note:** This does not deny the indispensable instrumental role of linguistic signs in logical teaching. It distinguishes logic's primary object—the intelligible contents of definition and argument—from the labels given to the verbal expressions through which they are conveyed. The original 60-word field compressed the whole objection, its examples, and the reply into a short conclusion.

Source: [digital Q187](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/187?lang=ar).
