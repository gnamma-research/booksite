## 3. Substantive technical findings

### A. H-INTRO 50–64 — what the rational instrument does

1. **Note50 (Q178–179)** revisits the **Meno-style paradox of inquiry**: a desired result must be known in one respect to be sought but unknown in another to be genuinely acquired. Al-Rāzī's reported objection to even this distinction and a separate omitted dispute by Sharaf al-Dīn al-Marāghī are clearly separated. Note50 remains PARTIAL: the source declines to supply Marāghī's complete exchange.
2. **Notes52–53 (Q179)** distinguish the several technical senses of `ʿilm` (mental form, justified certitude, wider belief including error) and the logical difference between a sensed individual and a universal middle term. This is a historically attributed theory, not a modern prohibition on learning about individuals through signs.
3. **Notes54–58 (Q180)** give the notion of a general `qānūn` as a **universal major premise applied to a particular minor** (e.g. Zayd is an agent; every agent is nominative; hence Zayd is nominative). The historic report that a gloss is arranged in different positions in some copies is NOT a verified manuscript collation. Even an already obvious particular result remains in the *potential application range* of a rule.
4. **Notes60–64 (Q180–182)** distinguish strict contradiction from broader incompatibility, intellectual errors *within the same person across time* from outward disagreements between two people, and protection through **observance** of inferential rules rather than mere possession. The claimed need for logic is explicitly restricted to the discursive thinker as such; immediate/non-discursive disclosure is not logically disproved by the argument.

### B. H-INTRO 65–82 — why logic is needed and what its science studies

5. **Notes66–68 (Q182–183):** the demonstration of need for logic has to explain *both* acquisition of conceptions and proof of judgments. The apparent three premises can be analyzed into four. The circularity/regress objection ("learning logic already requires logic") is addressed by **some immediately available first-figure norms** grounding later learned norms, with an important further distinction between **needing a principle** and **needing to learn it**. Note68 remains PARTIAL.
6. **Notes69–70 (Q183)** distinguish definition by a science's **end (rasm)** from an essential `ḥadd`, and the requirement to *observe* norms to avoid mistakes. The same end may serve several sciences unless specifically qualified.
7. **Note71 (Q183–184)** discusses the claimed **subject of logic**: previously known conceptual and assertoric contents *insofar as leading to unknown conclusions*. An independently quoted al-Taqrīb critique notes that “fire is hot,” though evident, can function as a premise in a further inference. The right distinction is capacity to lead to new understanding, not merely profundity. This multivoice note remains PARTIAL.
8. **Notes72–77 (Q184–186)** develop **the subject of a science as what has its essential accidents investigated**. Examples draw on medicine, jurisprudence and grammar. The intrinsic/foreign accident classification depends on whether an intermediary is internal/external and equal, broader, narrower or disjoint in extension; the borderline "more general internal constituent" is disputed. The separate long Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī source after 73 is kept *unnumbered*. Notes73,75,77 remain PARTIAL.
9. **Notes80–82 (Q186–187)** distinguish **capacity of a definiens/proof to lead to the unknown**, which restricts the logical subject, from **the manner in which it leads**, which is investigated as an attribute. The defining expression and syllogism are verbal means; on this account their meanings are the primary logical objects. The term `ḥujja` may be transferred from the result "victory over an opponent" to its cause, the argument.

### C. NEW H-DALALA 1–20 — signification and the words/meanings distinction

10. [**p188**](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/188?lang=ar) begins a fresh source heading, with the triad of **`muṭābaqa` (whole assigned meaning), `taḍammun` (part of assigned meaning), and `iltizām` (concomitant outside assigned meaning)**. The sources stress that this lexical introduction serves logic instrumentally: the *meanings*, not sounds, define and infer.
11. [**p189**](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/189?lang=ar) distinguishes conventional `waḍʿ` (writing, hand-count, gesture, route sign) from *natural* signs and *rational* indicators. A definition based merely on the hearer's unlearned comprehension risks collapsing natural and rational signs.
12. [**p190**](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/190?lang=ar) discusses **natural nonverbal signs** (pulse, blush, fear, gait) and rational inference: one and the same sign can lead to a judgment from habit without conscious inference or from known causal connection. A nonsense sound heard behind a wall indicates an unseen speaker by rational inference, regardless of the sound's lack of lexical assignment.
13. [**p191**](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/191?lang=ar) gives the empirical **2 × 3 = six** verbal/nonverbal × conventional/natural/rational types and distinguishes this observed completeness from the proposed logically exhaustive whole/part/external-concomitant division of conventional verbal meaning. A Quṭb al-Dīn quotation insists that conventional verbal signification proceeds *because of knowledge of assignment*, not simply because a listener happens to infer something from a word. The source’s rhetorical, linguistic and uṣūl criteria should not be homogenized.
14. Incomplete signification notes **1,12–15,18** are deliberately flagged, and local note **21 begins on Q191 and continues on [p192](https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/12170/192?lang=ar)**.
