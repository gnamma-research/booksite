### Main scholarly arguments in the added notes

1. **111 (Q156–157):** the historical gloss derives semantics of iḍāfa from **four relations between extensions** (coextensive, disjoint, inclusive, overlapping), then argues about `ʿaqāʾid al-Islām` being lāmī vs bayānī. The claim that an explanatory genitive violates consensus belongs to that glossator. It is not independent attestation of a universal grammatical consensus.
2. **112 (Q157):** lexical `īmān` as assent in the heart and role of speech/action in manifesting it. A **separate** Shiʿi Imāmī comment immediately follows and crosses p158, arguing Islam in its outward legal sense is broader than faith, supported by reported narrations and Qurʾān 49:14. Preserve different authors/voices and do not certify chains.
3. **113–114 (Q158):** lexical family j-n-n—concealed heart, fetus, jinn, garden, shield—requires vowelling; the unidentified printed siglum **M–B** must not be turned into a known author. `Sakanjabīn` analogy: composite differs from ingredient, used to assess relation between beliefs and Islam.
4. **115–116 (Q158–159):** the book may be called “insight” by figurative attribution; the agent and recipient of `ifhām` are unexpressed and the grammatical replacement of a pronominal or nominal expression by definite `al-` is debated. These are semantic/grammatical proposals, not source variants.
5. **117–119 (Q159):** `aw` need not make two functions mutually exclusive; a text can aid both teacher and learner. `Taḍmīn` explains why `yatadhakkar` may govern a preposition natural to “take from” or “learn from,” and why direct vs implicit adverbial governor matters.
6. **120–123 (Q159–160):** `lā siyamā` is analyzed as an idiom indicating special precedence, debated against a merely comparative likeness. The following noun admits genitive, nominative, accusative with alternate assumptions of ellipsis, relative `mā`, `tamyīz` and disjoined exception. These are not identical formal analyses.
7. **124–126 (Q160):** morphology of instrumental `faʿʿāl`, an early lexical sense of `ʿiṣām` as a waterskin fastening, and *sajʿ* as balanced prose endings. The concern to call Qurʾānic verse endings `fawāṣil` rather than `asjāʿ` reflects a terminological and decorum dispute.
