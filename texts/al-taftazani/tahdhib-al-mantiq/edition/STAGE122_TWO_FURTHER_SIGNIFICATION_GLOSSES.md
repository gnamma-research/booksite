## HWN-DALALA-039 — Q200 — what makes an expression compound?

**Shaykh ʿAbd al-Raḥīm:**

His words, “The third condition is that a part of its expression should signify a part of its meaning.”

Know that, in the *First Teaching* (*al-Taʿlīm al-Awwal*) that Aristotle composed for this art, this much alone was initially deemed sufficient to define a **compound expression**. When some investigators objected by adducing **simple expressions whose components do have a meaning**—such as “ʿAbd Allāh” or “rational animal” when each is used as a *proper name*—later writers added the requirement of **intention** (*qaṣd*), in order to exclude those expressions.

They did not classify such proper names as compound, as the grammarians customarily do, because their treatment of **words follows their treatment of meanings**. Accordingly, simplicity and composition in logic are determined by the **unity or multiplicity of meanings**, rather than merely by the number of verbal components.

**Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī:**

On his words, “The third condition is that a part of its expression should signify...”: an objection has been raised here. Suppose an expression is **simple with regard to its implicative or included meaning** (*maʿnā taḍammunī*), yet **compound with regard to its complete corresponding meaning** (*maʿnā muṭābaqī*). Then, under these two different considerations, both the definition of “compound” and that of “simple” will apply to it. Neither definition would be universally coextensive with what it defines.

**Reply.** If the definition includes the restriction to *correspondence* (*muṭābaqa*), as al-Kātibī and others include it, the answer is obvious: the expression will fall under the definition of the **compound** and no other. And even if that restriction is not included, there is nothing extraordinary in an expression being compound under one consideration and simple under another. “ʿAbd Allāh” and “rational animal” illustrate this difference, according as they are considered under a **proper-name assignment** or as **syntactically compound expressions**.

In sum, the presence of an **intended signification** in the definition of “compound,” and its absence in the definition of “simple,” must be judged **with regard to one and the same thing**. If the consideration is the full corresponding meaning, it is compound and not simple; if the consideration is the included meaning, it is simple and not compound. Do not overlook this point.

**Logical significance:** The apparent violation of exclusivity arises only when the two definitions are evaluated under *different semantic respects*. The passage distinguishes the complete signified meaning, signification by inclusion, and the speaker's intended analysis. It does not merely assert that multiword names behave like single words.

## HWN-DALALA-081 — Q212 — homonymy, ambiguity, and original assignment

**Shaykh ʿAbd al-Raḥīm:**

That is, the expression is **shared** (*mushtarak*) with respect to all the meanings taken together. In relation to each of them individually, however, it is called **indeterminate or ambiguous** (*mujmal*): that the expression has been assigned to this meaning, and has likewise been assigned to that meaning, is known, so in this respect it is shared among those meanings. But when the expression is uttered, **which one of them is intended**—this one or that one—is not known. In this respect it is indeterminate, because its indication is not clear.

**Mīrzā Muḥammad ʿAlī, investigating this question:**

On his words, “the first is called shared”: a **homonymous expression** (*al-mushtarak*) is what has been assigned to **several meanings**, by **several acts of assignment**, with an **original assignment** (*waḍʿ ibtidāʾī*).

The first condition, “to several meanings,” excludes the proper name (*ʿalam*), the univocal expression (*mutawāṭiʾ*), the expression whose meaning is graded or analogical (*mushakkak*), the indefinite (*nakira*), and literal and figurative usages (*ḥaqīqa* and *majāz*).

The condition “by several acts of assignment” excludes what has been assigned to several meanings **by one comprehensive assignment**. This includes demonstratives, relative expressions, and other indeterminate forms according to later logicians. When such a form is instituted, a **universal meaning** is first considered, and by one act of assignment it is then made applicable to each of its particulars. Accordingly, such indeterminate forms fall outside the definition of homonymy according to either of the two positions under discussion.

The expression “by an original assignment” excludes a **transferred expression** (*manqūl*). Although it has been assigned to several meanings by several assignments, these are not all original assignments: the assignment to the transferred meaning is plainly **secondary**.

As for the **newly applied name without a semantic connection to its earlier sense** (*murtajal*), some have held that it is included in homonymy. Since no suitability or connection to the original meaning is taken into account in assigning the new meaning, the second assignment is **original** in the relevant sense. What is meant by its originality is not that **all** the acts of assignment happened at the same chronological level, without any preceding another. Rather, the point is that the latest assignment is made **without taking account of the earlier meaning or its suitability**, and thus is **independent**.

Others have held that the *murtajal* is a **coordinate kind distinct from homonymy**. They interpret “original assignment” as an assignment in which the situation of the prior meaning is not taken into account **in any respect whatsoever**—even with regard to the **absence of suitability**. But when the *murtajal* is assigned to its later meaning, the earlier meaning is indeed considered insofar as there is **no suitability** between the two. Thus it retains a kind of dependence and secondariness, and falls outside a strictly independent original assignment.

**Analytical distinction:** Here *mushtarak* concerns multiple instituted meanings, whereas *mujmal* concerns which meaning is intended in a given utterance. The dispute over *murtajal* is not merely chronological: it asks whether *taking account of the lack of a semantic relation* already makes the newer act of naming dependent upon the earlier one.
