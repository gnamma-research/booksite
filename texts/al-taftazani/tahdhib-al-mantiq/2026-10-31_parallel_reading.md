# Tahdhīb al-Manṭiq — تهذيب المنطق

**Saʿd al-Dīn al-Taftāzānī** · Arabic base: Cairo, Maṭbaʿat al-Saʿāda, 1330 AH / 1912, pp. 3–14.

English facing Arabic; text normalized for reading, with source-variant and explanatory notes. Complete in coverage of the 19 logic divisions. Some technical readings await full independent manuscript collation; do not cite as a definitive manuscript-critical edition.

## L.01: Opening dedication and preface

*Cairo pp. 3*

**Arabic**

بسم الله الرحمن الرحيم. الحمد لله الذي هدانا سواء الطريق، وجعل لنا التوفيق خير رفيق، والصلاة والسلام على من أرسله هدى هو بالاهتداء حقيق، ونورا به الاقتداء يليق، وعلى آله وأصحابه الذين سعدوا في مناهج الصدق بالتصديق، وصعدوا في معارج الحق بالتحقيق. وبعد، فهذا غاية تهذيب الكلام في تحرير المنطق والكلام، وتقريب المرام من تقرير عقائد الإسلام. جعلته تبصرة لدى الأفهام، وتذكرة لمن أراد أن يتذكر من ذوي الأفهام، سيما الولد الأعز الحفي الحري بالإكرام، سمي حبيب الله عليه التحية والسلام. لا زال له من التوفيق قوام، ومن التأييد عصام، وعلى الله التوكل وبه الاعتصام.

**English**

Praise belongs to God, who has guided us to the straight course and made success [tawfīq] our best companion. Blessings and peace be upon the one whom He sent as a guidance worthy of being followed in finding guidance, and as a light whose example deserves to be followed; and upon his family and companions, who attained felicity, on the paths of truthfulness, through assent [taṣdīq], and ascended, on the steps of truth, through verification [taḥqīq]. To proceed: this is the utmost refinement of discourse, in setting forth logic and kalām, and in bringing near the intended aim through setting out the doctrines of Islam. I have made it an illumination for whoever seeks insight in the course of instruction, and a reminder for whoever among those possessing understanding wishes to recollect; and especially for the dearest, most affectionate son deserving of honour, the namesake of the Beloved of God—upon him greetings and peace. May success always sustain him, and divine support protect him. Our reliance is upon God, and in Him we seek protection.

## L.02: Knowledge, rational inquiry and the subject of logic

*Cairo pp. 4*

**Arabic**

العلم إن كان إذعانًا للنسبة فتصديق وإلا فتصور، وينقسمان بالضرورة إلى الضرورة والاكتساب بالنظر، وهو ملاحظة المعقول لتحصيل المجهول، وقد يقع فيه الخطأ فاحتيج إلى قانون يعصم عنه وهو المنطق. وموضوعه المعلوم التصوري والتصديقي من حيث يوصل إلى مطلوب تصوري فيسمى معرِّفًا، أو تصديقي فيسمى حجة.

**English**

If knowledge is assent to a relation, it is judgment (taṣdīq); otherwise, conception (taṣawwur). Both are divided into what is immediate and what is acquired through rational consideration. Rational consideration is attention to the intelligible in order to attain what is unknown. Error may occur in it, and so a rule was needed to protect the mind against error: logic. Its subject is what is already known, whether conceptual or propositional, insofar as this leads to a sought conception, called a means of definition (muʿarrif), or to a sought judgment, called a proof (ḥujja).

**Notes**

- The decisive distinction is between the act of conceiving something and assenting to a propositional relation. A means of definition and a proof correspond to these two kinds of cognitive aim.

## L.03: Signification, simple and compound expressions, and kinds of names

*Cairo pp. 4–5*

**Arabic**

دلالة اللفظ على تمام ما وضع له مطابقة، وعلى جزئه تضمن، وعلى الخارج التزام، ولا بد من اللزوم عقلا أو عرفا، وتلزمهما المطابقة ولو تقديرا ولا عكس. والموضوع له إن قصد بجزئه منه الدلالة على جزء المعنى فمركب، إما تام خبر أو إنشاء وإما ناقص تقييدي أو غيره، وإلا فمفرد، وهو إن استقل في الدلالة بهيئته على أحد الأزمنة كلمة، وبدونها اسم، وإلا فأداة. وأيضا إن اتحد معناه فمع تشخصه وضعا عَلَم، وبدونه متواطئ إن استوت أفراده، ومشكك إن تفاوتت إما بأولية أو أولوية، وإن كثر معناه فإن وضع لكل فمشترك، وإلا فإن اشتهر في الثاني فمنقول ينسب إلى الناقل، وإلا فحقيقة ومجاز.

**English**

Signification of the whole instituted meaning is correspondence (muṭābaqa), of part of that meaning inclusion (taḍammun), and of something outside it implication (iltizām). Implication requires a rational or customary necessary connection; the latter two kinds of signification presuppose correspondence, at least implicitly, though not the converse. An expression is compound if its constituent parts are intended to signify constituents of its meaning: it may be complete, either an assertive statement or a nonassertive utterance, or incomplete, whether restrictive or otherwise. If not, it is simple. If it signifies independently with reference to one of the three times, it is a verb (kalima); without that temporal feature, a noun; without independent signification, a particle. When an expression has one meaning instituted for a particular person or thing it is a proper name; otherwise, it is univocal when its instances stand equally under that meaning, and analogically graded (mushakkik) when they differ by priority or precedence. If several meanings are independently instituted for it, it is equivocal (mushtarak). If a second meaning becomes established it is a transferred term attributed to the person or convention responsible for the transfer; otherwise, we distinguish literal and figurative usage.

**Notes**

- Iltizām does not designate mere psychological association: a recognised rational or conventional implication is required. Univocal, analogically graded, equivocal and transferred predication are distinct classifications, not interchangeable translations.

## L.04: Particular and universal concepts; degrees of instantiation

*Cairo pp. 5*

**Arabic**

فصل. المفهوم إن امتنع فرض صدقه على كثيرين فجزئي وإلا فكلي؛ امتنعت أفراده أو أمكنت ولم توجد، أو وجد الواحد فقط مع إمكان الغير أو امتناعه، أو الكثير مع التناهي أو عدمه.

**English**

Chapter. If it is impossible to suppose that a concept truly applies to many [individuals], it is a particular [juzʾī]; otherwise it is a universal [kullī]. Instances of a universal may be impossible; or they may be possible but not exist; or only one may exist, either with others possible or with others impossible; or many may exist, whether finite or infinite in number.

**Notes**

- A universal is defined by the possibility of being predicated truly of many, not by the actual existence of several instances. Hence it can have no instances, one instance, or many.

## L.05: Relations between two universals

*Cairo pp. 5*

**Arabic**

والكليان إن تفارقا كلياً فمتباينان، وإلا فإن تصادقا كلياً من الجانبين فمتساويان ونقيضاهما كذلك، أو من جانب فأعم وأخص مطلقاً ونقيضاهما بالعكس، وإلا فمن وجه، وبين نقيضيهما تباين جزئي كالمتباينين. وقد يقال الجزئي للأخص وهو أعم.

**English**

If two universals are entirely separate in extension, they are disjoint. Otherwise, if they coincide in their instances universally in both directions, they are coextensive, and their contradictories are likewise coextensive. If the inclusion is universal in only one direction, one is absolutely more general and the other absolutely more specific, while the relation between their contradictories is reversed. Otherwise they overlap in some respects; their contradictories exhibit partial exclusion, as do those of disjoint universals. The term ‘particular’ may also designate what is more specific relative to another, and in this relative sense it extends beyond the absolute particular.

**Notes**

- These are logical relations between ranges of predication, not claims that the universals are numerically separate entities.

## L.06: The five predicables: genus

*Cairo pp. 5*

**Arabic**

والكليات خمس: الأول الجنس، وهو المقول على الكثرة المختلفة الحقيقة في جواب ما هو، فإن كان الجواب عن الماهية وعن بعض مشاركاتها هو الجواب عنها وعن الكل فقريب كالحيوان، وإلا فبعيد كالجسم النامي.

**English**

The five predicables are as follows. First, genus: what is predicated of many things differing in their specific natures in answer to ‘What is it?’ If the answer, for a quiddity and some of the things sharing its genus, is also the answer for all such sharers, it is a proximate genus, such as ‘animal’; otherwise a remote genus, such as ‘growing body’.

**Notes**

- Genus is predicable of distinct specific natures; the distinction between proximate and remote is relative to the classification being made.
- A genus is predicated essentially across several differing species in answer to 'What is it?', not merely as a trait shared accidentally.

## L.07: Species, differentia, property and common accident

*Cairo pp. 5–7*

**Arabic**

الثاني النوع، وهو المقول على الكثرة المتفقة الحقيقة في جواب ما هو. وقد يقال على الماهية الكلية المقول عليها وعلى غيرها الجنس في جواب ما هو، ويختص هذا النوع باسم الإضافي كالأول بالحقيقي، وبينهما عموم من وجه لتصادقهما على الإنسان وتفارقهما في الحيوان والنقطة. ثم الأجناس قد تترتب متصاعدة إلى العالي كالجوهر ويسمى جنس الأجناس، والأنواع قد تترتب متنازلة إلى السافل ويسمى نوع الأنواع، وما بينهما متوسطات. الثالث الفصل، وهو المقول على الشيء في جواب أي شيء هو في ذاته، فإن ميزه عن المشاركات في الجنس القريب فقريب وإلا فبعيد، وإذا نسب إلى ما يميزه فمقوم وإلى ما يميزه عنه فمقسم، والمقوم للعالي مقوم للسافل ولا عكس، والمقسم بالعكس. الرابع الخاصة، وهو الخارج المقول على ما تحت حقيقة واحدة فقط. الخامس العرض العام، وهو الخارج المقول عليها وعلى غيرها، وكل منهما إن امتنع انفكاكه عن الشيء فلازم بالنظر إلى الماهية أو الوجود؛ بين يلزم تصوره من تصور الملزوم أو من تصورهما الجزم باللزوم، وغير بين بخلافه. وإلا فعرض مفارق يدوم أو يزول بسرعة أو بطء.

**English**

Second, species: what is predicated of many things agreeing in specific nature in answer to ‘What is it?’ The word also applies to a quiddity of which, and of something else, a genus is predicated; the latter is a relative species, the former a real species. They overlap in human nature, while ‘animal’ and ‘point’ distinguish their applications. Genera can ascend toward a highest genus such as substance; species descend toward a lowest species, with intermediate ranks between. Third, differentia: what is predicated in answer to ‘What sort of thing is it in itself?’ It is proximate when it distinguishes something from those sharing its proximate genus, otherwise remote. Relative to what it constitutes it is constitutive, and relative to what it divides from it is divisive. What constitutes a higher kind also constitutes its subordinate kind, but not conversely; for what divides, the direction is reversed. Fourth, property: an extrinsic predicable belonging exclusively under one specific nature. Fifth, common accident: an extrinsic predicable applicable also to other natures. Either may be inseparable from its subject, in which case it is a concomitant, considered through essence or existence. An evident concomitant is known as following from the conception of the subject alone or from conceiving subject and concomitant together; otherwise it is non-evident. What may separate is a separable accident, whether enduring or departing quickly or slowly.

**Notes**

- The real versus relative species distinction and the five predicables should not be confused with a theory of five independent constituents of substances.
- A specific difference answers what a thing is essentially; an exclusive property accompanies a single species without forming part of its essence, while a common accident may belong to different kinds.

## L.08: Three senses of ‘universal’

*Cairo pp. 7*

**Arabic**

خاتمة. مفهوم الكلي يسمى كلياً منطقياً ومعروضه طبيعياً والمجموع عقلياً، وكذا الأنواع الخمسة، والحق وجود الطبيعي بمعنى وجود أشخاصه.

**English**

Conclusion. The concept of universality is the logical universal; the nature of which universality is predicated is the natural universal; and the two considered together are the intellectual universal. The same distinction applies to the five predicables. The correct account of the natural universal’s existence is that its individual instances exist.

**Notes**

- Natural universality is not an additional independently subsisting universal: the existent nature is realised in its individuals; logical universality concerns its predicability.
- The logical universal is predicability of many; the natural universal is the nature to which universality applies; the intellectual universal consists in their conceptual conjunction.

## L.09: Definitional accounts

*Cairo pp. 7*

**Arabic**

فصل في المعرف وأقسامه. معرف الشيء ما يقال لإفادة تصوره، ويشترط أن يكون مساوياً وأجلى، فلا يصح بالأعم والأخص والمساوي في المعرفة والجهالة والأخفى. والتعريف بالفصل القريب حد وبالخاصة رسم، فإن كان مع الجنس القريب فتام وإلا فناقص، ولم يعتبروا العرض العام. وقد أجيز في الناقص أن يكون أعم كاللفظي وهو ما يصدق به تفسير مدلول اللفظ.

**English**

Chapter on accounts that make things known and their divisions. A defining account is what is said to furnish conception of the thing; it must be coextensive with its object and clearer. Hence one cannot properly define through what is more general or more specific, equally known or unknown, or less intelligible. Explanation by proximate differentia constitutes an essential definition (ḥadd), and by a property a descriptive account (rasm). Either is complete when joined to the proximate genus; otherwise it is incomplete. The common accident is not counted as the required element. An incomplete account is sometimes permitted to be more general, as with a verbal explanation clarifying the meaning signified by an expression.

**Notes**

- The requirement of clearer, coextensive definition applies to the scientific aim of conveying a conception, not to every informal explanation of a word.
- An adequate definition must be coextensive with, and clearer than, what it defines. Essential definition employs proximate differentia; descriptive definition employs a distinctive property.

## L.10: Judgments: categorical propositions, quantification, existential import

*Cairo pp. 7–8*

**Arabic**

فصل في التصديقات. القضية قول يحتمل الصدق والكذب، فإن كان الحكم فيها بثبوت شيء لشيء أو نفيه عنه فحملية موجبة أو سالبة، ويسمى المحكوم عليه موضوعاً والمحكوم به محمولاً، والدال على النسبة رابطة وقد استعير لها «هو»، وإلا فشرطية ويسمى الجزء الأول مقدماً والثاني تالياً. والموضوع إن كان شخصاً معيناً سميت القضية شخصية ومخصوصة، وإن كان نفس الحقيقة فطبيعية، وإلا فإن بين كمية أفراده كلا أو بعضاً فمحصورة كلية أو جزئية وما به البيان سور، وإلا فمهملة وتلازم الجزئية. ولا بد في الموجبة من وجود الموضوع إما محققاً فهي الخارجية أو مقدراً فالحقيقية أو ذهناً فالذهنية.

**English**

Chapter on judgments. A proposition [qaḍiyya] is a discourse capable of truth or falsity. If it judges that something belongs to something else or is denied of it, the proposition is categorical [ḥamliyya], affirmative or negative. The thing judged about is the subject [mawḍūʿ], and what is affirmed or denied is the predicate [maḥmūl]. What indicates their predicative relation is the copula [rābiṭa], for which the pronoun huwa (“is/he”) has been adopted [as a linguistic sign]. Otherwise the proposition is conditional [sharṭiyya], whose first component is the antecedent [muqaddam] and the second the consequent [tālī]. If the subject is a determinate individual the categorical proposition is singular [shakhṣiyya] or specific [makhṣūṣa]. If it is the nature itself, the proposition is natural [ṭabīʿiyya]. Otherwise, if the number of its individuals is specified as all or some, it is quantified [maḥṣūra], universal or particular, and the expression making the quantity explicit is the quantifier [sūr]; otherwise it is indeterminate [muhmala], equivalent [in inferential effect] to a particular proposition. An affirmative [categorical proposition] requires the subject to exist: either as actually realised, in which case the proposition is external [khārijiyya]; or as supposed/estimated, in which case it is real or essentially quantified [ḥaqīqiyya]; or mentally, in which case it is mental [dhihniyya].

**Notes**

- The proposition is truth-apt; categorical propositions affirm or deny predicates of subjects. The copula expresses their relationship, rather than supplying an additional independent object.

## L.11: Infinite-negative and modal categorical propositions

*Cairo pp. 8–9*

**Arabic**

وقد يجعل حرف السلب جزءاً من جزء فتسمى معدولة وإلا فمحصلة. وقد يصرح بكيفية النسبة فموجهة، وما به البيان جهة، وإلا فمطلقة. فإن كان الحكم فيها بضرورة النسبة ما دام ذات الموضوع موجودة فضرورية مطلقة، أو ما دام وصفه فمشروطة عامة، أو في وقت معين فوقتية مطلقة، أو غير معين فمنتشرة مطلقة، أو بدوامها ما دام الذات فدائمة مطلقة، أو ما دام الوصف فعرفية عامة، أو بفعليتها فمطلقة عامة، أو بعدم ضرورة خلافها فممكنة عامة. فهذه بسائط. وقد تقيد العامتان والوقتيتان باللادوام الذاتي فتسمى المشروطة الخاصة والعرفية الخاصة والوقتية والمنتشرة. وقد تقيد المطلقة العامة باللاضرورة الذاتية فتسمى الوجودية اللاضرورية، أو باللادوام الذاتي فتسمى الوجودية اللادائمة، وقد تقيد الممكنة العامة باللاضرورة من الجانب الموافق أيضاً فتسمى الممكنة الخاصة. وهذه مركبات لأن اللادوام إشارة إلى مطلقة عامة واللاضرورة إشارة إلى ممكنة عامة مخالفتي الكيفية وموافقتي الكمية لما قيد بهما.

**English**

A negation particle may be made part of one of the terms of a proposition, in which case it is an infinitated proposition (maʿdūla); otherwise it is non-infinitated (muḥaṣṣala). The manner of the predicative relation may be expressly stated, giving a modal proposition (muwajjaha); the expression stating that manner is its modality (jiha). Otherwise it is unqualified or absolute (muṭlaqa).

If it judges the relation necessary for as long as the subject itself exists, it is absolutely necessary (ḍarūriyya muṭlaqa); if for as long as the subject bears its specified description, it is generally conditioned (mashrūṭa ʿāmma); if at a specified time, absolutely temporal (waqtiyya muṭlaqa); if at some unspecified time, absolutely diffuse (muntashira muṭlaqa). If it judges the relation to endure for as long as the subject exists, it is absolutely permanent (dāʾima muṭlaqa); if for as long as the subject bears its description, generally customary (ʿurfiyya ʿāmma). If it affirms the actuality of the relation, it is generally absolute (muṭlaqa ʿāmma); if it denies the necessity of the opposing relation, generally possible (mumkina ʿāmma). These are the simple modal forms.

The two general forms—the conditioned and the customary—and the two temporal forms—the temporal and the diffuse—may be qualified by the nonpermanence of their attribution to the subject as such. They are then called, respectively, the specifically conditioned (mashrūṭa khāṣṣa), specifically customary (ʿurfiyya khāṣṣa), specifically temporal (waqtiyya), and specifically diffuse (muntashira). The generally absolute may be qualified by the absence of essential necessity, becoming existential nonnecessary (wujūdiyya lā ḍarūriyya), or by the absence of essential permanence, becoming existential nonpermanent (wujūdiyya lā dāʾima). The generally possible may be qualified by the absence of necessity on the corresponding side as well, becoming specifically possible (mumkina khāṣṣa).

These are compound modalities: a nonpermanence qualification amounts to a generally absolute proposition of the opposite quality but the same quantity as that which it qualifies; a nonnecessity qualification similarly amounts to a generally possible proposition of opposite quality and corresponding quantity.

**Notes**

- The eight basic modalizations differ in whether the qualification concerns necessity, temporal duration, the subject's description, actuality or the absence of necessity. The ‘specific’ forms add a negative modal qualification to a basic one. The opposition of quality under the same quantity is part of al-Taftāzānī’s formal account; it cannot be flattened into a generic distinction between ‘possible’ and ‘necessary’.
- The standard modal forms distinguish necessity, permanence, description-dependent predication and temporal possibility. Their traditional inferential behavior should not be replaced by a generic modern necessity operator.

## L.12: Conditional propositions

*Cairo pp. 9*

**Arabic**

فصل في أقسام الشرطية. الشرطية متصلة إن حكم فيها بثبوت نسبة على تقدير أخرى أو نفيها، لزومية إن كان ذلك بعلاقة وإلا فاتفاقية. ومنفصلة إن حكم فيها بتنافي النسبتين أو لا تنافيهما صدقاً وكذباً معاً وهي الحقيقية، أو صدقاً فقط فمانعة الجمع، أو كذباً فقط فمانعة الخلو. وكل منهما عنادية إن كان التنافي لذاتي الجزئين وإلا فاتفاقية. ثم الحكم في الشرطية إن كان على جميع تقادير المقدم فكلية، أو بعضها مطلقاً فجزئية، أو معيناً فشخصية، وإلا فمهملة. وطرفا الشرطية في الأصل قضيتان حمليتان أو متصلتان أو منفصلتان أو مختلفتان، إلا أنهما خرجتا بزيادة أداة الاتصال والانفصال عن التمام.

**English**

Chapter. A conditional proposition is connected [muttaṣila] if it judges that one relation holds, or does not hold, on the supposition of another. It is implicative [luzūmiyya] when there is a connection between the two relations; otherwise it is coincidental [ittifāqiyya]. It is disjunctive [munfaṣila] if it judges that the two relations are incompatible, or not incompatible, with respect to truth and falsity together, in which case it is the true disjunctive [ḥaqīqiyya]; or with respect to truth only, in which case it prevents conjunction [māniʿat al-jamʿ]; or with respect to falsity only, in which case it prevents joint exclusion [māniʿat al-khulū]. Each [of these disjunctive types] is essentially opposed [ʿinādiyya] if the exclusion arises from the natures of its two components, and otherwise is coincidental. Again, if the judgment in a conditional holds under all suppositions concerning the antecedent, it is universal; if under some, without specification, particular; if under a determinate supposition, singular; otherwise indeterminate. The two members of a conditional are originally two categorical propositions, two connected conditionals, two disjunctives, or two different kinds. The addition of the connective or disjunctive particle deprives them of [their independent] completeness.

**Notes**

- In a true disjunction, both conjunction and joint falsity are excluded. A preventing-conjunction disjunction excludes simultaneous truth; a preventing-exclusion disjunction excludes simultaneous falsity.

## L.13: Contradictory propositions

*Cairo pp. 3–14*

**Arabic**

فصل في التناقض. التناقض اختلاف القضيتين بحيث يلزم لذاته من صدق كل كذب الأخرى وبالعكس، ولا بد من الاختلاف في الكم والكيف والجهة والاتحاد فيما عداها، والنقيض الضرورية الممكنة العامة، والدائمة المطلقة العامة، والمشروطة العامة الحينية الممكنة، والعرفية العامة الحينية المطلقة، وللمركب المفهوم المردد بين نقيضي الجزأين، لكن في الجزئية بالنسبة إلى كل فرد.

**English**

Contradiction is the opposition of two propositions such that the truth of either requires the falsity of the other, and conversely. They differ in quantity, quality and modal qualification, but agree in the other respects. The contradictory of a necessary proposition is generally possible; of a permanent proposition, generally absolute; of a generally conditioned proposition, a temporally possible form; and of a generally customary proposition, a temporally absolute form. In the compound case the relevant sense alternates between the contradictories of its components, with the particular force assessed in relation to each individual.

**Notes**

- A contradiction must preserve sameness of subject, predicate and the other relevant qualifications. The named modal forms are technical traditional subtypes and should not be equated with only modern necessity and possibility.
- Traditional contradiction rules require attention to quantity, quality and modality under a fixed subject and predicate. A change of supposition or time can destroy the intended contradictory relation.

## L.14: Simple conversion (ʿaks mustawī)

*Cairo pp. 3–14*

**Arabic**

فصل. العكس المستوي تبديل طرفي القضية مع بقاء الصدق والكيف، والموجبة إنما تنعكس جزئية لجواز عموم المحمول أو التالي، والسالبة الكلية تنعكس كلية والإلزام سلب الشيء عن نفسه، والجزئية لا تنعكس أصلاً لجواز عموم الموضوع أو المقدم. وأما بحسب الجهة فمن الموجبات تنعكس الدائمتان والعامتان حينية مطلقة، والخاصتان حينية لا دائمة، والوقتيتان والوجوديتان والمطلقة العامة مطلقة عامة، ولا عكس للممكنتين. ومن السوالب تنعكس الدائمتان دائمة، والعامتان عرفية عامة، والخاصتان عرفية لا دائمة في البعض. والبيان في الكل أن نقيض العكس مع الأصل ينتج المحال، ولا عكس للبواقي بالنقض.

**English**

Simple conversion exchanges a proposition’s two terms while preserving truth and quality. An affirmative converts only to a particular affirmative, since its predicate—or a conditional’s consequent—may be more general. A universal negative converts universally; otherwise something would have to be denied of itself. A particular negative does not convert, since its subject—or antecedent—may be general. With respect to modality, the two permanent and two general affirmative forms convert into a temporally qualified absolute; the two special forms into a temporally qualified nonpermanent; and the temporal, existential and general absolute forms into a general absolute. Neither possibility form converts. Among negatives, the two permanent forms convert as permanent, the two general forms as generally customary, and the two special forms, in some cases, as customary nonpermanent. Each valid conversion can be demonstrated by deriving an impossibility from the original and the contradictory of its converse; the invalid cases are defeated by counterexample.

**Notes**

- The names ḥīniyya muṭlaqa, ʿurfiyya ʿāmma and ʿurfiyya lā dāʾima express distinct modal restrictions; the enumeration should not be replaced by an undifferentiated conversion rule.

## L.15: Conversion by contraposition (ʿaks al-naqīḍ)

*Cairo pp. 11*

**Arabic**

فصل. عكس النقيض تبديل نقيضي الطرفين مع بقاء الصدق والكيف، أو جعل نقيض الثاني أولاً مع مخالفة الكيف. وحكم الموجبات ههنا حكم السوالب في المستوي وبالعكس. والبيان البيان والنقض النقض. وقد بين انعكاس الخاصتين من الموجبة الجزئية ههنا ومن السالبة الجزئية ثمة إلى العرفية الخاصة بالافتراض فتأمل.

**English**

Chapter. Conversion by contraposition [ʿaks al-naqīḍ] consists in interchanging the contradictories of the two terms while retaining truth and quality; or in placing the contradictory of the second term first while reversing the quality [of the predication]. The rules for affirmative propositions here are those of negative propositions in simple conversion, and conversely. The proof is the same proof and the counterexample the same counterexample. By assuming an instance [bi-l-iftirāḍ], it has been shown that the two specific forms can be converted from the particular affirmative here, and from the particular negative there [under simple conversion], to the specific customary form. Reflect upon this.

## L.16: Syllogism, terms, figures, and the four sets of conditions

*Cairo pp. 3–14*

**Arabic**

فصل في القياس. القياس قول مؤلف من قضايا يلزم لذاته قول آخر، فإن كان مذكوراً فيه بمادته وهيئته فاستثنائي وإلا فاقتراني حملي أو شرطي. وموضوع المطلوب من الحملي يسمى أصغر ومحموله أكبر والمكرر أوسط، وما فيه الأصغر صغرى والأكبر كبرى والهيئة شكلاً. والأوسط إما محمول الصغرى أو موضوع الكبرى فهو الشكل الأول، أو محمولهما فالثاني، أو موضوعهما فالثالث، أو عكس الأول فالرابع. ويشترط في الأول إيجاب الصغرى وفعليتها وكلية الكبرى لينتج الموجبتان مع الموجبة والموجبتين ومع السالبة السالبتين بالضرورة. وفي الثاني اختلافهما في الكيف وكلية الكبرى، أما مع دوام الصغرى أو انعكاس سالبة الكبرى وكون الممكنة مع ضرورة أو مع كبرى مشروطة، لينتج الكليتان سالبة كلية والمختلفتان في الكم أيضاً سالبة جزئية بالخلف أو عكس الكبرى أو الترتيب ثم النتيجة. وفي الثالث إيجاب الصغرى وفعليتها مع كلية إحداهما لينتج الموجبتان مع الموجبة أو بالعكس موجبة جزئية، أو مع السالبة الكلية أو الكلية مع الجزئية سالبة جزئية بالخلف أو عكس الصغرى أو عكس الترتيب ثم النتيجة. وفي الرابع إيجابهما مع كلية الصغرى أو اختلافهما مع كلية إحداهما لينتج الموجبة الكلية مع الأربع، والجزئية مع السالبة الكلية، والسالبتان مع الموجبة الكلية، وكليتها مع الموجبة الجزئية جزئية موجبة إن لم تكن بسلب وإلا فسالبة، بالخلف أو بعكس الترتيب ثم النتيجة أو بعكس المقدمتين أو بالرد إلى الثاني بعكس الصغرى أو الثالث بعكس الكبرى.

**English**

Chapter. A syllogism [qiyās] is a discourse composed of propositions from which, by virtue of that composition itself, another proposition necessarily follows. If the resulting proposition is mentioned within it in matter and form [bi-māddatihi wa-hayʾatihi], the syllogism is exceptive [istisnāʾī]; otherwise it is conjunctive [iqtirānī], either categorical or conditional. In a categorical syllogism, the subject of the sought conclusion is called the minor term [al-aṣghar]; its predicate, the major term [al-akbar]; and what is repeated, the middle term [al-awsaṭ]. The premise containing the minor term is the minor premise [al-ṣughrā], and that containing the major term the major premise [al-kubrā]. The middle term may be the predicate of the minor and the subject of the major: this is the first figure. Or it may be the predicate of both: the second figure. Or it may be the subject of both: the third figure. Or the reverse of the first arrangement: the fourth figure. First figure. The minor must be affirmative and actual [fiʿliyya], together with a universal major. It then produces, with two affirmative premises and a universal affirmative major, an affirmative conclusion [universal or particular according to the quantity of the minor]; with a universal negative major, a negative conclusion [universal or particular according to the minor], necessarily. Second figure. The premises must differ in quality, and the major premise must be universal; together with permanence of the minor premise, or the possibility of converting the negative major, or the presence of a possible premise with a necessary one, or a conditional major. Two universal premises then yield a universal negative; and where their quantities also differ, a particular negative. This is shown by reductio [khalf], or by conversion of the major or the minor, followed by arranging [the converted premises] and deriving the conclusion. Third figure. The minor premise must be affirmative and actual, with at least one premise universal. Two affirmatives, with the major universal, or the reverse, yield a particular affirmative; and with a universal negative, or with a universal and a particular [of opposing qualities], they yield a particular negative. This is established by reductio, or by conversion of minor or major followed by arranging and deriving the conclusion. Fourth figure. The premises must both be affirmative with the minor universal, or must differ in quality with at least one universal. A universal affirmative joined with [any of] the four [appropriate forms], a particular affirmative with a universal negative, either of the two negative forms paired with a universal affirmative, and the universal [negative] with a particular affirmative yield a particular affirmative if the result is not obtained by negation, and otherwise a particular negative. Proof is by reductio, or by reversing the order and then converting the conclusion, or by converting both premises, or by reduction to the second figure through conversion of the minor, or to the third through conversion of the major.

**Notes**

- The first figure requires an affirmative and actual minor and a universal major; the second requires opposite quality and a universal major; the third an affirmative and actual minor and one universal premise. The fourth figure has additional mixed-quality and universality constraints which should be interpreted using its technical mood system, not a simple modern truth-table.
- The minor term is the subject of the conclusion, the major its predicate, and the middle term is repeated across both premises. The four figures are distinguished by the middle term’s position.
- The phrase «الموجبة الكلية مع الأربع» remains compressed in the printed matn. No unattested list of four cases is supplied here.
- The extended ‘governing rule for the four figures’ found in some commentary recensions is not present in the Cairo 1912 base text, whose authorial discussion ends after the reductions of the fourth figure. The separately witnessed formulation is not interpolated into the translation.

## L.17: Conjunctive conditional and exceptive syllogisms

*Cairo pp. 13*

**Arabic**

فصل. الشرطي من الاقتراني إما أن يتركب من متصلتين أو منفصلتين أو حملية ومتصلة أو حملية ومنفصلة أو متصلة ومنفصلة، وينعقد فيه الأشكال الأربعة وفي تفصيلها طول. فصل. الاستثنائي ينتج من المتصلة وضع المقدم ورفع التالي، والحقيقية بوضع كل كمانعة الجمع ورفعه كمانعة الخلو، وقد يختص باسم قياس الخلف ما يقصد به إثبات المطلوب بإبطال نقيضه، ومرجعه إلى استثنائي واقتراني.

**English**

Chapter. The conditional variety of conjunctive syllogism may be composed of two connected conditionals, two disjunctives, a categorical and a connected conditional, a categorical and a disjunctive, or a connected and a disjunctive proposition. The four figures can be formed in it. A detailed treatment would be lengthy. Chapter. The exceptive syllogism yields a conclusion, from a connected conditional, by affirming its antecedent or denying its consequent; from a true disjunctive, it yields a conclusion when either member is affirmed, as in the preventing-conjunction form, or denied, as in the preventing- exclusion form. It may specifically be called a syllogism by reductio [qiyās al-khalf]: this is the procedure that aims to establish what is sought by invalidating its contradictory. It reduces to a combination of an exceptive and a conjunctive syllogism.

## L.18: Induction, analogy, and the five kinds of syllogistic discourse

*Cairo pp. 13–14*

**Arabic**

فصل. الاستقراء تصفح الجزئيات لإثبات حكم كلي، والتمثيل بيان مشاركة جزئي لآخر في علة الحكم ليثبت فيه، والعمدة في طريقه الدوران والترديد. فصل. القياس إما برهاني يتألف من اليقينيات وأصولها الأوليات والمشاهدات والتجربيات والحدسيات والمتواترات والنظريات، ثم إن كان الأوسط مع عليته للنسبة في الذهن علة لها في الواقع فلمّي وإلا فإنّي، وإما جدلي يتألف من المشهورات والمسلمات، وإما خطابي يتألف من المقبولات والمظنونات، وإما شعري يتألف من المخيلات، وإما سفسطي يتألف من الوهميات والمشبهات.

**English**

Chapter. Induction [istiqrāʾ] is a survey of particulars in order to establish a universal judgment. Analogy [tamthīl] is the disclosure that one particular shares with another the cause of a judgment, in order to establish that judgment of the former. The principal basis of its method is concomitant variation [dawarān] and disjunctive elimination [tardīd]. Chapter. A syllogism is either demonstrative [burhānī], being composed of certainties [yaqīniyyāt]. Their fundamental kinds are first principles [awwaliyyāt], direct observations [mushāhadāt], experiential judgments [tajribiyyāt], intuitive judgments [ḥadsiyyāt], multiply transmitted judgments [mutawātirāt], and theoretical judgments [naẓariyyāt] (Cairo reading). If the middle term is a cause of the predicative relation not only in the mind but also in reality, the demonstration is demonstration of why [limmī]; otherwise it is demonstration of that [innī]. Or a syllogism is dialectical [jadalī], composed of generally accepted and conceded premises [mashhūrāt, musallamāt]; rhetorical [khiṭābī], composed of accepted on authority and probable premises [maqbūlāt, maẓnūnāt]; poetic [shiʿrī], composed of imaginative premises [mukhayyalāt]; or sophistical [safsaṭī], composed of estimative and deceptively similar premises [wahmiyyāt, mushabbahāt].

**Notes**

- Cairo 1912, printed p. 13, reads «والنظريات»; the al-Yazdī textual tradition reads «والفطريات». The latter fits the usual sixfold account of certain premises and the parallel treatment in Sharḥ al-Maqāṣid. The Cairo copy-text is retained with the alternative recorded.

## L.19: Conclusion: the constituents of a science (Cairo 1912)

*Cairo pp. 3–14*

**Arabic**

فصل. أجزاء العلوم ثلاثة: الموضوعات، وهي التي يطلب في العلم عن أعراضها الذاتية، والمبادئ وهي حدود الموضوعات وأجزاؤها وأعراضها ومقدمات بينة أو مأخوذة يبتنى عليها قياسات العلم، والمسائل وهي قضايا تطلب في العلم وموضوعاتها موضوع العلم أو نوع منه أو عرض ذاتي له أو متركب، ومحمولاتها أمور خارجة عنها لاحقة لذاتها، وقد تقال المبادئ لما يبدأ به قبل المقصود، والمقدمات أيضا لما يتوقف عليه الشروع بوجه الخبرة، والله أعلم بالصواب.

**English**

The constituents of the sciences are three: subjects, principles and investigated problems. Subjects are those things whose essential properties the science investigates. Principles include the definitions of subjects, their parts and attributes, and self-evident or borrowed premises on which the science’s arguments are built. Problems are propositions investigated in the science: their subjects are the subject matter of the discipline itself, a species of it, one of its essential properties, or a compound of these; their predicates are matters external to those subjects yet attaching to them in virtue of their nature. The word ‘principles’ may also designate what is presented before the principal aim, and ‘preliminaries’ what one must know in order to undertake an inquiry competently. God knows best what is correct.

**Notes**

- This is the conclusion of the Cairo 1912 logic text. The longer discussion of the eight introductory headings appears in the separately typeset commentary tradition and must not be interpolated into this base witness.
- The five syllogistic arts are classified by the epistemic standing of their premises and the ends of their use; demonstration differs from dialectic, rhetoric, poetics and sophistry in more than persuasive force.
