Where does Ṭabāṭabāʾī’s theory of definition come from?

The precise thesis: existence is not a constituent of every definition

Bidāyat al-ḥikma, stage I, chapter 1, says: «مفهوم الوجود بديهي معقول بنفس ذاته ... فلا معرف له من حد أو رسم». Its meaning is that the concept of existence is immediately intelligible and cannot receive an essential or descriptive definition from something more evident. The argument also invokes the absence from existence of genus, differentia, and proper accident in the logical sense. The parallel early sections of Nihāya develop the same ontological assumptions.

This is not a prescription to insert the word “exists” into every definition. Quite the opposite: an ordinary contingent quiddity such as human being can be understood without its existence being part of the quiddity. The conceptual formula “rational animal” need not entail that any human being is actually present. Thus the following are distinct: the concept of existence, the existence of a defined thing, and the essential components of its quiddity. [1] [2]

Which logic texts are actually taught?

There is no single invariant Iranian seminary syllabus. Across modern and traditional routes, two introductory-to-intermediate books are central: Muḥammad Riḍā al-Muẓaffar’s al-Manṭiq and Mullā ʿAbd Allāh al-Yazdī’s Ḥāshiya on al-Taftāzānī’s Tahdhīb al-Manṭiq. A specialist historical comparison states that both remain materials for study, while Muẓaffar has increasingly replaced the older text in some Qum institutions. It is inaccurate to present all the historically important books below as mandatory companions to every Bidāya course. [3] [4]

Muẓaffar’s chapter headings are a particularly close answer to the question. They separate al-kulliyyāt al-khams, al-muʿarrif, shurūṭ al-taʿrīf, and, later, the difference between al-dhātī in the book of demonstration and in the introductory five-predicables discussion. The exact sequence makes clear that these ideas are taught as logic, prior to their application in metaphysics. [5]

The genealogy: Aristotle → Avicenna → the later handbooks

1. Aristotelian and Porphyrian inheritance

Aristotle’s Posterior Analytics II investigates the questions whether something is, what it is, and why it is so; Porphyry’s five predicables provide the familiar classification of genus, species, differentia, proper attribute, and common accident. The theory of demonstrative science and the taxonomy of definitions have related but nonidentical origins. Avicenna reworks both.

2. Avicenna: the fundamental source

Al-Shifāʾ, Ilāhiyyāt I.5 treats “existent,” “thing,” and “necessary” as fundamental notions present to thought. Al-Shifāʾ, Madkhal treats essences as what they are without an existential judgment being included in the quiddity. The Ishārāt says that the logician must know how explanatory discourse is constructed and how arguments lead from known to unknown. These passages establish the immediate historical source of Ṭabāṭabāʾī’s opening distinctions. [6] [7] [8]

Al-Shifāʾ, Burhān goes further: a merely verbal or nominal explanation, a real definition, a definition supplying a cause, and a demonstration of existence are different epistemic achievements. The book examines cases where a middle term explaining the cause supplies material of a scientific definition. This is the serious follow-up text—not merely an expanded list of types of definition. [9] [10]

3. The two al-Rāzīs must be distinguished

Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī (d. 1210) enters the picture as a critic and developer of Avicennian metaphysics and logic, notably through his Sharḥ al-Ishārāt and other systematic works. He is not the originator of the basic rule requiring a definition to be cognitively prior or clearer.

Quṭb al-Dīn al-Rāzī al-Taḥtānī (d. 1365) is a different philosopher, responsible for the well-known Sharḥ al-Shamsiyya. Confusing the two would obscure the historical lineage of the textbooks.

4. Later Iranian elaboration

Sabzawārī’s Sharḥ al-Manẓūma brings formal logical distinctions directly into the later metaphysics curriculum. The extended discussion of the relationship between definition and proof is a strong bridge from the elementary seminary logic books to the epistemological questions that matter in Nihāya. [11]

Four distinctions to preserve in translation

Essential definition (ḥadd) versus description (rasm)

Ḥadd tāmm ordinarily consists of proximate genus and proximate differentia; ḥadd nāqiṣ uses fewer essential constituents, typically the proximate differentia. Rasm defines through a characteristic outside the essence (often together with a genus). Some historical uses of “ḥadd” are broader, especially where definition and demonstration are being compared. A translator must not impose the narrow genus–differentia sense in every context. [11]

Defining the concept versus demonstrating an instance

A correct definition can allow us to understand the meaning of “phoenix,” but that does not prove its extramental existence. This is why nominal or explanatory definition is distinct from genuine scientific understanding of an existing subject and its causes. Conversely, “this exists” is a judgment, not itself a genus or differentia constituting the object’s quiddity. [10]

Primary conceptual clarity versus ontological transparency

The concept of existence may be immediately understood while the reality of existence and its modes remain difficult metaphysical problems. A lecture can help a student distinguish these two registers, but the distinction should be anchored first in Ṭabāṭabāʾī and Avicenna.

Negations and privations: the special rule

In Sabzawārī’s logic, the rule concerning a definition of negation requires stating the positive term relative to which absence is understood. “Blindness” is not just “nothing”: it is the absence of sight in the relevant kind of subject. That does not entail that every positive entity must have “existence” inserted as an element of its essence. The distinction is crucial if the two discussions are placed side by side. [12]

Two source-checked primary-text translations

The following are independent short translations of excerpts from Ibn Sīnā, not quotations from an existing English edition.

Avicenna, al-Shifāʾ, Ilāhiyyāt I.5 — the primitive concepts

إن الموجود، والشيء، والضروري، معانيها ترتسم في النفس ارتساماً أولياً

“The existent, the thing, and the necessary are meanings impressed upon the soul in a primary manner.”

The longer argument explains that such concepts do not require another concept that is clearer than themselves. The attempted definition can instead serve as a reminder. Source: Arabic witness, p. 29. The distinction between the content of these concepts and particular instances is essential.

Avicenna, al-Ishārāt wa-l-tanbīhāt, logic — explanatory discourse

أن يعرف مبادئ القول الشارح وكيفية تأليفه حداً كان أو غيره

“To know the principles of explanatory discourse and how to construct it, whether it is a definition or something else.”

The source establishes a logic of how to pass from what is already known to a previously unknown conception; a definition is one kind of explanatory discourse. Source: Ishārāt, logic, opening sections. The selected clauses do not constitute a translation of the complete work.

What should be translated for the site?

The smallest coherent logic dossier that would genuinely help a reader of Bidāya and Nihāya is not a full new translation of every historical logic book. It is a sequence of complementary primary-text selections, kept distinct from newly written explanation:

  1. Muẓaffar, al-Manṭiq, sections on the five predicables, kinds of definition, and conditions of definition (linked digital pp. 86–105 and 110–123). The reader can link to this published source without reproducing a substantial copyrighted passage.
  2. Avicenna, al-Shifāʾ, Ilāhiyyāt I.5, on the primitive notions of existence, thingness, and necessity; a short source-checked translation belongs alongside Bidāya I.1.
  3. Avicenna, al-Ishārāt, opening logic, on the explanatory discourse and demonstrative inference; translate short passages with source references and compare with Muẓaffar.
  4. Avicenna, al-Shifāʾ, al-Burhān, discussion of definition, on nominal explanation, real definition, causal definition, and how demonstration yields scientific knowledge. This is the most important advanced extension.
  5. Sabzawārī, logic portion of Sharḥ al-Manẓūma, the passages on ḥadd al-ism, sharing of definition and demonstration, and defining a negation through the positive term.
  6. Selected discussion from the Tahdhīb / Ḥāshiya Mullā ʿAbd Allāh and Sharḥ al-Shamsiyya only where they add a genuinely different argument or technical refinement. The existing Gnamma Tahdhīb al-Manṭiq reader is already available as a starting point.

Suggested cross-links in the combined reader: Bidāya I.1 ↔ Muẓaffar’s conditions ↔ Avicenna Ilāhiyyāt I.5; Bidāya V.2 ↔ logic of the three considerations of quiddity; Bidāya XI ↔ the predication and knowledge issues; and Nihāya’s discussion of demonstration ↔ al-Burhān. Mark unexamined source passages as suggested reading, not as completed translated apparatus.
