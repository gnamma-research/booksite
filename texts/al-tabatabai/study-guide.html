How the two books relate

Bidāya is the earlier systematic course; Nihāya revisits the same broad field at a more demanding level. “Beginning” should not be mistaken for an elementary introduction requiring no preparation. Both texts assume that the reader can distinguish definitions, premises, conclusions, objections, and replies. ʿAbd al-Jabbār al-Rifāʿī explicitly places logical training before this sequence and describes the second book as a deepening of the first. [1]

The common subject is the existent insofar as it is existent. The books work through existence and quiddity, modality, categories, unity, causation, change, knowledge, and the Necessary Existent. This makes them systematic metaphysical courses, rather than chronological histories of philosophy. Their chapter structures are independently inspectable in the Arabic contents. [2] [3]

The progression is best understood through the work demanded of the reader. First learn to reconstruct an argument and its technical vocabulary. Then ask which premises it needs, whether an objection reaches those premises, and how an answer changes the scope of the claim. This is a reading method for the pair, not a claim that every later chapter merely repeats an earlier one.

Chapter counts do not measure difficulty. Nihāya has fewer numbered chapters than Bidāya in several corresponding stages. Its first stage has five rather than twelve, while its category stage has twenty-one rather than eleven. Some discussions are consolidated; others are expanded. The table below records structure, not a quantitative measure of philosophical sophistication.

The translation likewise needs stable distinctions. Fī nafsihi, li-nafsihi, and bi-nafsihi are “in itself,” “for itself,” and “through itself.” Māhiyya is “quiddity.” Iʿtibārī varies with context: derivative ontological status, intellectual consideration, and practical convention are not interchangeable. These are editorial translation choices explained in the reader’s glossary.

Match topics, then chapters

The order changes in three important places: mental existence and linking existence exchange positions; unity and causation exchange positions; motion and priority exchange positions. A citation to a Roman numeral in one book should therefore never be transferred automatically to the other. [2] [3]

What comes after Nihāya in Iran?

The usual major continuation in philosophy is Mullā Ṣadrā’s al-Ḥikma al-mutaʿāliya fī al-asfār al-ʿaqliyya al-arbaʿa, normally called the Asfār. Al-Rifāʿī gives the progression from Bidāya through Nihāya into study of the Asfār. It is a useful orientation, but it should not be represented as one compulsory national reading list followed identically by every seminary student. [1]

Logic → Bidāya → Nihāya → selected or extended study of the Asfār

There is direct evidence for studying selected portions. Recollections published by the Beheshti Foundation describe classes with Javādī Āmulī covering Bidāya and Nihāya alongside portions of the Asfār: volume I on existence, volume III on motion, and volume VIII on the soul. The same recollections also mention earlier study of Sabzawārī’s Sharḥ al-Manẓūma and classes on the Ishārāt. This is evidence of an actual teaching route, not a regulation binding every institution. [4]

A documented institutional example: 1404–1405 SH / 2025–26

Navvab Seminary in Mashhad provides a concrete example in its 1404–1405 SH (2025–26) admissions booklet. Its specialised level-four philosophy entrance list includes Nihāyat al-ḥikma, al-Shawāhid al-rubūbiyya, and sections IV–V of Sharḥ al-Ishārāt. The programme lists four ten-credit courses in Transcendent Wisdom, alongside Peripatetic philosophy, Illuminationist philosophy, Islamic mysticism, and a thesis. These entries show the move into specialisation. They do not establish that a particular Asfār volume is compulsory nationally. [5]

The theoretical ʿirfān sequence

A related but distinct course of study is theoretical mysticism. The Majmaʿ-e ʿĀlī-ye Ḥikmat-e Islāmī’s official channel transmits Ḥasan-zāda Āmulī’s sequence: Tamhīd al-Qawāʿid, then Qayṣarī’s Sharḥ Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam, then Fanārī’s Miṣbāḥ al-uns, the commentary on Qūnawī’s text. This sequence explains where Tamhīd belongs; it should not replace the philosophy progression after Nihāya. [6]

How I would use the next stage

After a careful reading of Nihāya, choose an Asfār discussion of existence with a competent teacher, and compare the premises, objections, and conclusions against Nihāya’s corresponding stage. Continue into motion and the soul as the course permits. Keep a record of places where Ṭabāṭabāʾī condenses, reformulates, or questions a Ṣadrian argument. This is an editorial study recommendation, informed by the documented teaching route above.

The existing English editions

Bidāya already has a published English translation: The Elements of Islamic Metaphysics (Bidāyat al-Ḥikmah), translated by Sayyid ʿAlī Qulī Qarāʾī, ICAS Press. The publisher currently advertises a fully revised second edition with explanatory notes and a technical glossary. The website links to that edition; it does not reproduce its English text. [7]

An English Nihāya translation has been announced: The Pursuit of Philosophy: A Comprehensive Text on Metaphysics, Ontology, and Theology, translated and explicated by Sayyid Amjad H. Shah Naqavi, The Shīʿah Institute Press. The publisher’s biography, which lists publications through 2026, still marks it forthcoming. No released complete edition was verified for this project. The new working translation on this site is independent of that announced work. [8]

English source for Muẓaffar

Shaykh ʿAbdul-Ḥakīm Carney’s user-uploaded English Logic is now cited by PDF page in the Muẓaffar reader. It is a 75-page partial translation: pp. 2–37 cover preliminary logic and universals, pp. 38–71 develop syllogistic reasoning, and pp. 71–74 cover induction and analogy. It does not contain a continuous translated chapter on the full theory of definition or the later five logical arts. See the exact coverage and terminology audit.

The notes at PDF pp. 10–12 distinguish external, mental, spoken and written existence in an analysis of signification; this prepares, but should not be identified with, the Ṣadrian doctrine that quiddities are modes or limits of existence.

The logic behind the two books

The relation of ḥadd, quiddity, and existence is inherited from the Avicennian theory of definition. It is taught especially clearly in al-Muẓaffar’s al-Manṭiq, through the five universals, the four kinds of defining account, and the questions mā, hal, and limā. The metaphysical conclusion that existence itself cannot be defined, because it has no genus or differentia, should be distinguished from that logical framework.

About this website edition

The Nihāya reader contains the original authorial text in twelve stages, with 130 numbered chapters. The author’s introduction and five stage introductions bring the reading-unit count to 136. The English is a fresh, AI-assisted working translation. It preserves argumentative prose rather than replacing chapters with summaries; explanatory and textual notes are separately labelled.

The early printed witness is the Qum 1404 AH edition supplied by Princeton through Arabic Collections Online. NYU identifies that digitized Arabic material as public domain. The searchable transcription follows the later Qum 1417 AH edition and supplies the page locators beneath the reader’s sections. These two pagination systems differ. [9] [3]

The later publisher’s and editor’s introductions, editorial footnotes, bibliography, and indexes are excluded. Selected difficult readings have been checked visually against the early scan; these checks are recorded where they matter. The entire transcription has not been exhaustively collated against that scan, and neither a manuscript-critical edition nor completed independent specialist review is claimed.

For Bidāya, the current website provides original reading guidance and the structural correspondence, with four numbered authorial chapters plus the author’s introduction represented as newly translated. The user-supplied Arabic–English edition supplies all 110 Arabic chapters; a fresh English translation is underway. The included published English has not been copied. Online availability of an edition has not been treated as permission to republish it.

Use the reader’s “About this edition” panel for source details and current coverage. Bookmarks and personal notes remain in your browser. They do not alter the public text.
