Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK112.02

Text and explanation

T XVI 632–633; K IV 817

Huwa [He/it] is the pronoun of the matter (ḍamīr al-shaʾn), and “God is One” is that matter, as when you say, “It is: Zayd is departing.” It is as though one had said: the matter is this—namely, that God is one, with no second beside Him. If you ask, “What is the syntactic position of huwa?”, I answer: nominative, as the subject (mubtadaʾ); its predicate is the sentence. If you ask, “A sentence functioning as a predicate must contain something referring back to the subject. Where, then, is that reference?”, I answer: this sentence has the same status as the single predicate in your statement “Zayd is your servant-boy”: in meaning, it is the subject itself. For “God is One” is precisely the matter expressed by huwa. This is not the case with “Zayd—his father is departing,” since Zayd and the sentence denote two different meanings; something connecting them is therefore indispensable. Ibn ʿAbbās reports: Quraysh said, “Muḥammad, describe to us your Lord, to whom you summon us,” and the sūrah was revealed. That is: the one whose description you have asked me for is God. Aḥad is a substitution (badal) for “God”; alternatively, [it is construed with an understood subject] “He is One.” It has the meaning of wāḥid, and its original form is waḥad.

On K112.02

Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ. Meccan; it is also said to be Medinan. Four verses. In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful. His words, “The one whose description you asked me for is God, and aḥad is a substitution.” Abū al-Baqāʾ says: “Huwa is a subject meaning ‘the one asked about,’ for they said, ‘Is your Lord made of copper or gold?’ On this interpretation, Allāh may be the predicate of the subject, with aḥad a substitution, or the predicate of an omitted subject. Allāh may also be a substitution, with aḥad the predicate. The hamza of aḥad replaces wāw, since it means al-wāḥid.” Substitution of hamza for a wāw carrying fatḥa is rare. It is also said that the hamza is original, like the hamza in aḥad used for generality.

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT112.02

“It has the meaning of wāḥid”

XVI 633–634 | on K112.02
On K112.02

His words, “It has the meaning of wāḥid.” There are two possibilities. First, this may attach to the second interpretation: huwa answers their request, “Describe your Lord to us,” and is the pronoun referring to the one asked about. In that case, a distinction must be drawn between wāḥid and aḥad. He says in al-Aḥzāb: “Aḥad originally means waḥad, that is, al-wāḥid; then it was assigned to general negation, applying equally to masculine and feminine, one and more than one.” The author of al-Nihāya transmits from al-Azharī: “The difference between al-wāḥid and al-aḥad is that aḥad was formed to negate whatever number is mentioned with it: you say, ‘No one came to me.’ Wāḥid is a name formed for the beginning of number: you say, ‘One person came to me,’ but do not say [in this affirmative construction], ‘Aḥad came to me.’ Al-wāḥid is unique in essence, without likeness or counterpart; al-aḥad is unique in meaning. It is also said: al-wāḥid is that which is not divisible, is not doubled, does not admit division, and has neither counterpart nor likeness. Only God Most High combines these two descriptions.” Al-Azharī says in Tafsīr asmāʾ Allāh al-ḥusnā: “Al-aḥad is among the attributes God has reserved to Himself: nothing shares it with Him, and nothing other than God is described as al-aḥad. One does not say ‘an aḥad man’ or ‘an aḥad dirham’; one says ‘a wāḥid man.’” Knowing this, we say: when they said, “Describe to us your Lord, to whom you summon us,” they were told: the one asked about is God, one, unique in essence, without likeness or counterpart. The discourse thus serves to distinguish, and the description differentiates. Although it entails magnification, [when] huwa is taken as the pronoun of the matter, the descriptions serve magnification alone. For this is the beginning of the Messenger's ﷺ mission, guiding the people and drawing attention to an object of worship of immense dignity and overwhelming dominion. It is as though one had said: Say, Muḥammad, the matter and affair is that God is One, with no second beside Him. Allāh indicates all the attributes of perfection; aḥad, all the attributes of majesty. Accordingly, it is appropriate to say “One, with no second beside Him,” because aḥad indicates the negation of whatever is mentioned with it. The second possibility is that [his explanation] attaches to both interpretations together: either huwa is the pronoun of the matter, or huwa means the one asked about. There would then be no distinction between aḥad and wāḥid. Al-Jawharī says, “Al-aḥad means al-wāḥid, and is the beginning of number.” The author of al-Nihāya says, “Al-wāḥid is the solitary one who has always been alone, with no other beside Him.” [TEXT CORRECTIONS T112-C1: The scan at p.633 reads لا يتجزأ and إلا الله, correcting the transcription. ʿAbd Allāh and Ubayy read “Huwa Allāhu aḥad,” without “Say.” In the Prophet's ﷺ reading it is “Allāhu aḥad,” without “Say: He”; and he said, “Whoever recites ‘Allāhu aḥad’—it is equivalent to the Qurʾān.” Al-Aʿmash read “Qul huwa Allāhu al-wāḥid.” It has also been read “aḥadu llāhu,” without tanwīn: the tanwīn is dropped because it meets the lām of the definite article. Al-Ṣamad is on the pattern faʿal with the meaning of a passive participle (mafʿūl), from ṣamada ilayhi, “he directed himself towards him.” It is the master to whom people turn with their needs. Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT112.04

“Nor remembering God, except a little”

XVI 635 | on K112.03
On K112.03

His words, “Nor remembering God, except a little.” Its opening is: “I found him not returning to goodwill under reproach.” That is: I reminded him. “Nor remembering” is intended with tanwīn; it was dropped because two unvocalized consonants met. Allāh consequently remains accusative, not genitive through annexation (iḍāfa). Dhākir is genitive, coordinated with mustaʿtib: “nor remembering.” That is: I reminded him of the affection formerly between us, but he was found not to turn back, through reproach, from the ugliness of what he had done. His words, “The sounder usage is tanwīn”: this is the well-known reading. His words, “The master to whom people turn with their needs.” Al-Zajjāj cited al-Asadī: “Early came the death-announcer with news of the two best of Banū Asad: ʿAmr ibn Masʿūd and the master to whom all turn.” Al-Ṣamad: that is, the one to whom everything turns—the one who created all things, and whom nothing can do without. Muḥyī al-Sunna transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, al-Ḥasan and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr: “Al-Ṣamad is the one with no interior cavity.” Al-Shaʿbī said: “The one who neither eats nor drinks.”

Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK112.04

Text and explanation

T XVI 636; K IV 818

The meaning is: He is the God whom you know, and whom you acknowledge as the creator of the heavens and the earth and your own creator. He is one, alone in divinity, with no partner in it. He is the one to whom every creature turns; they cannot dispense with Him, whereas He has no need of them. “He did not beget,” because He shares no genus with anything, such that He might have a consort of His own genus and the two might produce offspring. His statement “How could He have a child, when He has no consort?” [6:101] indicates this meaning. “Nor was He begotten,” because everything begotten is originated and a body; He, however, is pre-eternal (qadīm), with no beginning to His existence, and is not a body. No one equals Him: that is, no one resembles Him or is like Him. It is also possible to take this from suitability between spouses (kafāʾa) in marriage, thereby denying Him a consort.

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT112.05

“His statement ‘How could He have a child, when He has no consort?’ indicates this meaning”

XVI 636 | on K112.03–04
On K112.03, K112.04

Al-Rāghib: “That which has no interior cavity is of two kinds: what is below the human being, such as inanimate things; and what is above, namely the Creator, exalted and holy. The purpose of al-Ṣamad is to indicate that He differs from those to whom they ascribed divinity. Something of this kind is indicated by ‘His mother was a woman of truth; they both used to eat food’ [5:75].” His words, “His statement ‘How could He have a child, when He has no consort?’ indicates this meaning”: these are coordinated with “because He shares no genus.” That is, “He did not beget” either indirectly signifies (kināya) His transcendence above belonging to a genus—for whoever shares a genus with anything takes a consort of his genus, and whoever takes a consort produces offspring—or [the reasoning proceeds] in reverse: how could He have a child when He has taken no consort? Generation occurs only between two partners of one genus. He transcends having a congener, so a consort cannot properly be ascribed to Him, and neither can generation. He states this in explaining the verse in al-Anʿām.

Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK112.05

Text and explanation

T XVI 636, 638; K IV 818

They asked him to describe Him to them, and what encompasses His attributes was revealed to him. “He is God” points them to the creator and originator of things. Implicit in that is His description as powerful and knowing, since creation requires power and knowledge, occurring as it does with the utmost precision, coherence and order. This also describes Him as living, hearing and seeing. “One” describes His oneness and denies partners. “Al-Ṣamad” describes Him as exclusively the one who is needed; if He is exclusively needed [and not needy], He is self-sufficient. His being self-sufficient together with His being knowing entails that He is just and does not perform evil acts: He knows the evil of the evil act, and knows that He has no need of it. “Nor was He begotten” describes pre-eternity and primordiality. “He did not beget” denies likeness and a shared genus. “No one is equal to Him” confirms this and settles the judgment definitively.

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT112.06

“Thus His statement ‘He is God.’”

XVI 636–637 | on K112.05
On K112.05

His words, “Thus His statement ‘He is God.’” The fāʾ introduces a detailed exposition; the general statement being expounded is “what encompasses His attributes.” Since Allāh names the essence—and in the Opening of the Book he established the impossibility of its being a descriptive epithet—it nevertheless has in each context a meaning appropriate to that context. The particular character of the polytheists' question required its explanation as “the Creator,” because of His statement, “If you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will certainly say: God” [31:25]. Thus Allāh here, as an answer, points them to the one who creates things. You know that knowledge and power are what make creatorship possible: these two attributes are therefore included under it. This is what “implicit in that is His description as powerful and knowing” indicates. He cannot be powerful and knowing without being knowing, living, hearing and seeing. Then these descriptions were followed by the meaning of oneness in “One.” Since uniqueness excludes recourse to another, He was affirmed to possess ṣamadiyya, so that recourse would be to Him. Once this established an essence entailing the attributes of creatorship, knowledge, power, life and divinity, their perfection was to be explained, together with their difference from creatures' attributes in past and future. At this point it was said: “He neither begot nor was begotten, and no one is equal to Him.”

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT112.07

Commentary

XVI 637–638 | continuation
On K112.05

Ḥujjat al-Islām gives a concise discussion of them: “‘Aḥad’: He is the wāḥid from whom partnership is excluded, and the aḥad in whom there is no composition. Wāḥid negates partner and likeness; aḥad negates multiplicity within His essence. Al-Ṣamad is the self-sufficient one whom others need. He is aḥad in essence and wāḥid in attributes: for if He had a partner in His dominion, He would not be self-sufficient and needed by others; rather, He would depend for His subsistence and existence on the components of His composition. Thus ṣamadiyya proves both wāḥidiyya and aḥadiyya. “‘He did not beget’ proves that His continuing existence is not like human existence, whose species persists through generation and reproduction; rather, His existence continues eternally, without beginning or end. ‘Nor was He begotten’ proves that His existence is not like the existence of the human soul, which comes into being after nonexistence and then remains forever, either in a lofty Garden that does not perish or in an abyss that does not end. “‘No one is equal to Him’ proves that His true existence is an existence that bestows existence on another without receiving existence from another. Thus ‘He is God, One’ proves His holy, transcendent essence. Ṣamadiyya entails His freedom from need and others' need of Him. ‘Nor was He begotten,’ at the end of the sūrah, removes from Him what characterizes others. There is no clearer path to knowledge of God Most High than removing from Him the attributes of creatures.” [REVIEW T112-U1: The scan prints “nor was He begotten” in the last sentence, although it is not the sūrah's final clause. It is preserved. The wording about partnership, composition and attributes also needs comparison with the work being quoted.]

Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK112.06

Text and explanation

T XVI 638–639; K IV 818–819

If you ask, “In eloquent Arabic, an adjunct (ẓarf) that is supplementary (laghw), rather than indispensable to the predication (mustaqirr), is postponed, not placed first—Sībawayh explicitly says so in his book. Why, then, is it placed first in the most eloquent and most fully Arabic of speech?”, I answer: this utterance is directed precisely at denying equivalence to the essence of the Creator—glory be to Him. This adjunct [lahu, ‘to Him’] is where that meaning converges and is centred. It is therefore the most important and most pertinent element, the one most entitled and most suited to precedence. Kufuʾan has been read with ḍamma on both kāf and fāʾ, and also with ḍamma or kasra on the kāf together with an unvocalized fāʾ [kufʾan, kifʾan].

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT112.08

“Exclusively the one who is needed”

XVI 638 | on K112.05–06
On K112.05, K112.06

His words, “Exclusively the one who is needed”: the exception is unfilled (istithnāʾ mufarragh); that is, God is exclusively the one who is needed—relative, that is, to creatures. His words, “Supplementary, not indispensable to the predication.” An indispensable adjunct (ẓarf mustaqirr) is one required to complete the utterance, as when it functions as the predicate in “There was not in it anyone better than you.” A supplementary adjunct (laghw) is one without which the utterance is complete, as in “No one was better than you in it.” In the first case, the indispensable adjunct is put first because it is intended as the focus. The normal arrangement was abandoned in the verse because it was directed at explaining divine oneness. Ibn al-Ḥājib says: “It was placed first out of concern for the correspondence of verse endings. Had it merely preceded aḥad, the aim would have been achieved; but it would then have separated the two parts which are [the predicate and] the subject of predication. It was therefore placed before both, and the aim was achieved.” [REVIEW T112-U2: The printed wording is اللذين هما مسند إليه, apparently incomplete as a description of the two parts. The bracketed supplement conveys the apparent argument but requires checking the quoted source.]

On K112.06

The author of al-Intiṣāf says: “Sībawayh reports hearing a rough Arab recite ‘wa-lam yakun aḥadun kufuwan lahu.’ The uncouth man followed his habitual manner, his coarse disposition missing the subtle meaning that required the adjunct and predicate to precede the subject. His words, “Kufuʾan has been read, with ḍamma on kāf.” Ḥafṣ reads with ḍamma on it and on fāʾ, without hamza [kufuwan]. Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK112.07

Text and explanation

T XVI 640–642; K IV 819

If you ask, “Why is this sūrah the equivalent of the whole Qurʾān, despite its shortness and the closeness of its two ends?”, I answer: “It is for some great cause that whoever becomes a master becomes one.” That is solely because it encompasses God's attributes, His justice and His oneness. For anyone who acknowledges its excellence and believes the Messenger of God's ﷺ statement about it, this is sufficient proof that the science of divine oneness (ʿilm al-tawḥīd) has an exalted standing with God. How could it be otherwise? Knowledge follows its object: it is ennobled by the nobility of what is known, and lowered by its lowliness. The object of this science is God Most High and His attributes, and what is and is not admissible concerning Him. What, then, do you think of the nobility of its rank, the grandeur of its position, its eminence over every other science, and its winning the prize ahead of them? Whoever disparages it does so because of the weakness of his knowledge of its object, the smallness of his reverence for Him, his lack of fear of Him, and his remoteness from considering his own ultimate fate. O God, gather us among those who know You, act for You, affirm Your justice and Your oneness, and fear Your threat. It is called “The Sūrah of the Foundation,” because it encompasses the foundations of religion. Ubayy and Anas transmitted from the Prophet ﷺ: “The seven heavens and the seven earths were founded upon ‘Say: He is God, One.’” That is: they were created solely to serve as signs of God's oneness and of the knowledge of His attributes expressed by this sūrah. It is related from the Messenger of God ﷺ that he heard a man reciting “Say: He is God, One,” and said, “It has become assured.” He was asked, “Messenger of God, what has become assured?” He answered, “Paradise has become assured for him.”

“The equivalent of the whole Qurʾān”

XVI 640 | on K112.07
On K112.07

His words, “The equivalent of the whole Qurʾān.” It is transmitted with fatḥa or kasra on the ʿayn. Al-Akhfash says: “ʿIdl, with kasra, means an equivalent. ʿAdl, with fatḥa, is originally the verbal noun in ‘I made this nicely equal [to that]’; you turn it into a noun for an equivalent to distinguish it from a ʿidl, a load of goods.” Al-Farrāʾ says: “ʿAdl, with fatḥa, is what equals something while belonging to a different kind. ʿIdl, with kasra, is its like. You say, ‘I have the ʿidl of your servant-boy, or of your sheep,’ when it is a servant-boy equal to a servant-boy or a sheep equal to a sheep. If you mean its value in something of another kind, you pronounce the ʿayn with fatḥa. Some Arabs sometimes pronounce it with kasra; that was an error on their part.”

His words, “It suffices as proof for whoever acknowledges.” Man iʿtarafa [whoever acknowledges] is the object of kafā [suffices]. Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT112.15

Commentary

XVI 642–643 | concluding addition
On K112.07

A conclusion from the words of Shaykh Faṣīḥ al-Dīn—may God have mercy on him: “‘God, al-Ṣamad’ is not joined by a conjunction to the preceding sentence, because it confirms and explains its content. The same applies to ‘He did not beget,’ because it confirms the content of ‘God, al-Ṣamad’: the absolutely self-sufficient one, whom everything needs, cannot be a begetter or begotten, since that necessarily entails need. “‘Nor was He begotten’ is joined to ‘He did not beget,’ because ‘was not begotten’ does not express the meaning of ‘did not beget,’ and thus does not confirm its meaning; rather, the two sentences confirm the content of the preceding sentence. ‘And no one is equal to Him’ is joined [by a conjunction] because its content does not confirm the content of the two preceding sentences: it expresses that He cannot have any equal in anything mentioned concerning essence and attributes. He is therefore one, with no partner—exalted, holy and supremely great. “The predicate is definite in ‘God, al-Ṣamad’ to negate [the claim of] anyone who asserts and names another as ṣamad. It is indefinite in ‘God, One,’ because they did not call things aḥad in this sense.” [REVIEW T112-U4: The scan reads نفيا لنفي من زعم وسمى غيره صمدا, literally “to negate the negation of whoever asserted and named another ṣamad.” This double negation is problematic. The bracketed rendering gives the apparent intended argument, not a verified emendation. Retain the flag pending another witness or the quoted source.] The sūrah is complete.
