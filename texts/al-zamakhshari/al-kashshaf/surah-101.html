The day, the moths, and the mountains [IV:789–790]

The adverbial “on the day” is accusative through an omitted verb indicated by al-qāriʿa (القارعة): “It strikes on the day when people will be like scattered moths.” He likens them to moths in their multitude, dispersal, weakness, abasement, and flight toward the summoner from every direction, as moths fly toward a fire. Jarīr says: “Al-Farazdaq, so far as I have known, and his people are like moths that swarm the fire of one warming himself.”

Among their proverbs are “weaker than a moth,” “more abased,” and “more ignorant.” They are called farāsh (فراش) because they spread out and disperse. He likens the mountains to ʿihn (عِهْن), wool dyed in different colours, because the mountains are variously coloured; and to carded wool because their parts separate. Ibn Masʿūd read ka-l-ṣūf (كالصوف), “like wool.”

Jarīr’s comparison [XVI:554–555]

Arabic lemma: إن الفرزدق

Meccan; ten verses. In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful. [The source’s count is retained; the verse text above uses the eleven-verse numbering.]

On the line beginning “Al-Farazdaq”: mā (ما) ʿalimtu (علمت) means “what I have known,” and is parenthetical. He satirizes al-Farazdaq and his people: they are weak, abased, and ignorant, like moths which ghashīna (غشين)—that is, came in the covering darkness of night—to the fire at which the poet, Jarīr, warms himself. It is also said that ghashīna (غشين) means “plunged into.” Another explanation takes mā (ما) in mā (ما) ʿalimtu (علمت) as introducing a verbal noun, with a period of time understood: throughout the duration of my knowledge of them, al-Farazdaq and his people have been weak.

al-Qāriʿah · Heavy and light scales

Heavy and light scales [IV:790]

Mawāzīn (موازين) is the plural of mawzūn (موزون), something weighed: the deed that possesses weight and importance with God. Or it is the plural of mīzān (ميزان), a scale; their heaviness is their outweighing.

An example is Abū Bakr’s report in his testament to ʿUmar, God be pleased with them both: “The scales of those whose scales are heavy on the Day of Resurrection are heavy only because they followed the truth and because of their weight in this world. A scale into which nothing but good deeds is placed rightly becomes heavy. The scales of those whose scales are light are light only because they followed falsehood and because of their lightness in this world. A scale into which nothing but evil deeds is placed rightly becomes light.”

al-Qāriʿah · “His mother will be an abyss”

8 But as for him whose scales are light

English verse translation: Marmaduke Pickthall (1930).

Zamakhsharī · الكشافK101.04

فأمه هاوية من قولهم إذا دعوا على الرجل بالهلكة : هوت أمه، لأنه إذا هوى أى سقط وهلك، فقد هوت أمه ثكلا وحزنا قال:

هوت أمه ما يبعث الصبح غاديا ... وماذا يرد الليل حين يئوب

فكأنه قيل. وأما من خفت موازينه فقد هلك. وقيل هاوية من أسماء النار، وكأنها النار العميقة لهوى أهل النار فيها مهوى بعيدا، كما روى «يهوى فيها سبعين خريفا » أى فمأواه النار. وقيل للمأوى: أم، على التشبيه، لأن الأم مأوى الولد ومفزعه. وعن قتادة: فأمه هاوية، أى فأم رأسه هاوية في قعر جهنم، لأنه يطرح فيها منكوسا

“His mother will be an abyss” [IV:790]

“His mother will be an abyss” derives from their expression when invoking destruction upon a man: hawat ummuhu (هوت أمّه), “May his mother fall!” For when he hawā (هوى)—falls and perishes—his mother falls through bereavement and grief. The poet says: “May his mother fall! What a man the morning rouses to set out, and what a man the night brings back when he returns!”

It is thus as though He said: “As for one whose scales are light, he has perished.”

It is also said that Hāwiya (هاوية) is one of the names of the Fire. It is as though it were the deep Fire, so called because its people fall within it to a great depth, as in the report, “He falls in it for seventy autumns.” That is, his abode is the Fire. An abode is metaphorically called a mother because the mother is the child’s refuge and place of recourse.

From Qatāda: “His mother will be an abyss” means that the crown of his head will fall into the bottom of Jahannam, because he is cast into it headfirst.

The testament and the elegy [XVI:555–556]

Arabic lemma: ومنه حديث أبي بكر رضي الله عنه

On “an example is Abū Bakr’s report”: the author of Jāmiʿ al-Uṣūl (الأصول) transmits the report from Razīn al-ʿAbdarī. We have given it in full under al-Aʿrāf.

On the line “May his mother fall!”: its author is Kaʿb ibn Saʿd al-Ghanawī, lamenting his brother. Yabʿathu (يبعث) means rouses from sleep; al-ghādī (الغادي) is one who sets out in the morning and is circumstantial. Hawat ummuhu (هوت أمّه) is an imprecation whose occurrence is not intended; rather, it expresses wonder and praise. That is: what a man the morning sends forth from him when he sets out, and what a man the night returns from him when he comes back! “From him” is omitted in both places because the discourse indicates it, just as it is omitted in “The clarified butter: two minas for a dirham.” This involves abstraction, tajrīd (تجريد): the morning sends forth from him a raider, and the night a man returning with booty.

Ṭībī · فتوح الغيبT101.03

Seventy autumns [XVI:556]

Arabic lemma: سبعين خريفا

On “seventy autumns”: according to some, autumn stands for the year because fruits and crops develop in this season. The final portion of a period is used to designate the whole.

Ṭībī · فتوح الغيبT101.04

قوله: (في التفسير الأول)، أي: إذا فسر "أمه هاوية" بالدعاء، ومن قولهم: هوت أمه؛ وإنما جعل الضمير للداهية، لأن الشخص إذا سقط وهلك وصارت أمه ثكلى وخزيا، فقد أصابته الداهية. وعلى التفسير الثاني: أمه بمعنى المأوى، و {هاوية} من أسماء النار. وأظهر التفسيرين الأول، لأن {فأمه هاوية} مقابل لقوله: {فهو في عيشة راضية}، والهلاك أنسب إلى العيش لأنه الحياة المختصة بالحيوان، فكما بولغ في القرينة التالية بما أردف به، بولغ في السابقى بالإسناد المجازي.

الراغب: "العيش: الحياة المختصة بالحيوان، وهو أخص من الحياة، لأن الحياة تقال في الحيوان، وفي الباري تعالى، وفي الملك، ويشتق منه المعيشة لما يتعيش منه، قال تعالى: {نحن قسمنا بينهم معيشتهم في الحياة الدنيا} [الزخرف: 32]. وقال في أهل الجنة: {فهو في عيشة راضية}، وقال? في الحديث: "لا عيش إلا عيش الآخرة".

Destruction, life, and the parallel expressions [XVI:556]

Arabic lemma: في التفسير الأول

On “on the first interpretation”: that is, if “his mother will be an abyss” is explained as an imprecation, from their saying “May his mother fall!” The pronoun is made to refer to calamity because, when a person falls and perishes and his mother becomes bereaved and disgraced, a calamity has befallen him. On the second interpretation, “his mother” means his abode, and Hāwiya (هاوية) is one of the names of the Fire.

The first interpretation is the more evident, because “his mother will be an abyss” stands opposite “he will be in a life of contentment.” Destruction corresponds more aptly to ʿaysh (عيش), since this is the life peculiar to animate creatures. Just as the second paired clause is intensified by what follows it, the first is intensified by figurative predication.

Al-Rāghib says: “ʿAysh (عيش) is life peculiar to animate creatures, and is narrower than ḥayāt (حياة), ‘life,’ because ḥayāt (حياة) is said of animate creatures, the Creator Most High, and the angel. From it is derived maʿīsha (معيشة), that by which one lives. God Most High says, ‘We have apportioned among them their livelihood in the life of this world’ [43:32]. Of the people of Paradise He says, ‘He will be in a life of contentment.’ And the Prophet says in the report, ‘There is no life but the life of the hereafter.’”

The pronoun and the pausal hāʾ [IV:790–791]

In hiya-h (هِيَهْ), the pronoun refers to the calamity indicated by “his mother will be an abyss” on the first interpretation; or it refers to Hāwiya (هاوية). The final hāʾ (هاء (ه)) is for pausing. When the reciter connects the reading, he omits it. It is also said that one ought not to connect here, lest connection cause its omission, since it is written in the codex. Retaining it in connected recitation has also been permitted.

The stopping point [XVI:557]

Source and review notes

al-Qāriʿah · source and review notes
TEXTUAL NOTES T101-U1: The Ṭībī chapter heading says ten verses, while the DKA heading says eleven. This difference has not been silently regularized. K101-U1: The poetic imprecation is rendered literally in the base text, with its actual force of astonished praise explained in Ṭībī’s layer. The embedded base has yathūb, “returns,” where DKA has yaʾūb; the sense is the same. T101-U2: Printed XVI:556 confirms khuzyā, “disgraced,” against the electronic transcription’s defective خِزْيا form. The season explanation and the author’s preference for the first interpretation are retained without editorial reconciliation.
