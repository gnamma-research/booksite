AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ: AL-KASHSHĀF

K001.01 — Names, place of revelation, and verse count [K-DKA I:1]
It is Meccan. It has also been said to be both Meccan and Medinan, since it was revealed once in Mecca and again in Medina. It is called “Mother of the Qurʾān” (Umm al-Qurʾān), because it includes the meanings found in the Qurʾān: praising God Most High as He deserves; imposing worship through commands and prohibitions; and promises and threats. For the same reason it is called “The Treasure” (al-Kanz) and “The Comprehensive” (al-Wāfiya). It is called “Praise” (al-Ḥamd), and “The Oft-Repeated” (al-Mathānī), because it is repeated in every rakʿa. It is called “Prayer” (al-Ṣalāh), because prayer is excellent, or valid, through its recitation within it; and “Healing” (al-Shifāʾ) and “The Healer” (al-Shāfiya). It has seven verses by agreement, although some count “upon whom You have bestowed favour” as a verse ending and do not count the basmala, whereas others take the opposite position.

K001.02 — The basmala as a verse [K-DKA I:1]
“In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.”

The Qurʾān readers and jurists of Medina, Basra, and Syria hold that the naming formula (al-tasmiya) is a verse neither of al-Fātiḥa nor of the other sūrahs. It was written only to separate them and to obtain blessing by beginning with it, just as every matter of consequence is begun by mentioning it. This is the position of Abū Ḥanīfa—God have mercy upon him—and his followers. Accordingly, they do not pronounce it aloud in prayer.

The readers and jurists of Mecca and Kufa hold that it is a verse of al-Fātiḥa and of every sūrah. This is the position of al-Shāfiʿī and his companions—God have mercy upon them—and accordingly they pronounce it aloud. They argue: the early generations entered it into the written Qurʾān, despite their injunction that the Qurʾān be kept free of additions; for that reason they did not enter Āmīn. Had it not belonged to the Qurʾān, they would not have entered it. It is related from Ibn ʿAbbās: “Whoever omits it has omitted one hundred and fourteen verses from the Book of God Most High.”

K001.03 — The omitted governing verb [K-DKA I:2]
If you ask, “To what is the bāʾ attached?” I answer: to something omitted, supplied as “In God’s name I read,” or “I recite,” since what follows the naming formula is something read. Likewise, when a traveller halts or departs and says, “In God’s name, and with blessings,” the meaning is “In God’s name I halt,” or “In God’s name I depart.” The same applies to someone slaughtering an animal and to anyone who begins an action with “In God’s name”: the person implicitly supplies the action for which the naming formula has been made the beginning.

A parallel omission of what governs a prepositional phrase occurs in His saying, mighty and exalted, “With nine signs, to Pharaoh and his people” [27:12]: that is, “Go with nine signs.” Likewise the Arabs’ prayer for a bridegroom, “With harmony and sons,” and the Bedouin’s “With good fortune and blessing,” mean “May you wed,” or “May you marry [with these].” Another example is the poet’s saying:

I said, “To the food!” and a group of them replied,
“We begrudge mankind their food.”

[The invitation supplies an understood verb such as “Come.”]

K001.04 — Why the divine name comes first [K-DKA I:3]
If you ask, “Why have you supplied the omitted verb after the phrase?” I answer: between the action and that to which it is attached, the latter is more important. People used to begin with their gods’ names, saying, “In al-Lāt’s name,” or “In al-ʿUzzā’s name.” The professed monotheist must therefore intend to reserve the beginning exclusively for the name of God, mighty and exalted. This is achieved by placing it first and the verb afterwards, as in “You alone we worship,” where placing the pronoun first is made explicit to convey exclusiveness. His saying “In God’s name are its sailing and its mooring” [11:41] provides evidence.

If you ask, “But He said, ‘Read in the name of your Lord’ [96:1], putting the verb first,” I answer: putting the verb first is more effective there, since that was the first sūrah revealed, and the command to read was therefore more important.

K001.05 — Instrument or accompaniment [K-DKA I:3–4]
If you ask, “What does attaching God’s name to reading mean?” I answer: there are two possibilities.

First, it is attached as the pen is attached to writing in “I wrote with the pen.” The believer holds that an action is not legally accorded weight, or performed according to the Sunna, unless it issues from mentioning God’s name, because of the Prophet’s saying—blessings and peace upon him—“Every matter of consequence that is not begun with God’s name is cut short.” Otherwise it is an action that counts as no action. He therefore treats his action as performed by means of God’s name, as writing is performed by means of the pen.

Second, it is attached as oil is attached to growing in “It grows with oil” [23:20]: the meaning is “Seeking blessing through God’s name, I read.” Likewise, someone praying for a bridegroom, “With harmony and sons,” means “May you wed accompanied by harmony and sons.” This interpretation is more idiomatic Arabic and better.

If you ask, “How, then, can God, blessed and exalted, say, ‘Seeking blessing through God’s name, I read’?” I answer: this is spoken upon the tongues of the servants, just as a person composes poetry in another’s voice. The same applies to “Praise belongs to God, Lord of the worlds” and what follows. Much of the Qurʾān follows this pattern: its meaning is to teach His servants how to seek blessing through His name and how to praise, glorify, and magnify Him.

K001.06 — The bāʾ, and the form and spelling of ism [K-DKA I:4–5]
If you ask, “Single-letter particles of meaning ought to be built on fatḥa, the sister of sukūn, as are the kāf of comparison, the lām of inception, the conjunctive wāw and fāʾ, and others. Why, then, are the possessive lām and bāʾ built on kasra?” I answer: the lām is so marked to distinguish it from the lām of inception; the bāʾ because it invariably remains a particle and governs the genitive.

Ism, “name,” is one of the ten nouns whose beginnings the Arabs formed with sukūn. When they pronounce these at the beginning of speech, they add a hamza to avoid beginning with an unvocalized consonant. Their practice is to begin with a vocalized consonant and pause on an unvocalized one, keeping their language free of every impediment and harshness and making it supremely well ordered and firm. When the word occurs in connected speech, no addition is needed. Some dispense with that addition and instead vocalize the unvocalized consonant, saying sim or sum. The poet says:

In the name of Him whose name is in every sūrah.

It is among nouns whose final radical has been omitted, like yad, “hand,” and dam, “blood.” Its original form is simw [سِمْو], as its transformations demonstrate: asmāʾ, “names”; sumayy, its diminutive; and sammaytu, “I named.” Its derivation is from sumuww, “elevation,” because naming gives prominence to the named thing and raises its mention. Similarly, a byname is called nabaz, from nabz in the sense of nabr, raising the voice. Nibz is also the upper bark of a palm.

If you ask, “Why is the alif omitted in writing here, but retained in ‘In the name of your Lord’?” I answer: because of its frequent use, its omission follows the rule for connected speech rather than initial pronunciation, although initial pronunciation is the basis of spelling. They also say that the bāʾ was lengthened to compensate for dropping the alif. It is related that ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz told his scribe: “Lengthen the bāʾ, make the teeth clear, and round the mīm.”

K001.07 — Allāh and ilāh [K-DKA I:5–6]
The original form of Allāh is al-ilāh. The poet says:

God forbid that you should be like a gazelle.

A parallel is al-nās, whose original form is al-unās. The poet says:

Deaths come upon
people living in security.

The hamza was omitted, and the definite article substituted for it. Accordingly, in direct address one says yā Allāh, with the initial hamza pronounced, just as one says yā ilāh.

Ilāh is a generic noun, like “man” and “horse”: it applies to every object of worship, rightly or wrongly worshipped. It then became predominant for the One rightly worshipped. Similarly, al-najm is the name of any star, but became predominant for the Pleiades; al-sana, “the year,” for a year of drought; al-bayt, “the house,” for the Kaʿba; and al-kitāb, “the book,” for Sībawayh’s book. Allāh, with the hamza omitted, is exclusively applied to the One rightly worshipped and has not been used of another.

From this noun were derived taʾallaha, allaha, and istaʾlaha (تألّه، ألّه، استأله), just as istanwaqa and istaḥjara were derived from nāqa, “she-camel,” and ḥajar, “stone.”

If you ask, “Is it a noun or an adjective?” I answer: a noun, not an adjective. You qualify it with an adjective but do not use it to qualify something else. You do not say “an ilāh thing,” just as you do not say “a man thing.” You say “one ilāh, a ṣamad,” just as you say “a noble, good man.” Moreover, His attributes, exalted is He, must have a subject of which they are predicated. If all of them were made adjectives, they would remain without a noun that they qualify, which is impossible.

If you ask, “Does this noun have a derivation?” I answer: derivation means that two or more forms are united by a single meaning. The form of this noun and the form of their expression aliha, “he was bewildered,” together with its relatives daliha and ʿaliha, are united by the meaning of bewilderment and amazement. Conceptions are bewildered in knowing the One worshipped, and discerning minds are amazed. For this reason error has multiplied, falsehood has spread, and sound reflection is scarce.

If you ask, “Is its lām pronounced emphatically?” I answer: yes. Al-Zajjāj has stated that its emphatic pronunciation (tafkhīm) is a received practice. All the Arabs follow it, and their agreement proves that they inherited it from one generation to another.

K001.08 — Al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm [K-DKA I:6–8]
Al-Raḥmān is on the pattern faʿlān, from raḥima, just as ghaḍbān, “angry,” and sakrān, “drunk,” are from ghaḍiba and sakira. Al-Raḥīm likewise is on faʿīl from that root, as marīḍ and saqīm, “ill,” are from mariḍa and saqima. Al-Raḥmān contains an intensification absent from al-Raḥīm. Accordingly, they say, “Raḥmān of this world and the next, and Raḥīm of this world.” [K001-U1: “this world” is the reading in both acquired transcriptions; do not silently substitute “the next.”] They say that an increase in form corresponds to an increase in meaning.

Al-Zajjāj said that al-ghaḍbān means someone filled with anger. Among the Arabs’ amusing expressions that have rung in my ears is their calling one kind of conveyance a shuqduf. This is a light conveyance, without the weight of Iraqi camel litters. On the road to al-Ṭāʾif I asked one of them, “What is this litter called?”—meaning the Iraqi litter. He said, “Is not that one called the shuqduf?” “Yes,” I answered. “This one,” he said, “is called the shuqandāf.” He increased the structure of the name for the increase in the thing named.

Al-Raḥmān is among adjectives that have become predominant designations, like al-Dabarān, al-ʿAyyūq, and al-Ṣaʿiq. It has not been used of anyone other than God, mighty and exalted, just as Allāh is among predominant nouns. As for Banū Ḥanīfa calling Musaylima “Raḥmān of al-Yamāma,” and their poet saying of him,

You are the rain of humankind; may you remain a raḥmān,

this is an instance of their obstinacy in unbelief.

If you ask, “When you say ‘Allāh is raḥmān,’ do you make it fully declinable or not?” I answer: I compare it to its fellows in the same category—ʿaṭshān, “thirsty,” gharthān, “hungry,” sakrān, “drunk”—and do not make it fully declinable.

If you ask, “But a condition for preventing faʿlān from full declension is that its feminine be faʿlā. Its exclusive application to God prevents its having such a feminine; why deny it full declension?” I answer: just as that exclusiveness prevents a feminine on faʿlā, like ʿaṭshā, it also prevents a feminine on faʿlāna, like nadmāna. The absence of a feminine caused by this subsequent exclusiveness is therefore immaterial. One must return to its original status before that exclusiveness, comparing it to its counterparts.

If you ask, “What does it mean to describe God Most High by mercy (raḥma), when that means tenderness and compassionate inclination, and raḥim, ‘womb,’ is related to it because it curves around what is inside?” I answer: this is a metaphor for His bestowing favours upon His servants. When a king inclines compassionately towards his subjects and feels tenderness for them, he reaches them with kindness and favour; when harshness and cruelty seize him, he treats them roughly and withholds his goodness and kindness.

If you ask, “Why put the more intensive of the two descriptions before the less intensive, when the normal progression is from lower to higher—as in ‘so-and-so is learned, consummately learned; brave, valiant; generous, abundantly giving’?” I answer: when He says al-Raḥmān, encompassing great and mighty blessings and their foundations, He follows it with al-Raḥīm, as a completion and supplement, to encompass their small and subtle forms.

K001.09 — Praise and gratitude [K-DKA I:8–9]
“Praise belongs to God, Lord of the worlds, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.”

Ḥamd, “praise,” and madḥ, “commendation,” are closely related: they are the act of praising and proclaiming what is fine, whether a favour or something else. You say, “I praised the man for his beneficence,” and “I praised him for his lineage and courage.”

Shukr, “gratitude,” concerns a favour specifically, and is expressed through the heart, the tongue, and the bodily members. The poet says:

Your favour has gained you three things from me:
my hand, my tongue, and my hidden heart.

Ḥamd is expressed by the tongue alone and is therefore one branch of shukr. Hence the Prophet’s saying—peace upon him—“Praise is the head of gratitude. A servant who does not praise God has not thanked Him.” He made it gratitude’s head because mentioning a favour with the tongue and praising its giver spreads knowledge of it more widely and indicates its standing more clearly than inward belief and the conduct of the limbs. The heart’s action is hidden; bodily action admits of different interpretations. The tongue’s action is different: it is speech, which makes every hidden thing explicit and clarifies every ambiguity.

The opposite of ḥamd is dhamm, “blame”; the opposite of shukr is kufrān, “ingratitude.”

K001.10 — Case, definiteness, and readings of al-ḥamd [K-DKA I:9–10]
Al-ḥamdu is nominative because it is the subject of a nominal sentence (mubtadaʾ); its predicate is the adverbial phrase li-llāh, “to God.” Its underlying form is accusative, as some read it, with an implied governing verb. It belongs to the verbal nouns that the Arabs put in the accusative through implicit verbs to make statements, as in shukran, “thanks,” kufran, “ingratitude,” ʿajaban, “wonder,” and the like, including subḥānaka, “glory be to You,” and maʿādha llāh, “God forbid.” They put these expressions in the place of their verbs and let them serve their function. They consequently do not use the verbs alongside them, treating such usage like an abrogated law.

The change from accusative to nominative as the subject of a nominal sentence indicates the meaning’s permanence and stability. An example is His saying, “They said, ‘Peace!’ He said, ‘Peace!’” [11:69]. The second “peace” is nominative, indicating that Abraham—peace upon him—greeted them with a greeting better than theirs: the nominative signifies peace established for them, rather than peace newly occurring.

The meaning is “We praise God with praise.” Accordingly, “You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help” follows, explaining their praise of Him. It is as though it had been asked, “How do you praise?” and the answer were, “You alone we worship.”

If you ask, “What does the definite article signify here?” I answer: it resembles the definite article in “He sent them to the jostling [at the watering place]” (arsalahā al-ʿirāk). It specifies the genus: pointing to what everyone recognizes as what praise is, and what jostling is, among the kinds of action. The exhaustive inclusion that many people imagine is a mistaken assumption on their part.

Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī read al-ḥamdi li-llāh, putting kasra on the dāl to assimilate it to the lām. Ibrāhīm ibn Abī ʿAbla read al-ḥamdu lu-llāh, putting ḍamma on the lām to assimilate it to the dāl. What emboldened them to do this—although such vowel assimilation normally occurs within a single word, as in their expressions munḥadur al-jabal and Mughīra—was that the two words, so frequently used together, came to occupy the position of a single word. Ibrāhīm’s reading is the better of the two, since he made a fixed vowel follow an inflectional vowel, which is stronger, unlike al-Ḥasan’s reading. [K001-U2: the precise vocalization of the illustrative forms must be checked against a fully vocalized witness.]

K001.11 — Rabb and the worlds [K-DKA I:10–11]
Al-rabb means “the owner.” An example is Ṣafwān’s saying to Abū Sufyān: “I would rather have a man of Quraysh rule me than a man of Hawāzin.” You say rabbahu, yarubbuhu, fa-huwa rabb, “he ruled him, he rules him, so he is a lord,” just as you say namma ʿalayhi, yanimmu, fa-huwa namm, “he informed against him, he informs against him, so he is an informer.”

It may also be a verbal noun used adjectivally for intensification, as ʿadl, “justice,” is used as an adjective [meaning thoroughly just]. They use al-Rabb without qualification for God alone. Of others it is restricted by a possessive construction, as in “master of the house” or “owner of the she-camel,” and His sayings “Return to your master” [12:50] and “He is my master, who has made my lodging good” [12:23].

Zayd ibn ʿAlī—God be pleased with them both—read rabba l-ʿālamīn in the accusative as an expression of praise. It is also said that the accusative is governed by what “Praise belongs to God” implies, as though the wording were “We praise God, Lord of the worlds.”

Al-ʿālam is a name for beings possessed of knowledge: angels and the two weighty kinds, humans and jinn. It is also said to mean everything through which the Creator is known, whether bodies or accidents (aʿrāḍ).

If you ask, “Why is it plural?” I answer: to include every kind to which the name applies.

If you ask, “It is a noun, not an adjective, whereas the plural with wāw and nūn is made only from adjectives of rational beings or proper names treated similarly,” I answer: this is permissible because it contains an adjectival meaning, namely the indication of knowledge.

K001.12 — Mālik, malik, and the Day of Recompense [K-DKA I:11–12, lacuna restored from K-JK I:54–55 and T-DUB I:734–737]
“Master of the Day of Recompense.”

It has been read malik yawmi l-dīn, “King of the Day of Recompense”; mālik, “Master”; and malk, with the lām lightened [unvocalized]. Abū Ḥanīfa—God be pleased with him—read malaka yawma l-dīn, with a verbal form and “day” in the accusative [“He possessed [authority on] the Day of Recompense”]. Abū Hurayra—God be pleased with him—read mālika in the accusative; others read malika. This accusative expresses praise. Some read mālikun in the nominative.

Malik is the preferred reading, because it is the reading of the people of the two sanctuaries, and because of His sayings “To whom belongs kingship today?” [40:16] and “King of humankind” [114:2], and because ownership (milk, مِلْك) is general, whereas kingship (mulk, مُلْك) is particular.

The Day of Dīn is the Day of Recompense. Hence their saying, “As you deal, so shall you be dealt with,” and the line in the Ḥamāsa:

Nothing remained but aggression:
we dealt with them as they had dealt with us.

If you ask, “What is this possessive construction?” I answer: it is the addition of an active participle to an adverbial expression, through an extension of usage that treats the latter like a direct object. Compare their saying, “O you who robbed the household on the night!” (yā sāriqa l-laylati ahla l-dār): the meaning remains temporal. The sense here is “Master of the whole affair on the Day of Recompense,” like His saying “To whom belongs kingship today?” [40:16].

K001.13 — How an active participle can qualify the definite divine name [K-JK I:55; T-DUB I:737–741; the end survives in K-DKA I:13]
If you ask, “An active participle’s possessive construction is not a true possessive construction and does not impart definiteness. How, then, may it qualify a definite noun?” I answer: it is not a true possessive construction only when the active participle is intended in the present or future, so that its elements are understood as separable: for example, “owning now,” or “tomorrow.”

But if a past meaning is intended, as in “He owned his slave yesterday,” or continuous time, as in “Zayd is an owner of slaves,” the construction is a true one, like “master of slaves.” That is the meaning in “Master of the Day of Recompense.” The meaning may also be “He possessed the affairs on the Day of Recompense,” as in “The inhabitants of the Garden called out” [7:44] and “The people of the Heights called out” [7:48]. Abū Ḥanīfa’s reading malaka yawma l-dīn supports this.

These descriptions applied to God—glory be to Him—state that He is Lord and Owner of the worlds, with nothing in them outside His sovereignty and lordship; that He bestows every blessing, outward and inward, great and small; and that He owns the whole affair at the last, on the Day of Reward and Punishment. Coming after “Praise belongs to God” has indicated that praise belongs exclusively to Him and that He deserves it, these descriptions demonstrate that no one could be more entitled to praise and to being extolled as He deserves than One whose attributes these are.

K001.14 — Iyyāka and exclusive worship [K-DKA I:13]
“You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help.”

Iyyā is an independent accusative pronoun. The suffixes attached to it—the kāf, hāʾ, and yāʾ in iyyāka, iyyāhu, and iyyāya—indicate second, third, and first person. They occupy no syntactic case position, just as the kāf in araʾaytaka does not. They are not pronominal nouns. This is al-Akhfash’s position, adopted by the exacting scholars.

As for al-Khalīl’s report that some Arabs say, “When a man reaches sixty, let him beware of young women” (fa-iyyāhu wa-iyyā l-shawābb), this is an anomalous form on which one should not rely.

The object is placed first to convey exclusiveness, as in His sayings “Say: Is it other than God that you command me to worship?” [39:64] and “Say: Shall I seek a lord other than God?” [6:164]. The meaning is “We single You out for worship, and single You out in seeking assistance.”

It has also been read iyāka, with the yāʾ lightened; ayyāka, with fatḥa on the hamza and a doubled yāʾ; and hiyyāka, with the hamza changed to hāʾ. Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī said:

Beware of the affair whose ways in, though spacious,
leave its ways out too narrow for you.

Worship (ʿibāda) is the utmost degree of submission and abasement. Related to it is thawb dhū ʿabda, a cloth of extreme thickness and strength of weave. Hence the word is used only for submission to God Most High, since He bestows the greatest blessings and therefore deserves the utmost submission.

K001.15 — The turn from third person to direct address [K-DKA I:13–14]
If you ask, “Why turn from third-person expression to direct address?” I answer: in the science of eloquence this is called iltifāt. It can run from third person to direct address, from direct address to third person, and from third person to first person, as in His sayings “Until, when you are in the ships and they sail with them” [10:22] and “God is He who sent the winds, which raise a cloud, and We drove it” [35:9].

Imruʾ al-Qays made three turns in three lines:

Your night at al-Ithmid grew long;
the carefree sleeper slept, while you did not.

He passed the night, and his night endured,
like the night of one with an inflamed, afflicted eye.

That was because of news that reached me,
which I was told concerning Abū al-Aswad.

This accords with their habit of varying speech and employing it in different ways. Moving speech from one mode to another better refreshes the listener’s attention and rouses attentive listening than continuing in a single mode. Particular instances may have particular benefits.

The benefit peculiar to this passage is that, after the One deserving praise has been mentioned and these mighty attributes applied to Him, knowledge has attached itself to an object of immense standing: One worthy of praise, utmost submission, and recourse for assistance in important affairs. That known One, distinguished by these attributes, is then addressed: “You—O One whose attributes these are—we single out for worship and assistance. We worship no other and seek no other’s help.” The direct address thus indicates more clearly that worship is His because of this distinguishing character, without which no one deserves worship.

K001.16 — Worship and asking for help [K-DKA I:14–15]
If you ask, “Why associate asking for help with worship?” I answer: to bring together that through which the servants draw near to their Lord and that which they seek and need from Him.

If you ask, “Why put worship before asking for help?” I answer: because presenting the means of approach before requesting the need makes them deserving of a favourable response.

If you ask, “Why leave the request for help unrestricted?” I answer: to include everything in which help is sought. Better still is to intend seeking His help and His granting of success (tawfīq) in performing worship. “Guide us” would then explain the assistance requested, as though He had asked, “How shall I help you?” and they answered, “Guide us on the straight path.” This is better because the discourse coheres, each part taking hold of the next.

Ibn Ḥubaysh [Zirr ibn Ḥubaysh in the Turkish identification, p.90] read nistaʿīn, with kasra on the nūn.

K001.17 — Guidance and the straight path [K-DKA I:15]
“Guide us on the straight path.”

Hadā originally takes its complement with lām or ilā, as in His sayings “This Qurʾān guides to that which is most upright” [17:9] and “You surely guide to a straight path” [42:52]. Here it is treated like ikhtāra, “choose,” in “Moses chose his people” [7:155; that is, chose from among them].

To ask for guidance when they are already guided means asking for increased guidance through the bestowal of gracious aids (alṭāf), as in His sayings “Those who are guided, He increases in guidance” [47:17] and “Those who strive for Our sake, We shall surely guide upon Our ways” [29:69]. It is related from ʿAlī and Ubayy—God be pleased with them both—that “Guide us” means “Make us steadfast.” Command and supplication have a single form, since each is a request; they differ only in rank. ʿAbd Allāh read arshidnā, “Direct us rightly.”

Al-sirāṭ means the main road, from saraṭa a thing, “swallowed it,” since the road swallows travellers when they enter it. Likewise it is called laqam because it gulps them down. Ṣirāṭ results from changing the sīn to ṣād because of the ṭāʾ, like muṣayṭir for musayṭir. The ṣād may also be tinged with the sound of zāy. All these forms have been read. The most eloquent is pure ṣād, the dialect of Quraysh and the form established in the authoritative codex (al-imām). Its plural is suruṭ, like kitāb and kutub. It may be masculine or feminine, like ṭarīq and sabīl. What is intended is the path of truth, the religion of Islam.

K001.18 — Why repeat “path”? [K-DKA I:15–16]
“The path of those upon whom You have bestowed favour, not those who have incurred wrath, nor those astray.”

“The path of those upon whom You have bestowed favour” is a substitute (badal) for “the straight path.” A substitution has the force of repeating the governing word, as though it said, “Guide us on the straight path; guide us on the path of those upon whom You have bestowed favour.” Compare “those who were deemed weak,” followed by “those of them who believed” [7:75].

If you ask, “What benefit does the substitution have? Why not simply say ‘Guide us on the path of those upon whom You have bestowed favour’?” I answer: its benefit is emphasis, through the doubling and repetition it contains, and the suggestion that the explanation and interpretation of “the straight path” is the path of the Muslims. This bears witness to the straightness of the Muslims’ path in the strongest and most emphatic way.

Compare saying, “Shall I point you to the most generous and excellent of people? So-and-so.” This describes his generosity and excellence more forcefully than “Shall I point you to so-and-so, the most generous and excellent?” You have mentioned him twice, first generally and then specifically. You have made “so-and-so” the explanation and clarification of “the most generous and excellent,” establishing him as a sign of generosity and excellence. It is as though you said, “Whoever seeks a man combining both qualities should turn to so-and-so: he is the particular, designated person in whom both meet, without dispute or challenge.”

“Those upon whom You have bestowed favour” are the believers. Favour is left unrestricted to include every favour, since whoever receives the blessing of Islam has no blessing left that does not reach and encompass him. It is related from Ibn ʿAbbās that they are Moses’ companions before they changed. It is also said that they are the prophets. Ibn Masʿūd read ṣirāṭa man anʿamta ʿalayhim, using man, “those whom.”

K001.19 — Ghayr and those who incurred wrath [K-DKA I:16–17]
“Not those who have incurred wrath” (ghayri l-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim) is a substitute for “those upon whom You have bestowed favour.” The sense is that those favoured are those who have been kept safe from God’s wrath and from error. Alternatively, it is an adjective: they combine unrestricted favour—the favour of faith—with safety from God’s wrath and error.

If you ask, “How can ghayr qualify a definite expression when it does not become definite even when annexed to definite nouns?” I answer: “those upon whom You have bestowed favour” does not designate a fixed set, as in the poet’s saying:

I may indeed pass by the base man as he reviles me.

Moreover, those who incurred wrath and those astray are the opposite of those favoured. Ghayr therefore does not have here the indeterminacy that prevents it from becoming definite.

It has also been read in the accusative as a circumstantial expression (ḥāl). This is the reading of God’s Messenger—God bless him and grant him peace—and of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, and it is also related from Ibn Kathīr. The bearer of the circumstance is the pronoun in ʿalayhim, “upon them”; its governing verb is anʿamta, “You bestowed favour.”

It is said that those who incurred wrath are the Jews, because of His saying, mighty and exalted, “those whom God cursed and against whom He was angry” [5:60]; and that those astray are the Christians, because of His saying, “who had gone astray before” [5:77].

If you ask, “What does God’s wrath mean?” I answer: the will to exact retribution from the disobedient and to send punishment down upon them, and to do to them what a king does when angry with those under his power. We seek refuge in God from His wrath, and ask Him for His good pleasure and mercy.

If you ask, “What is the difference between the first ʿalayhim and the second?” I answer: the first occupies an accusative position as an object; the second occupies a nominative position as the subject [of the passive expression al-maghḍūb].

K001.20 — Negation and a variant pronunciation [K-DKA I:17]
If you ask, “Why does lā enter in wa-lā l-ḍāllīn, ‘nor those astray’?” I answer: because ghayr contains the meaning of negation. It is as though the wording were “neither those who incurred wrath nor those astray.”

You can say anā Zaydan ghayru ḍārib, “I am not striking Zayd,” although you cannot say anā Zaydan mithlu ḍārib, “I am like one striking Zayd,” with that word order. The former is equivalent to anā Zaydan lā ḍārib, “I am not striking Zayd.”

It is related that ʿUmar and ʿAlī—God be pleased with them both—read wa-ghayri l-ḍāllīn, “and not those astray.” Ayyūb al-Sakhtiyānī read wa-lā l-ḍaʾllīn, with a hamza, just as ʿAmr ibn ʿUbayd read wa-lā jaʾnn [55:39]. This is the speech of someone who goes to great lengths to avoid two unvocalized consonants meeting. Similar examples are Abū Zayd’s report of their saying shaʾbba and daʾbba.

K001.21 — Āmīn [K-DKA I:17–18]
Āmīn is a sound made the name of the verb istajib, “Respond,” just as ruwayd, ḥayyahal, and halumma are sounds made the names of the verbs “Give respite,” “Hasten,” and “Come.”

It is related from Ibn ʿAbbās: “I asked God’s Messenger—God bless him and grant him peace—what Āmīn meant, and he said, ‘Do [it].’” It has two pronunciations, with its alif long or short. A poet said:

May God have mercy upon a servant who says “Āmīn.”

Another said:

“Amīn”—and may God increase the distance between us.

It is related from the Prophet—God bless him and grant him peace—“Gabriel, peace upon him, taught me to say Āmīn when I finished reciting the Opening of the Book, and said, ‘It is like the seal upon a letter.’” It is not part of the Qurʾān, as shown by its not having been entered into the codices.

It is related from al-Ḥasan that the imam does not say it, since he is the one making the supplication. The same is related from Abū Ḥanīfa—God have mercy upon him—but the well-known report from him and his companions is that he says it quietly. ʿAbd Allāh ibn Mughaffal and Anas related quiet pronunciation from God’s Messenger—God bless him and grant him peace. Al-Shāfiʿī holds that it is pronounced aloud. Wāʾil ibn Ḥujr related that when the Prophet—God bless him and grant him peace—recited “nor those astray,” he would say Āmīn and raise his voice with it.

K001.22 — Closing reports [K-DKA I:18–19]
It is related that God’s Messenger—God bless him and grant him peace—said to Ubayy ibn Kaʿb: “Shall I tell you of a sūrah whose like has not been revealed in the Torah, the Gospel, or the Qurʾān?” “Yes, Messenger of God,” I replied. He said, “The Opening of the Book: it is the seven oft-repeated verses and the mighty Qurʾān that I have been given.”

It is related from Ḥudhayfa ibn al-Yamān that the Prophet—God bless him and grant him peace—said: “God may send punishment upon a people, decisively decreed; then a child among them recites in the elementary school, ‘Praise belongs to God, Lord of the worlds.’ God Most High hears him and thereby lifts the punishment from them for forty years.”

END OF AL-KASHSHĀF’S FĀTIḤA COMMENTARY

T001.01 — Meccan revelation and “Mother of the Qurʾān” [I:677]
On his words, “The sūrah of the Opening of the Book is Meccan; it is also said to be Meccan and Medinan”:

Al-Kawāshī says: “The correct view is that it is Meccan.” The Qāḍī [al-Bayḍāwī] says: “It has been established that it is Meccan, because of His saying, ‘We have indeed given you seven of the oft-repeated’ [15:87], which is Meccan.”

On his words, “because it includes the meanings found in the Qurʾān”:

That is, the Qurʾān sets out in detail the meaning that al-Fātiḥa contains in summary. In a related way Mecca is called “Mother of the Settlements,” because the earth was spread out from beneath it. Imām Muḥammad ibn Ismāʿīl al-Bukhārī said: “Al-Fātiḥa was called Mother of the Book because writing the codices begins with it, and recitation in prayer begins with it.”

T001.02 — The four kinds of religious knowledge [I:678]
The Qāḍī says: “It comprises theoretical wisdom and practical rulings, which consist in following the straight path, and knowledge of the ranks of the fortunate and the stations of the wretched.”

This may be expanded by saying that it comprises four kinds of knowledge upon which religion depends.

The first is knowledge of the foundations (ʿilm al-uṣūl). Its principal points are knowledge of God and His attributes, indicated by “to God, Lord of the worlds, the All-Merciful, the Merciful”; knowledge of prophethood, intended by “upon whom You have bestowed favour”; and knowledge of the return to God, intimated by “Master of the Day of Recompense.”

The second is knowledge of the branches (ʿilm al-furūʿ). Its foundation is acts of worship, intended by “You alone we worship.” Worship is bodily and financial. Both forms require the affairs of livelihood, comprising transactions and marriages; and these necessarily require judicial determinations. The branches are therefore established upon these foundations.

The third is knowledge of that through which perfection is obtained: the science of character (ʿilm al-akhlāq). Its highest aspect is reaching the presence of the All-Sufficient (al-ṣamadāniyya), taking refuge in the precinct of divine uniqueness (al-fardāniyya), following His path, and remaining upright upon it. This is indicated by “You alone we ask for help; guide us on the straight path.”

The fourth is knowledge of narratives and accounts of past communities and bygone generations, both their fortunate and their wretched, together with the promises made to those among them who acted well and the threats directed at their wrongdoers. This is intended by “upon whom You have bestowed favour, not those who have incurred wrath, nor those astray.”

We shall disclose this meaning more fully when we begin interpreting the sūrah according to this pattern. Keep it in mind, so that it may serve as a decisive judge.

T001.03 — Commands, names, repetition, and prayer [I:679]
On his words, “and imposing worship through commands and prohibitions”:

Al-Asās says: “Taʿabbadanī so-and-so and iʿtabadanī mean ‘he made me like a slave to him.’ Taʿabbada so-and-so means ‘he practised devotion,’ and ‘he sat in his place of worship.’”

Here the verb takes bāʾ because it incorporates the meaning of imposing an obligation (taklīf): that is, He imposed commands and prohibitions upon him as worship, meaning the things commanded and prohibited. It is also possible for bāʾ to be as in “I wrote with the pen,” with command and prohibition retaining their literal meanings.

On “and al-Wāfiya for that reason”:

That is, it is called “The Treasure” and “The Comprehensive” for the meaning already mentioned: its inclusion of the meanings.

On “in every rakʿa”:

That is, in every prayer. God Most High says, “Bow with those who bow” [2:43]. It has also been said that this is because it is paired with another sūrah in every rakʿa.

On “because it is excellent, or valid”:

This explains why the name “prayer” is appropriate to al-Fātiḥa. The Ḥanafīs say that it is called the sūrah of Prayer because it is excellent: reciting it in prayer is preferable to reciting something else. The Shāfiʿīs explain the name by saying that prayer is valid only through it.

T001.04 — Verse endings and counting the basmala [I:680]
On his words, “except that some count ‘upon whom You have bestowed favour’ but not the naming formula, while others take the opposite position”:

The author of al-Murshid says: “If you stop at ‘upon whom You have bestowed favour,’ it is the end of a verse according to the people of Medina and Basra. The stop is permissible but not good, because ghayr, when genitive, is connected to it as an adjective or substitute, and when accusative is a circumstantial expression or an exception. Its permissibility rests only upon the transmitted report that he—God bless him and grant him peace—used to stop at verse endings. This is a verse ending according to those I have mentioned, and that explains its permissibility.” His words end here.

I say: the second position is preferable, because the cadence of “upon whom You have bestowed favour” does not match that of the sūrah’s verse endings, and because of what Muḥyī al-Sunna related in Sharḥ al-Sunna, from Ibn Jurayj:

“My father informed me, from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, that ‘We have indeed given you seven of the oft-repeated and the mighty Qurʾān’ [15:87] means Mother of the Qurʾān. My father said: Saʿīd ibn Jubayr recited it to me until he finished it, then said, ‘In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful: the seventh verse.’ Saʿīd said: Ibn ʿAbbās recited it to me just as I have recited it to you, then said, ‘In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful: the seventh verse.’”

T001.05 — The readers’ positions on the basmala [I:681]
On “the readers of Medina, Basra, and Syria”:

Al-Shuʿla says: “From Mecca, Ibn Kathīr, and from Kufa, ʿĀṣim and al-Kisāʾī, hold that the basmala belongs to al-Fātiḥa and to every sūrah. This is the view of Ibn ʿAbbās and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, and the position of al-Shāfiʿī, ʿAṭāʾ, al-Zuhrī, and Ibn al-Mubārak—God be pleased with them.

“Ḥamza, also from Kufa, holds that it belongs to al-Fātiḥa alone, the entire Qurʾān being treated as a single sūrah. This is the view of Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyab.

“Abū ʿAmr of Basra, Nāfiʿ of Medina, and Ibn ʿĀmir of Syria hold that it is a verse neither of al-Fātiḥa nor of another sūrah. Its occurrence in al-Naml is part of a verse. This is the view of Ibn Masʿūd, and the position of Abū Ḥanīfa, Mālik, and Aḥmad—God be pleased with them.”

T001.06 — A “matter of consequence” and the metaphor of being cut short [I:681–682]
On “every matter of consequence”:

Imām Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal related in his Musnad, from Abū Hurayra—God be pleased with him—that God’s Messenger—God bless him and grant him peace—said: “Every speech or matter of consequence that is not opened with the remembrance of God is cut short”—or he said, “severed.”

Al-Nihāya says: “Bāl means a condition or affair. A matter dhū bāl is an important one to which attention and concern are devoted. In another usage, bāl means the heart.”

It has been said that such a matter is called dhū bāl, “possessing a heart,” because, by occupying the heart, it is as though it owns it and has become its possessor. An important affair may also be called dhū bāl through an implicit metaphor (istiʿāra makniyya), with “cut short” supplying a reinforcing feature (tarshīḥ), as in “Your hater is the one cut off” [108:3]. Similarly, he—blessings upon him—gave the affair a head and the summit of a hump in his saying, “The head of the affair is Islam, and the summit of its hump is jihād.” Al-Tirmidhī transmitted this report.

T001.07 — The number one hundred and fourteen [I:682–683]
On “whoever omits it has omitted one hundred and fourteen verses”:

This statement is either made by predominance (taghlīb), or to intensify the rebuke; or it includes the occurrence in al-Naml, since the negation concerns omitting everything to which the name basmala applies; or it rests upon his belief that Sūrat Barāʾa, too, ought to be headed by the basmala.

Supporting this last possibility is what we have related from al-Tirmidhī and Abū Dāwūd: Ibn ʿAbbās asked ʿUthmān—God be pleased with them both—“What led you not to write ‘In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful’?”—meaning in Barāʾa. [The source then says “the report,” without reproducing its remainder.]

T001.08 — Supplying a verb: reading or beginning? [I:683]
On “to something omitted”:

Prepositions are never without something to which they are attached, because their function is to convey the meanings of verbs to nouns. They indicate an action in general, however, and a contextual indication is needed to specify it. Here that indication is what follows the naming formula: “Praise belongs to God.” This is something read and recited, and so indicates that the implicit verb is “I read” or “I recite.” The explanation “because what follows” determines which expression is to be supplied.

It would have been more appropriate to say, “What follows the naming formula is the reading,” since beginning with the formula concerns the action that the person pronouncing it intends to perform. This is indicated by his saying, “Every agent who begins his action with ‘In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful’ implicitly supplies that for which the naming formula was made a beginning.” What is implicit is the action, not its object, just as what follows the slaughterer’s naming formula is the slaughtering, not the slaughtered animal. [T001-U1: the print and transcription actually read al-dhābiḥ, “the slaughterer,” at the final occurrence; “slaughtering” is a contextual emendation, not a silently verified reading.]

T001.09 — Al-Intiṣāf’s objection and al-Inṣāf’s reply [I:683–685]
The author of al-Intiṣāf says:

“What the grammarians supply is ‘I begin the act of reading.’ The validity of supplying a general expression is preferable. Do you not see that, wherever a prepositional phrase functions as a predicate, adjective, relative clause, or circumstantial expression, they supply ‘being’ or ‘remaining’ as what governs it, preferring this because it is general?

“Moreover, supplying the verb ‘begin’ independently accomplishes the purpose intended by the naming formula, since its purpose is that a beginning be made with it. Supplying the verb ‘begin’ is therefore more effective.

“As for the explicit verb ‘read’ in His saying, ‘Read in the name of your Lord’ [96:1], that is because reading is the more important thing there. For this reason the verb there is put before the phrase attached to it, unlike the basmala, in which beginning is more important.”

The author of al-Inṣāf replies:

“What al-Zamakhsharī states is more correct, since it is more specific, more directly connected with the purpose, and more comprehensive in its scope. It implies that the naming formula applies to the whole reading and accompanies it, or that the whole reading is by means of God, according to the two interpretations to be explained below. Supplying ‘I begin,’ by contrast, implies that it accompanies the beginning of the reading.

“His appeal to what grammarians supply is unhelpful, since they did this only by way of illustration and approximation. If you said, ‘Zayd is on the horse,’ ‘Zayd is among the scholars,’ or ‘Zayd is in Basra,’ you would supply ‘riding,’ ‘counted,’ and ‘residing,’ which would be more closely connected than ‘remaining.’

“As for his claim that ‘the purpose is that the naming formula be placed at the beginning,’ we accept what this entails. This actually occurs by starting with it, not by implying the verb ‘begin.’ Someone who prays and starts with the opening takbīr, or begins ablution by washing his face, does not need to imply ‘I began with that’ in order to count as beginning. He does, however, need the naming formula’s blessing and its encompassing his whole action.”

[T001-U2: the opening expression أبتدئ فعل القراءة is retained as printed. Its punctuation and relation to the argument for a general verb merit comparison with al-Intiṣāf itself.]

T001.10 — The traveller’s sentence, Arabs and Bedouin, and the wedding formula [I:685]
On “and says, ‘In God’s name’”:

This is coordinated with “halts.” The response to “when” is “the meaning is.” “Who begins in his action” qualifies “every agent.”

His expression “the Arabs’ saying,” followed by “the Bedouin’s saying,” suggests a distinction. Al-Nihāya says: “Al-aʿrāb are those who inhabit the desert and do not reside in towns. Al-ʿarab is the name of this well-known people and has no singular formed from its own expression, whether they reside in the desert or in towns.”

Al-Mughrib says: “Al-ʿarabī refers to the inhabitants of towns and villages; al-aʿrābī to the inhabitants of the deserts.”

On “for the bridegroom”:

Al-Nihāya says: “Aʿrasa the man, and he is a muʿris, means that he went in to his wife when the marriage was consummated. One does not use the form عرس, as ordinary people do.”

Al-Jāmiʿ says: “Rifāʾ means good companionship and harmony, from mending a garment. By ‘with harmony and sons’ they mean that this marriage is accompanied by both. God’s Messenger—God bless him and grant him peace—forbade it because it was one of the distinctive formulas of the Age of Ignorance.”

T001.11 — What the wedding explanation modifies [I:686]
On “meaning ‘May you wed,’ or ‘May you marry’”:

This may relate to the first expression, with “the Bedouin’s saying” parenthetical, because “with good fortune and blessing” is a general formula used of anyone intending to undertake something. Alternatively, it relates to both expressions, which is the preferable interpretation.

T001.12 — The poet’s invitation to the jinn [I:686]
On “I said, ‘To the food!’,” the verse:

Before it comes:

They came to my fire. I said, “Who are you?”
They said, “The jinn.” I said, “Good evening to you!”

Al-Aṣmaʿī said: “ʿIm ṣabāḥan, ‘Good morning,’ means anʿim, ‘Enjoy [a good morning].’ The reconstructed past verb is waʿama, with present yaʿimu. The past is not actually uttered, just as the past forms of daʿ and dhar, ‘leave,’ are not uttered.” Al-Anbārī mentioned this.

The poet claimed that the jinn came to him while he was beside his fire, and that he greeted them and invited them to eat. One says ḥasadahu l-shayʾa and ḥasadahu ʿalā l-shayʾ, “he envied him the thing.” That is: “We envy them because they eat, whereas we do not eat.”

“To the food” means “Come!”

T001.13 — Identifying the more important expression [I:686]
On “because the more important of the action”:

The action is “I recite” or “I read.” In “and what is attached to it,” the lām of mutaʿalliq is read with kasra in both occurrences: this is “In God’s name.” Min in “of the action” marks the starting point [of the comparison]. That is, the more important of “I read” and “In God’s name” is “In God’s name.”

- T001-U1 and T001-U2 remain open.
- The modern Dubai footnotes sometimes criticize the hadith chains or supply manuscript variants. They are separate editorial material, not automatically part of Ṭībī’s own commentary.

SŪRAT AL-SHARḤ (94): THE EXPANSION
Complete first-draft translation of al-Zamakhsharī and al-Ṭībī
Checkpoint C05 • 27 October 2026

Coverage: all eight verses; the complete Kashshāf chapter and all distinct authorial commentary in Futūḥ al-Ghayb XVI, 492–503. The Kashshāf text repeated within Ṭībī's edition is represented in the first layer rather than translated twice. Modern editorial apparatus is outside the authorial translation. Primary base: K-DKA IV, 770–772; T-Dubai XVI, 492–503, through the working transcriptions. This is a complete first draft, not a claim of completed critical review. Source difficulties are recorded below. Reports are translated with their source's attributions; no independent authentication is implied.

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K094.01 — Heading and verses 1–4
Meccan; eight verses. [K-DKA adds: revealed after al-Ḍuḥā.]
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.
“Have We not expanded your breast for you, and lifted from you your burden, which made your back creak, and raised your renown for you?”

K094.02 — Expansion and the burden
The question repudiates the absence of expansion, and thus affirms and establishes that the expansion occurred. It is as though He had said, “We expanded your breast for you.” Accordingly, “and We lifted” is coordinated with it in consideration of its meaning.
“We expanded your breast” means: We made it spacious enough to accommodate the concerns of prophethood and the calling of both classes of accountable beings, humans and jinn, together; or spacious enough to bear the hardships with which the unbelievers of your people and others confront you; or We expanded it by the knowledge and wisdom We deposited in it, removing the constriction and distress accompanying blindness and ignorance. Al-Ḥasan said: “It was filled with wisdom and knowledge.”
Abū Jaʿfar al-Manṣūr is reported to have read a-lam nashraḥa laka, giving the ḥāʾ a fatḥa. They said: perhaps he articulated the ḥāʾ distinctly and fully at its point of articulation, so that the listener thought he had pronounced a fatḥa.
The burden that made his back creak—that is, caused it to emit naqīḍ, the sound of creaking and coming apart because of its weight—is a figure for what weighed upon God's Messenger and distressed him: lapses before prophethood; or his lack of knowledge of rulings and revealed laws; or his desperate eagerness for the obstinate among his people to accept Islam and his grief over them. Lifting it from him means forgiving him; or teaching him the revealed laws; or establishing his excuse after he had conveyed the message and exerted himself fully in conveying it.
Anas read “and We loosened” (wa-ḥalalnā), and “and We laid down” (wa-ḥaṭaṭnā). Ibn Masʿūd read: “and We loosened from you your heavy load” (wa-ḥalalnā ʿanka wiqraka).

K094.03 — Raising his renown; clarification after an initial general expression
Raising his renown consists in joining his mention to God's in the testimony of faith, the call to prayer, the announcement that prayer is beginning, the tashahhud, and sermons; and in more than one place in the Qurʾān: “God and His Messenger have the greater right that they should please Him” (9:62); “whoever obeys God and His Messenger” (4:13); and “obey God and obey the Messenger” (5:92). It also consists in calling him “God's Messenger” and “God's Prophet.” His mention in the scriptures of earlier peoples, and the pledge taken from the prophets and their communities that they would believe in him, belong to this as well.
If you ask what benefit there is in adding “for you” (laka), when the meaning stands without it, I answer: the addition achieves what is achieved by initially leaving something unspecified and then clarifying it. It is as though He said, “Have We not expanded for you…?” One understands that something has been expanded. He then says, “your breast,” clarifying what had been understood only generally. The same applies to “for you your renown” and “from you your burden.”

K094.04 — Verses 5–6: hardship and ease
“For with hardship comes ease; indeed, with hardship comes ease.”
If you ask how “for with hardship comes ease” connects with what precedes, I answer: the idolaters used to taunt God's Messenger and the believers for their poverty and straitened circumstances, until it occurred to him that their aversion to Islam arose from its adherents' poverty and their contempt for them. God reminded him of the great blessings He had bestowed upon him, then said, “For with hardship comes ease.” It is as though He said: We have granted you all that We have granted you, so do not despair of God's bounty; with the hardship in which you now find yourselves comes ease.
If you ask: maʿa means accompaniment; how can ease and hardship accompany one another? I answer: He means that God will bring them ease a short time after the hardship in which they were living. He brings the anticipated ease so near that He makes it seem simultaneous with the hardship, to give further consolation and strengthen their hearts.

K094.05 — “One hardship will not overcome two eases”
If you ask about the saying of Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn Masʿūd, “One hardship will not overcome two eases”—also transmitted as a report traced to the Prophet, who came out one day laughing and saying, “One hardship will not overcome two eases”—I answer: this proceeds on the apparent sense and rests on strong hope, and on the principle that God's promise should be understood only in the fullest and most ample sense that its wording can bear.
The explanation is that the second sentence may repeat the first, as “woe that day to those who deny” is repeated to establish its meaning in people's minds and fix it in their hearts, or as an individual word is repeated in “Zayd came to me—Zayd.” Alternatively, the first sentence may promise that hardship will necessarily be followed by ease, and the second may be a fresh promise that hardship will be followed by ease. On the interpretation that it is a new statement, there are two eases.
The hardship is one because its definiteness must either refer to something already known—the hardship in which they were living—in which case it is the same hardship, just as Zayd is the same in “With Zayd there is wealth; with Zayd there is wealth”; or it refers to the class familiar to everyone, in which case it is likewise the same. Ease, however, is indefinite and denotes some member of its class. If the second statement is new rather than a repetition, it unquestionably denotes a member other than the first.
If you ask what the two eases mean, I answer: they may mean the conquests made easy for them in the days of God's Messenger and those made easy in the days of the caliphs; or ease in this world and ease in the next. Compare: “Say: Do you await for us anything but one of the two good outcomes?” (9:52), namely the good of victory and the good of reward.
If you ask what the indefiniteness signifies, I answer: magnification—as though He said, “With hardship comes a mighty ease—and what an ease!” In Ibn Masʿūd's codex it occurs only once. If you ask: if it is not repeated in his reading, why did he say, “By Him in whose hand is my soul, if hardship were in a hole, ease would pursue it until it entered upon it; one hardship will not overcome two eases”? I answer: by the two eases he seems to have intended the magnifying force of the indefinite “ease,” interpreting it as the ease of both abodes. Those are indeed two eases.

K094.06 — Verses 7–8: continuing in worship
“So when you have finished, exert yourself; and direct your longing toward your Lord.”
If you ask how “so when you have finished, exert yourself” connects with what precedes, I answer: after enumerating His earlier blessings and promising those to come, He urged him to gratitude, diligence in worship, and exertion in it: to join one act of worship to another, pursue them successively, and take care that none of his times be empty of them. Whenever he finishes one act of worship, he follows it with another.
Ibn ʿAbbās said: when you finish your prayer, exert yourself in supplication. Al-Ḥasan said: when you finish warfare, exert yourself in worship. Mujāhid said: when you finish your worldly business, exert yourself in prayer. Al-Shaʿbī saw a man lifting a stone and said: “This is not what someone with time to spare has been commanded to do.” Sitting idle without occupation, or occupying oneself with what benefits neither one's religion nor one's worldly affairs, is evidence of foolish judgment, feeble understanding, and heedlessness taking control. ʿUmar said: “I dislike seeing any of you idle and aimless, engaged neither in work for this world nor in work for the next.”
Abū al-Sammāl read fariġta, with kasra on the rāʾ; this is not an eloquent usage. Among novel interpretations is the report that one of the Rāfiḍa read fa-nṣib, with kasra on the ṣād, meaning: appoint ʿAlī to the imamate. Were this valid for the Rāfiḍī, it would equally be valid for a Nāṣibī to read it in this fashion and make it a command to naṣb, which is hatred and hostility toward ʿAlī.
“And direct your longing toward your Lord”: make Him exclusively the object of your longing; ask only for His bounty, relying upon Him. Fa-raġġib is also read: encourage people to seek what is with Him.
The Prophet is reported to have said: “Whoever recites ‘Have We not expanded,’ it is as though he came to me while I was grieving and relieved my grief.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T094.01 — The force of the question (XVI, 492)
On “it affirms and establishes the expansion”: He repudiates the absence of expansion. Once that absence is repudiated, expansion is established, because the interrogative hamza expresses repudiation; repudiation is negation, and negation applied to negation becomes affirmation. The hamza may not be treated here as a question seeking acknowledgment (taqrīr).

T094.02 — The breadth of the Prophet's breast (492–493)
On “We made it spacious enough to accommodate the concerns of prophethood and the calling of both classes of beings”: if you ask why he explains expansion of the breast here more comprehensively and expansively than at “He said: My Lord, expand my breast for me” (20:25), where he says, “When he was commanded to go to Pharaoh the tyrant, he knew that he had been charged with a mighty undertaking and a tremendous affair, requiring endurance possible only for someone with a steadfast heart and an expansive breast; he therefore asked his Lord to grant him expansion of his breast”—I answer: concerns are proportionate to aspirations. How well al-Ṣāḥib said:
“A woman said: Why have anxieties assailed you,
when your command is obeyed among the nations?
I said: Leave me with the grief that chokes me;
anxieties are proportionate to aspirations.”
Every situation calls for its own discourse. When the one to whom God spoke was sent to Pharaoh the tyrant, he requested expansion, as God says: “Go to Pharaoh: he has exceeded all bounds. He said: My Lord, expand my breast for me” (20:24–25). But when the Beloved was summoned to the station of “two bow-lengths, or nearer” (53:9), he was told, “Have We not expanded your breast for you?” This will appear in the report of Mālik ibn Ṣaʿṣaʿa.
Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq said: “Have We not expanded your breast for you to witness Me and behold Me?” Ibn ʿAṭāʾ said: “Have We not emptied your innermost consciousness of everything, so that you ceased to behold created existence and whatever is other than the Real? Your breast was expanded for vision, whereas Moses' breast was expanded for speech.” Sahl said: “Have We not broadened your breast with the light of messengership, making it a repository of realities?”

T094.03 — Opening the breast; the root meaning of sharḥ (493–494)
On al-Ḥasan's saying, “It was filled with wisdom and knowledge”: perhaps he alludes to what we have transmitted from al-Bukhārī, Muslim, al-Tirmidhī, and al-Nasāʾī, from Mālik ibn Ṣaʿṣaʿa, from the Prophet: “While I was beside the House, between sleeping and waking, a basin of gold containing Zamzam water was brought to me, and my breast was opened as far as such-and-such.” Qatāda said—addressing Anas—“What does he mean?” He replied: “To the bottom of my belly.” He continued: “My heart was taken out and washed with Zamzam water, then put back in its place, then filled with faith and wisdom. Then an animal smaller than a mule and larger than a donkey was brought to me”—the lengthy report continues.
The Imām [Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī] said: “It is not unlikely that the black blood they washed from his heart was a sign of inclination and readiness to commit sins, and reluctance to perform acts of obedience. Once they removed it, that was a sign that its possessor would persevere in obedience and guard against evil deeds. God does whatever He wills and decrees whatever He wishes.”
Al-Rāghib said: “The original sense of sharḥ is spreading out flesh and the like. One says sharaḥtu al-laḥma and sharraḥtuhu. From this comes expansion of the breast: its spreading out through divine light, tranquility from God, and a spirit from Him.”

T094.04 — The reading nashraḥa (494–495)
On “he read a-lam nashraḥa, with fatḥa on the ḥāʾ”: its original form is nashraḥan. The nūn was omitted and the fatḥa on the ḥāʾ retained as an indication of it. This is in al-Muntaqā.
Ibn Jinnī said: “A-lam nashraḥa, with fatḥa on the ḥāʾ, is transmitted from Abū Jaʿfar al-Manṣūr. Ibn Mujāhid said: ‘This is wholly impermissible.’” Ibn Jinnī said: “The obvious position, and what accords with customary usage, is as Ibn Mujāhid states. Something like it nevertheless occurs in what I read to Abū ʿAlī in Abū Zayd's Nawādir:
‘From which of death's two days shall I flee—
a day not decreed, or a day decreed?’
It is said that he intended lam yuqdar-an, with the light emphatic nūn. In our view omitting it is impermissible, because the emphatic nūn is most closely allied to expansion and elaboration, not concision and abbreviation. In Abū Zayd's Nawādir there is another verse, said to be fabricated:
‘Strike away the anxieties that visit you by night,
as with a sword you strike a horse's forelock.’
He intended iḍrib-an, with the light nūn, and omitted it.”

T094.05 — The burden and the ordered correspondence of its meanings (495)
On “the sound of creaking and coming apart”: al-Ṣiḥāḥ says: “Anqaḍa al-ḥimlu ẓahrahu means that the load weighed upon his back. Its original sense concerns sound. Naqīḍ is the sound of camel-litters and riding gear.” Al-Rāghib says: “‘It made his back creak’ means that it broke it until it produced a creaking sound; the naqīḍ of joints is their sound. ‘Back’ is a metaphor, likening sins to a load that weighs its bearer down.”
On “lifting it from him means forgiving him”: the expression consists of a subject and predicate. This sentence is coordinated with a corresponding sentence, “the burden is a figure”—that is, a metaphor preceded by a comparison. “And We lifted” is then a reinforcement of the metaphor (tarshīḥ), because it is a description appropriate to that from which the metaphor is drawn. This is what he means by “lifting it from him means forgiving him,” and the rest.
When “burden” is borrowed for sin, its reinforcement is appropriately understood as forgiveness. When it is borrowed for ignorance of rulings, the fitting explanation is instruction in the revealed laws. When it is understood as his desperate eagerness for their acceptance of Islam, the corresponding interpretation is the establishment of his excuse: do not be consumed by eagerness for their guidance or let yourself perish in grief over them, for you have exerted yourself in conveying the message and have established the proof against them. This is the rhetorical pattern of gathering and ordered distribution (laff wa-nashr).

T094.06 — Readings, renown, and the prophetic covenant (496)
On “Anas read wa-ḥalalnā and wa-ḥaṭaṭnā”: Ibn Jinnī reports that Abān said: “I said to Anas, ‘Abū Ḥamza, what of wa-waḍaʿnā?’ He said: ‘Waḍaʿnā, ḥalalnā, and ḥaṭaṭnā are equivalent. Gabriel came to the Prophet and said: Recite according to seven aḥruf, provided you do not mix forgiveness with punishment or punishment with forgiveness.’”
I say: a lengthy report from Anas occurs in Muslim, al-Tirmidhī, Abū Dāwūd, and al-Nasāʾī, ending: “Then he said: Each of them is healing and sufficient—whether you say ‘All-Hearing, All-Knowing’ or ‘Mighty, Wise’—provided you do not conclude a verse of punishment with mercy or a verse of mercy with punishment.” [These are Ṭībī's transmitted attributions.]
On “calling him God's Messenger and God's Prophet”: Jaʿfar said: “No one mentions you as messenger without mentioning Me as Lord.” Ibn ʿAṭāʾ said: “I made faith in Me complete through mentioning you together with Me.”
On “the pledge taken from the prophets and their communities that they would believe in him”: perhaps he means what is indicated by “When God took the covenant of the prophets: Whatever scripture and wisdom I give you, then a messenger comes to you confirming what is with you, you shall believe in him and you shall support him” (3:81).

T094.07 — The added laka and the connection with consolation (497)
On “the addition of ‘for you’”: the author said: “Laka may be added to express particularization, as in ‘You alone we worship’ (1:5), although the meaning is complete with ‘we worship You’; or it may be a matter of giving priority to what is more important.”
Al-Sayyid Ibn al-Shajarī says in al-Amālī: “The lām in laka is causal, as when you say, ‘I did that to honor you.’ If you omit the lām, you say, ‘I did it, honoring you.’ If you omit the verbal noun, you restore the lām and say, ‘I did that for you.’ Thus the meaning is: Have We not expanded your breast for your guidance? Compare: ‘Whomever God wishes to guide, He expands his breast for Islam’ (6:125). Once the verbal noun is omitted, the lām must be expressed. Likewise, ‘We raised your renown for you’ means: We raised your renown to honor you.”
On “the idolaters used to taunt”: his explanation amounts to this. The occasion of revelation of “Have We not expanded your breast for you?” was that the idolaters taunted God's Messenger and the believers for poverty, causing him concern. This was dispelled by “Have We not expanded your breast for you?” The question repudiates the denial of expansion, thereby emphatically establishing it: have you not seen what God did for you at the outset—expanding your breast and raising your renown—when you knew nothing of what you know now and your name was then obscure? We did for you what We did. Judge what is to come by that, and do not worry about their taunting you and the believers for poverty, for with hardship comes ease.

T094.08 — The report and the apparent sense (498)
On “it has been transmitted as a report traced to the Prophet”: Mālik transmits in al-Muwaṭṭaʾ from Zayd ibn Aslam: “Abū ʿUbayda wrote to ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, mentioning the assembled Roman forces and his apprehension concerning them. ʿUmar wrote back: To proceed: whatever hardship befalls a believing servant, God brings relief after it. One hardship will not overcome two eases.”
On “this proceeds on the apparent sense”: ‘apparent’ (ẓāhir) means wording admitting more than one interpretation, with one of them rendered more probable by a cogent indication. What they said follows the apparent sense: the revelation admits both repetition and a new statement. The indication favoring one possibility—the new statement, because it is fuller and more ample—is that God's promise is to be understood in the fullest of the possible senses. “And on the principle that God's promise…” is an explanatory coordination with “and rests on strong hope,” which is similarly related to “proceeds on the apparent sense.” “The explanation is…” sets out the two possibilities.

T094.09 — How the unrepeated reading can also signify two eases (499)
On this basis, even without repetition—as in Ibn Masʿūd's reading—the intended meaning is conveyed. The indefinite yusran can signify either some instance of ease or magnification. Since the matter rests on strong hope, the latter is preferred. The difference is that the first interpretation signifies the intended meaning by linguistic convention, as will follow, while the second signifies it by implication and indirect expression (kināya). Magnification in yusran requires ease to reach its utmost extent; otherwise it would not mean ease in both abodes. From this follows the multiplicity of ease, and the saying, “One hardship will not overcome two eases.” This is what he indicates by “those are indeed two eases.”
If this meaning is intended through repetition, it is more eloquent than treating the sentence as a fresh statement. Without the transmitted saying and prophetic report drawing attention to this subtle point, it would not be understood. One may also say: since the verse concerns the noble Companions and promises them relief after distress, it must be understood as ease in both abodes. In this world that consists of wealth after poverty, strength after weakness, and honor after humiliation. As for the next world, there is no dispute about it.

T094.10 — Definite and indefinite repetition (499–500)
On “the hardship is one”: know that, according to the exact investigators, the definite article lām is assigned to indication and reference to something known. The author of al-Takhmīr says: “The lām is for indication itself. Sometimes the indication points to an individual already familiar to the addressee, and sometimes to a class. Its meaning is one in every case. Understand this: people's errors concerning it are considerable, and it is a point of doctrinal grammatical importance.”
I say: it consequently requires something previously available to be indicated. Whenever the discourse contains something that can serve as such a referent in any way, that is selected. Al-Bazdawī says: “The defining lām refers back to what is known: a thing is mentioned and then returned to, the second being the first. An example is our scholars' ruling about someone who acknowledges a thousand with a particular qualification, then acknowledges it again with the same qualification: the second is the first. If each expression is indefinite, there is disagreement over whether unity of the sitting is a condition for the second to be identical with the first. According to Abū Ḥanīfa it is; according to Abū Yūsuf it is not.”
The author of al-Muṭliʿ transmits from al-Farrāʾ that when the Arabs mention an indefinite noun and then repeat an indefinite noun like it, they become two: “When you earn a dirham, spend a dirham.” The second differs from the first. If they repeat it in definite form, it is the same. Al-Zajjāj states something similar.
Al-Sayyid says in al-Amālī: “Hardship is definite and ease indefinite because, when a noun is repeated indefinitely, the second differs from the first: ‘A man came to me, and I said to a man such-and-such.’ The same applies when the first is definite and the second indefinite: ‘The man arrived, and I said to a man such-and-such.’ If the first is indefinite and the second definite, the second is the first; likewise when a definite noun follows a definite noun: ‘The man arrived, and I honored the man.’ Hence Ibn ʿAbbās said, ‘One hardship will not overcome two eases.’”

T094.11 — The question about indefiniteness; successive acts of devotion (501–502)
On “then what does this indefiniteness mean?”: the fāʾ indicates an objection. If the two eases mean what you have explained in either of the two ways, they ought to be definite; why, then, are they indefinite?
On “when you finish your prayer, exert yourself in supplication”: this is coordinated with “when he finishes one act of worship, he follows it with another.” Both “you have finished” and “exert yourself” are unrestricted. They may remain unrestricted: when you finish one act of worship, follow it with another. Or they may be specified as prayer and supplication, because prayer is the best of the acts of worship and supplication is its marrow; or as warfare and worship, as in the saying, “We have returned from the lesser struggle to the greater struggle”; or as worldly business and prayer, because ‘having free time’ is most often used of worldly affairs. An example is the report, “[Take advantage of] your free time before you become occupied.” This transmission from Mujāhid is mentioned in Sharḥ al-Sunna.
On “idle and aimless” (fāriġan sabahlalan): al-Nihāya says: “In ʿUmar's report, ‘I dislike seeing any of you aimless, engaged neither in work for this world nor in work for the next,’ the indefiniteness in ‘world’ and ‘next world’ goes back to the noun annexed to them, namely ‘work’: neither in any work belonging to this world's affairs nor in any work belonging to the next world's affairs. One says jāʾa yamshī sabahlalan when someone comes and goes idly, engaged in nothing.”

T094.12 — Exclusive longing and the unusual conjunction (503)
On “make Him exclusively the object of your longing”: particularization is conveyed by placing the prepositional phrase before the verb. Al-Sayyid says in al-Amālī: “The fāʾ occurs together with the wāw, and ilā is governed by what comes after the fāʾ. Similarly, in ‘and your garments—purify them’ (74:4), what precedes the fāʾ is made accusative by what follows it. This is among the remarkable features of their speech: ordinarily fāʾ coordinates, introduces a response, or introduces something resembling a response, such as the predicate of an incomplete noun—that is, a relative pronoun whose relative clause is verbal. Here it falls outside its assigned function.”
The sūrah is complete, by God's praise and aid. God suffices us; how excellent a trustee He is.

AL-TĪN (95) — THE FIG
Complete first-pass translation of both authorial layers • C06 • 8 November 2026

SOURCE AND STATUS
Kashshāf: Dār al-Kitāb al-ʿArabī, 1407 AH, IV:773–775, OpenITI Shamela0023627-ara1; compared with the embedded base in Ṭībī and the Turkish edition, VI:1354–1358. Ṭībī: Futūḥ al-Ghayb, Dubai, 1434/2013, XVI:504–508, OpenITI Sham19Y0020891-ara1; printed XVI:505 selectively checked. Both authorial layers are translated through their endings; repeated base text and modern editorial footnotes are not presented as distinct Ṭībī commentary. Reports and historical medical statements retain their authors’ attribution. This is a complete first draft, with final scholarly review pending.

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K095.01 — Heading and verses [IV:773]
Meccan; eight verses. [The DKA heading additionally places its revelation after al-Burūj.]
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

By the fig and the olive, (1) and Mount Sīnīn, (2) and this secure city: (3) We have indeed created the human being in the finest proportion; (4) then We returned him to the lowest of the low, (5) except those who believe and do righteous deeds: theirs is a reward unceasing. (6) So what makes you deny the requital after this? (7) Is God not the wisest of judges? (8)

K095.02 — The fig and olive [IV:773]
He swears by these two because they are remarkable among the varieties of fruit-bearing trees. It is related that a dish of figs was presented to God’s Messenger. He ate some and said to his companions: “Eat! If I were to say that a fruit had descended from Paradise, I would say it was this one, because the fruit of Paradise has no stones. So eat them: they stop haemorrhoids and help against gout.”

Muʿādh ibn Jabal passed an olive tree, took a twig from it, and cleaned his teeth with it. He said: “I heard God’s Messenger say, ‘What an excellent tooth-stick is the olive, from the blessed tree! It sweetens the mouth and removes decay.’ And I heard him say, ‘It is my tooth-stick and the tooth-stick of the prophets before me.’” From Ibn ʿAbbās: “It is this fig of yours and this olive of yours.”

It is also said that they are two mountains of the Holy Land called in Syriac Ṭūr Tīnā and Ṭūr Zaytā, because they are places where figs and olives grow. Another view makes “the fig” the mountains between Ḥulwān and Hamadhān, and “the olive” the mountains of Syria, because these fruits grow there: as though He said, “By the places where figs and olives grow.”

K095.03 — Sīnīn, the secure city, and the blessed places [IV:773–774]
Al-ṭūr, “the mountain,” is put in a genitive construction with Sīnīn, which is the locality. Sīnūn resembles Yabrūn in permitting declension with wāw and yāʾ, or retention of the yāʾ while the nūn takes the case vowels.

“The city” is Mecca—may God protect it. Amīn derives from amuna al-rajulu amānatan fa-huwa amīn, “the man proved trustworthy, and so is trustworthy.” Ammān is also said, just as karrām is used for karīm. Its trustworthiness consists in protecting whoever enters it, as a trustworthy person protects what is entrusted to him. It may also be a faʿīl form with a passive sense, from aminahu, since the city is secure from calamities. Thus it is described as secure in His saying, “a secure sanctuary” [28:57], meaning one possessing security.

The purpose of swearing by these things is to disclose the honour of the blessed places, and the good and blessing manifested there through the residence of prophets and righteous people. The place where figs and olives grow is Abraham’s place of migration and Jesus’ birthplace and place of upbringing. The Mount is the place from which Moses was called. Mecca is the location of the House that is guidance for all peoples, and God’s Messenger’s birthplace and the place of his commissioning.

K095.04 — Finest proportion and the lowest condition [IV:774]
“In the finest proportion”: in the finest adjustment of his shape and form, and the most harmonious arrangement of his limbs. Then, when he failed to give thanks for the blessing of that beautiful, upright, well-proportioned creation, his final outcome was that We returned him lower than those low in constitution and composition: uglier in form than the ugly, and most deformed in creation. These are the inhabitants of the Fire. Or: lower than the lowest inhabitants of its descending levels.

Or: after that proportioning and beautification, We returned him lower than those lowest in beauty of form and shape, reversing his constitution. His back, once straight, became bent; his hair, once black, turned white; his skin, once tender, dried and wrinkled; his hearing and sight, once sharp, grew dull. Everything about him changed: his gait became shuffling, his voice faint, his strength weakness, and his mental alertness senility.

ʿAbd Allāh [ibn Masʿūd] read asfala al-sāfilīn, with the definite article: “the lowest of the low.”

K095.05 — The exception [IV:774]
If you ask how the exception works on the two interpretations, I answer: on the first it is a connected exception, and its connection is plain. On the second it is disconnected: “But those among the aged who were righteous have an abiding, uninterrupted reward”—for their obedience, their patience under God’s trial of old age and decrepitude, their endurance of hardships, and their continuing to worship despite their faltering ability to rise.

K095.06 — Who is addressed, and what makes him a denier? [IV:774–775]
If you ask who is addressed by “So what makes you deny?”, I answer: the human being is addressed through a shift of person. That is: what makes you a liar on account of the requital and your denial of it after this proof? You lie when you deny recompense, because everyone who denies the truth is a liar. What, then, compels you to become a liar through denying recompense? The bāʾ is like that in His saying, “those who take him as their patron and those who, because of him, associate others [with God]” [16:100].

The meaning is that you can see no clearer proof of the Creator’s power than the human being’s creation from a drop, his proportioning as a well-formed human, his gradual progress through stages of growth until he reaches maturity and full stature, and then his reversal until he reaches the most abject age. Whoever has power to do all this with the human being is not incapable of bringing him back. So what, human being, is the cause of your denying recompense after this decisive proof?

It is also said that the address is to God’s Messenger.

“Is God not the wisest of judges?” is a threat to the unbelievers: He will judge them as they deserve. It is related from the Prophet that, when he recited it, he said: “Indeed; and I am among those who bear witness to that.”

K095.07 — Closing report [IV:775]
From God’s Messenger: “Whoever recites Sūrat ‘By the fig,’ God gives him two qualities: well-being and certainty as long as he remains in this world; and when he dies, God gives him a reward according to the number of those who have recited this sūrah.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T095.01 — Fruit-stones and the properties of figs and olives [XVI:504–505]
Meccan; eight verses. In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

On “without stones”: it is transmitted both with a quiescent jīm, ʿajm, and with fatḥa, ʿajam. Dīwān al-Adab has: “ʿAjam, with the middle consonant vowelled, means fruit-stones”; it does not list ʿajm in this sense. Al-Jawharī says: “Ordinary people say ʿajm, with the jīm quiescent.”

On “they stop haemorrhoids,” the Qāḍī [al-Bayḍāwī] says: “The fig is a pleasant fruit with no waste. As food it is light and quickly digested, and as a medicine it has many benefits: it loosens the bowels, dissolves phlegm, cleanses the kidneys, removes gravel from the bladder, opens obstructions of the liver and spleen, and fattens the body. The olive is a fruit, a condiment, and a medicine. Its oil is light, very beneficial, and pleasant; yet it sometimes grows where it contains no oil, as in the mountains.”

T095.02 — Tooth decay and trustworthy places [XVI:505]
On “removes decay”: one says ḥafirat asnānuhu ḥafaran when the sockets of his teeth—that is, their roots—have deteriorated. One also says ḥafarat ḥafran; ḥafra denotes a single occurrence.

On “and so is amīn; ammān is also said”: that is, they say, “in a trustworthy place.”

T095.03 — The vocabulary of ageing [XVI:506]
On “dried and wrinkled,” al-Asās says: “Tashannana jilduhu min al-haram means his skin shrivelled and dried from old age. One says, ‘an old man like a worn-out waterskin.’”

On “tender,” baḍḍ, with the single-dotted bāʾ and dotted ḍād: al-Asās says: “Al-Aṣmaʿī said: abyaḍ baḍḍ means intensely white. Al-Mubarrad said: it means a person with fine skin on which everything leaves an impression. One says imraʾa ghaḍḍa baḍḍa, a fresh, tender-skinned woman.”

On “his gait became shuffling”: dalīf is slow walking. Al-Asās says: “The old man and the fettered man dalafa dalīfan wa-dulūfan; this is a little more than creeping.”

On “senility”: kharaf, with the middle consonant vowelled, means deterioration of the mind.

T095.04 — The two interpretations and the exception [XVI:506–507]
On “how does the exception work on the two interpretations?”: someone has taken this to mean the Ḥijāzī and Tamīmī grammatical schools. That is not correct. It means the two interpretations just mentioned, as the answer and the introductory fāʾ in the question show.

On “on the first it is a connected exception”: that is, when being returned to “the lowest of the low” means being returned lower than those low in constitution and composition, namely the inhabitants of the Fire, or lower than the lowest inhabitants of its descending levels. Al-Wāḥidī reports from Mujāhid: “‘Then We returned him’ to the Fire. The Fire is the lowest of the low because Jahannam has levels one beneath another. He then makes the exception, ‘except those who believe and do righteous deeds’: these are not returned to the Fire.”

On “on the second it is disconnected”: that is, when “the lowest of the low” means being returned lower than those lowest in beauty of form and shape. Hence he says: “But those among the aged who were righteous have an abiding reward.”

T095.05 — The causal bāʾ [XVI:507]
On “‘those who, because of him, associate others’ [16:100]”: that is, because of Satan they associate others with God. The bāʾ in bihi is not the complement of mushrikūn, “associating others”; its proper complement is omitted.

T095.06 — The alternative addressee and the force of the question [XVI:508]
On “it is also said that the address is to God’s Messenger”: this is coordinated with “the human being is addressed.” On this interpretation there is no shift of person, and mā has the sense of man, “who”: who, then, calls you a liar, truthful and confirmed Messenger, regarding the true religion you have brought—or on account of religion—after the appearance of these proofs indicating your prophethood? “Is God not the wisest of judges?” He will judge between you and those who deny you.

If the address is taken to concern the human being, one should adopt the interpretation involving a shift of person, because of the preceding “We have indeed created the human being in the finest proportion,” and take the bāʾ as causal, since the human being is himself the denier. The meaning is: “Human being, what drives you to be a liar through denying recompense?” The expression both conveys wonder and arouses it. Having established that He created the human being in the finest proportion and then returned him to the most abject age, God has indicated the perfection of His power to originate and restore. He then asks why the human being denies recompense, because the cause of something wondered at is obscure. This is, as you see, manifest and clear. It is indicated by his saying, “So what, human being, is the cause of your denying recompense after this decisive proof?” On this interpretation, “Is God not the wisest of judges?” threatens the unbelievers, declaring that He judges them as they deserve.

T095.07 — The response to the final verse [XVI:508]
On “he said, ‘Indeed; and I am among those who bear witness to that’”: the report is transmitted by al-Tirmidhī and Abū Dāwūd from Abū Hurayra, who said that God’s Messenger said: “Whoever among you recites ‘By the fig and the olive,’ and reaches ‘Is God not the wisest of judges?’, should say: ‘Indeed; and I am among those who bear witness to that.’”

End of the sūrah.

TEXTUAL NOTES
K095-U1: The derivative forms ammān and karrām are retained as morphological examples; the unvowelled electronic source alone does not establish their full vocalization. The chapter’s argument is about the city’s trustworthiness and security.
T095-U1: XVI:505 prints عند الغذاء, “as food,” correcting the electronic الغداء, “the midday meal.” The source’s historical therapeutic claims are translated as quoted claims, not endorsed as medical guidance.
K095-U2: The verse rendering gives an intelligible English question; K095.06 and T095.06 preserve the author’s more specific grammatical construal, “what makes you a liar on account of denying requital,” and its alternative address to the Prophet.

AL-QADR (97) — DECREE
Complete first-pass translation of both authorial layers • C06 • 8 November 2026

SOURCE AND STATUS
Kashshāf: Dār al-Kitāb al-ʿArabī, 1407 AH, IV:780–781, OpenITI Shamela0023627-ara1; compared with the embedded base in Ṭībī and the Turkish edition, VI:1372–1374. Ṭībī: Futūḥ al-Ghayb, Dubai, 1434/2013, XVI:522–525, OpenITI Sham19Y0020891-ara1; printed XVI:524 selectively checked. Both authorial layers are translated through their endings. Repeated Kashshāf base text is not counted again as Ṭībī’s commentary. Modern editorial footnotes are excluded. Transmitted reports retain the authors’ attributions; they have not been independently authenticated. Full draft coverage is distinct from final scholarly review.

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K097.01 — Heading and verses [IV:780]
Meccan; also said to be Medinan. Five verses. [The DKA heading additionally places its revelation after ʿAbasa.]
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

Surely We sent it down on the Night of Decree. (1) And what will make you know what the Night of Decree is? (2) The Night of Decree is better than a thousand months. (3) The angels and the Spirit descend in it, by their Lord’s permission, for every matter. (4) Peace it is, until the rising of dawn. (5)

K097.02 — The Qurʾān’s greatness and its descent [IV:780]
He magnifies the Qurʾān in three ways. First, He attributes its sending down to Himself and makes it His exclusive act, to the exclusion of anyone else. Second, He uses its pronoun instead of its explicit name, attesting its prominence and its having no need for attention to be called to it. Third, He elevates the rank of the time in which it was sent down.

It is related that it was sent down all at once on the Night of Decree, from the Preserved Tablet to the lowest heaven. Gabriel dictated it to the scribes; thereafter he brought it down to God’s Messenger in instalments over twenty-three years. From al-Shaʿbī: the meaning is, “We began sending it down on the Night of Decree.”

K097.03 — When the night occurs and why it is concealed [IV:780]
They differ about its time. Most hold that it falls in Ramaḍān, on one of the odd nights of the final ten; the most frequent view is that it is the seventh of those nights. Perhaps the reason for concealing it is that the person seeking it will keep many nights alive in worship, hoping to coincide with it, so that his worship increases and his reward is multiplied; and that, if it were made known, people should not rely on attaining its excellence and then neglect the other nights.

K097.04 — Decree, distinction, and a thousand months [IV:780–781]
The meaning of “the Night of Decree” is the night on which affairs are determined and decided, from His saying, “In it every wise affair is distinguished” [44:4]. It is also said to have been named thus because of its importance and distinction above all other nights.

“And what will make you know what the Night of Decree is?” means that your knowledge has not reached the utmost of its excellence or the limit of its exalted rank. He then explains this by saying that it is “better than a thousand months.” Its excellence reaches this height because of the religious benefits He mentions as occurring in it: the descent of the angels and the Spirit, and the determination of every wise affair.

Concerning the specification of this period, it is mentioned that God’s Messenger spoke of a man of the Children of Israel who bore arms in God’s path for a thousand months. The believers marvelled at this and their own deeds seemed paltry to them; so they were given a night better than that warrior’s entire period. It is also said that, in former times, a man was not called a worshipper until he had worshipped God for a thousand months. They were therefore given a night which, if they kept it alive in worship, would entitle them to be called worshippers more than those worshippers of old.

K097.05 — The descent and the peace of the night [IV:781]
“Descend”: to the lowest heaven; or, according to another view, to the earth. “The Spirit” is Gabriel. It is also said to be a kind of angelic creature which the angels see only on that night.

“For every matter”: they descend on account of every matter God has decreed for that year until the next. The reading min kulli imriʾ, “for every person,” is also transmitted: on account of every human being. It is said that, on that night, they encounter no believing man or woman without greeting them with peace.

“Peace it is”: it is nothing but safety; that is, God decrees in it only safety and good, whereas on other nights He decrees both affliction and safety. Or it is nothing but greetings of peace, because of the many greetings they address to believers.

Maṭlaʿ is read both with fatḥa and with kasra on the lām: maṭlaʿ and maṭliʿ.

K097.06 — Closing report [IV:781]
From God’s Messenger: “Whoever recites Sūrat al-Qadr is given a reward like that of someone who fasted Ramaḍān and kept the Night of Decree alive in worship.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T097.01 — Exclusive agency and the night’s reflected honour [XVI:522–523]
Meccan; five verses. In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful. [The embedded Kashshāf heading instead records disagreement about its place of revelation.]

On “and makes it His exclusive act”: he means that the construction belongs to the category of placing the semantic agent first, as in “I took care of your concern,” and “I fulfilled your need.” The choice of the plural form conveys a magnification beneath which every other magnification falls.

On “elevates the rank of the time in which it was sent down”: there is a subtle point here. He first says, “He magnifies the Qurʾān in three ways,” then says, “elevates the rank of the time.” The expected expression would be “elevates its rank by sending it down on this night.” He departs from that expression to indicate that the night was honoured by its descent within it, becoming possessed of importance and distinction. The Qurʾān’s own distinction and importance therefore follow all the more. He then rises further in elevating the night’s rank by saying, “And what will make you know what the Night of Decree is?”; higher still with “The Night of Decree is better than a thousand months”; and higher yet with “The angels and the Spirit descend in it.”

T097.02 — How “sending down” applies to speech [XVI:523]
On “it is related that it was sent down all at once”: if you ask, “In your explanation of the preface you said that sending down means moving a thing from above to below, and that this belongs to bodies and is not literally realized in speech. Speech is therefore described by an attribute of its bearer because of its connection with him. This figurative use works when Gabriel brings the Qurʾān down to the Prophet, but how does it work for its descent from the Tablet to heaven, since that is without an intermediary?”—I answer: in that case sending down is borrowed from bodies and applied to meanings. The transfer of the Qurʾān from the Tablet to heaven, and its being established there, is likened to a body’s descent from a higher place to a lower, and it is said: “Surely We sent it down on the Night of Decree.”

On this basis, its manifestation in the visible world—that is, the Tablet—from the unseen world, which is the higher world, can also be explained as a descent. On the first interpretation it is a transferred expression without comparison, a majāz mursal; on the second it is a figurative expression preceded by a comparison.

T097.03 — Ubayy’s testimony concerning Ramaḍān [XVI:523–524]
On “that it falls in Ramaḍān”: we have transmitted from Muslim, al-Tirmidhī, and Abū Dāwūd, from Zirr ibn Ḥubaysh, who said: “I heard Ubayy ibn Kaʿb being told, ‘ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd says: whoever stands in prayer throughout the year will encounter the Night of Decree.’ Ubayy replied, ‘By God, besides whom there is no god, it is certainly in Ramaḍān’—he swore and made no exception—‘and, by God, I know which night it is. It is the night on which God’s Messenger commanded us to stand in prayer: the twenty-seventh night.’” The report continues.

T097.04 — Eternal determination and the meaning of qadr [XVI:524]
On “the night on which affairs are determined”: the Imām [Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī] transmits from al-Wāḥidī that qadr in the language means taqdīr: making something according to the measure of another, without increase or diminution. He says: “It is named thus because it is the night for the determination of affairs and judgements. From Ibn ʿAbbās: God Most High determined in it everything that would occur in that year—rain, provision, bringing to life, and causing to die—until the following year, as in His saying, ‘In it every wise affair is distinguished’ [44:4]. The meaning is not that God’s determination comes into being only on that night. He determined all measures in pre-eternity, before creating the heavens and earth. Rather, what is meant is making those determinations manifest to the angels.”

On “it is also said to have been named thus because of its importance”: the Imām transmits from al-Zuhrī: “The Night of Qadr is the night of greatness and distinction, from the expression ‘So-and-so has qadr with so-and-so,’ meaning standing and honour. His saying, ‘The Night of Decree is better than a thousand months,’ supports this. It may mean that whoever performs acts of obedience becomes possessed of rank and distinction, or that acts of obedience themselves possess additional worth and distinction on that night.” From Abū Bakr al-Warrāq: “It is called the Night of Qadr because in it a book possessing qadr descended, through the tongue of an angel possessing qadr, upon a community possessing qadr.”

T097.05 — The syntax of “Peace it is” [XVI:525]
On “it is nothing but safety”: he means that hiya, “it,” is the subject and salām, “peace,” the predicate. The predicate is placed first, and the night is made peace itself, to convey exclusivity.

The author of al-Kashf says: “Hiya is a subject and salām a preceding predicate. Salām has the sense of an active participle: it brings peace. This interpretation is necessary if ḥattā, ‘until,’ is to depend on it. If salām were taken as a verbal noun, ḥattā could not depend on it, because one may not separate a governing expression from its complement. It may instead depend on ‘the angels descend.’ Hiya cannot be the subject with the phrase beginning ḥattā as its predicate, for that would convey no useful information: every night has this characteristic [of lasting until dawn].”

T097.06 — The two readings of maṭlaʿ [XVI:525]
On “maṭlaʿ is read”: al-Kisāʾī reads maṭliʿ, with kasra on the lām; the others read it with fatḥa. Al-Zajjāj says: “With fatḥa it is the verbal noun, meaning rising: one says ṭalaʿa al-fajru ṭulūʿan wa-maṭlaʿan, ‘dawn rose.’ With kasra it is a noun for the time of rising.” According to some, it cannot here mean the place of rising. God knows best.

The sūrah is complete, praise be to God Most High.

TEXTUAL NOTES
K097-U1: The first interpretative statement makes God the exclusive agent of revelation; Ṭībī’s explicit grammatical explanation confirms this. The Turkish comparator’s wording appears to make the Qurʾān itself the agent; it has not been followed.
K097-U2: The DKA text supplies the two distinct explanations of the thousand months. The embedded base in the Ṭībī transcription telescopes part of their wording; the complete DKA passage is retained.
T097-U1: Ṣila and mawṣūl in the discussion of the verbal noun concern the relation of a governing expression to its dependent material; the intervening hiya is the difficulty. The English renders the grammatical relation rather than implying an ordinary relative pronoun.

AL-ZALZALAH (99) — THE EARTHQUAKE
Complete first-pass translation of both authorial layers • C06 • 8 November 2026

SOURCE AND STATUS
Kashshāf: Dār al-Kitāb al-ʿArabī, 1407 AH, IV:783–785, OpenITI Shamela0023627-ara1; compared with the embedded base in Ṭībī and the Turkish edition, VI:1382–1386. Ṭībī: Futūḥ al-Ghayb, Dubai, 1434/2013, XVI:536–545, OpenITI Sham19Y0020891-ara1; printed XVI:536, 539, and 545 selectively checked. Both authorial layers are translated through their endings, including Ṭībī’s full grammatical and doctrinal discussions. Repeated base text and modern editorial footnotes are excluded from the distinct commentary. Reports retain the authors’ attributions without independent authentication. This is a complete first draft, not a final critical edition.

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K099.01 — Heading and verses [IV:783]
Medinan; also said to be Meccan. Eight verses. [The DKA heading additionally places its revelation after al-Nisāʾ.]
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

When the earth is shaken with its shaking, (1) and the earth brings out its burdens, (2) and the human being says, “What is the matter with it?”—(3) on that day it will tell its tidings, (4) because your Lord has inspired it. (5) On that day people will come forth separately, to be shown their deeds. (6) Whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it, (7) and whoever does an atom’s weight of evil will see it. (8)

K099.02 — The shaking and the burdens [IV:783]
Zilzālahā is read with kasra or fatḥa on the zāy. With kasra it is a verbal noun; with fatḥa, a noun. Among word-patterns, faʿlāl with fatḥa occurs only in a reduplicated form.

If you ask what “its shaking” means in the possessive construction, I answer: the shaking that befits it in accordance with wisdom and God’s will—the violent shaking beyond which there is none. Compare: “Honour the pious man with his honour, and abase the impious man with his abasement,” meaning the honour and abasement they respectively deserve. Or it means its entire shaking, every degree of shaking possible for it.

Athqāl is the plural of thiql, household goods, as in “They carry your burdens” [16:7]. He makes the things buried within the earth its burdens.

K099.03 — The human being’s question and the earth’s speech [IV:783–784]
“And the human being says, ‘What is the matter with it?’”—that it has shaken so violently and cast out what was in its belly. This occurs at the second blast, when it shakes and casts out its dead alive. They say this because of the dreadful event that overwhelms them, just as they say, “Who has raised us from our sleeping-place?” [36:52]. It is also said that this is the unbeliever’s speech, because he did not believe in resurrection; the believer says, “This is what the All-Merciful promised, and the messengers spoke truly” [36:52].

If you ask what the earth’s telling and its being inspired mean, I answer: this is figurative language for God’s bringing about in it states that take the place of telling by the tongue. Someone who asks, “What is the matter with it?”, looks at those states and knows why it shook and why it cast out the dead, and that this is what the prophets used to forewarn of and caution against.

It is also said that God literally makes it speak and it reports the good and evil done upon it. It is related from God’s Messenger: “It bears witness against everyone concerning what he did upon its surface.”

K099.04 — Adverbials and the objects of “tell” [IV:784]
If you ask what governs the accusative of idhā, “when,” and yawmaʾidhin, “on that day,” I answer: yawmaʾidhin substitutes for idhā, and tuḥaddithu, “it tells,” governs both. Alternatively, idhā may be governed by an omitted verb, and yawmaʾidhin by tuḥaddithu.

If you ask where the two objects of tuḥaddithu are, I answer: the first is omitted and the second is “its tidings.” The underlying expression is “it tells created beings its tidings.” But the aim is to mention its telling the tidings, not to mention created beings, in order to magnify the day.

K099.05 — “Because your Lord has inspired it” [IV:784]
If you ask what the bāʾ in “because your Lord” depends on, I answer: on “it tells.” The meaning is: it tells its tidings because your Lord inspired it and commanded it to tell.

The meaning may also be: on that day, by telling that your Lord has inspired it, it tells its tidings—on the understanding that its telling that your Lord has inspired it is itself the telling of its tidings. Compare your saying, “You have given me every counsel by counselling me about religion.”

“Because your Lord” may also substitute for “its tidings,” as though it were said, “On that day it tells of its tidings: that your Lord has inspired it,” because you can say both ḥaddathtuhu kadhā and ḥaddathtuhu bi-kadhā, “I told him such-and-such.”

Awḥā lahā means awḥā ilayhā, “inspired it.” It is figurative, like “that We say to it, ‘Be,’ and it is” [cf. 16:40; 36:82]. The poet says:
“He inspired it to be still, and it became still.”

Ibn Masʿūd read tunabbiʾu akhbārahā, “it reports its tidings”; Saʿīd ibn Jubayr read tunbiʾu, without doubling the consonant.

K099.06 — Separate departures and the order of the verses [IV:784–785]
They come forth from the exits of their graves to the standing-place “separately”: some white-faced and secure, others black-faced and frightened. Or they leave the standing-place separately, divided between the paths of Paradise and the Fire, to be shown the recompense of their deeds.

In the Prophet’s reading, li-yaraw has fatḥa: “so that they may see.” Ibn ʿAbbās and Zayd ibn ʿAlī read yurahu with ḍamma: “he will be shown it.” It is related that a Bedouin put the words “good, he will see it” later [than the corresponding clause about evil]. When told, “You have put one part before and another after,” he replied:
“Take the hollow of Harshā or its back:
either side of Harshā is a road for them.”

K099.07 — The atom’s weight and the two groups [IV:785]
A dharra is a small ant. It is also said that dharr is the dust visible in a beam of sunlight.

If you ask, “The unbeliever’s good deeds are nullified by unbelief, and the believer’s evil deeds are pardoned through avoiding major sins: what, then, is the meaning of recompense for atom-weights of good and evil?”—I answer: the meaning is “whoever does an atom’s weight of good” from the company of the felicitous, and “whoever does an atom’s weight of evil” from the company of the wretched, because this follows “people will come forth separately.”

K099.08 — Closing report [IV:785]
From God’s Messenger: “Whoever recites Sūrat ‘When the earth is shaken’ four times is like someone who recites the whole Qurʾān.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T099.01 — The word-pattern faʿlāl [XVI:536]
Medinan; nine verses. In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful. [The embedded Kashshāf heading instead records disagreement about the place of revelation; both headings here give nine verses.]

On “among word-patterns, faʿlāl with fatḥa occurs only in a reduplicated form,” al-Kawāshī has: “Nāqa jizʿāl occurs, meaning a she-camel which [limps; the printed text here reads ‘looks out/climbs’], as does qasṭāl, a word for dust; neither is reduplicated. It is said that Bahrām and Shahrām are foreign.” As for qahqār, it is a weak linguistic form. Al-Ṣiḥāḥ has: “Qahqarr, with doubled rāʾ, is hard stone. Aḥmad ibn Yaḥyā alone used to say qahqār.”

T099.02 — A shaking beyond which there is none [XVI:537]
On “beyond which there is none”: no shaking comes beyond it—that is, none surpasses it or is stronger. Al-Mughrib says: “The expression wa-in kāna laysa bi-lladhī lā baʿda lahu means ‘although it is not the ultimate in excellence.’ It derives from their saying, ‘This is something beyond which there is no further extreme of excellence or badness.’ Sometimes they abbreviate it and say laysa baʿdahu, ‘there is none beyond it’; then the lā that negates an entire class is introduced, and [baʿd] is used like a fully declinable noun.”

On “or its entire shaking”: the measure appropriate to it and attributed to it. The difference from the preceding interpretation is that the first refers to the agent and the requirement of His will. Hence he says, “the shaking that befits it in accordance with wisdom.” The second, although indicating comprehensiveness, is less intense than the first. His phrase “befits it in accordance with wisdom” alludes to his doctrinal position.

The Imām says: “That is, the shaking prescribed for it, if it is conceived as a living being. It is related that it shakes from the intensity of Isrāfīl’s voice.” This works only if it is conceived as alive and frightened, just as it is made speaking in “it tells its tidings.”

On “He makes the things buried within it its burdens,” al-Rāghib says: “Its burdens are said to be its treasures, or the human bodies it contains at the resurrection. ‘They carry your burdens’ [16:7] means your heavy loads.”

T099.03 — The implied circumstance and the earth’s testimony [XVI:538]
On “‘What is the matter with it?’—that it has shaken”: it is said that this indicates an omission in the expression. The omitted clause is circumstantial, referring to the pronoun governed by the preposition, because it functions as an object: what has happened to it in this state? Compare His saying, “What is the matter with them, turning away from the reminder?” [74:49].

On “it bears witness against everyone concerning what he did upon its surface”: Imām Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal and al-Tirmidhī transmit from Abū Hurayra that God’s Messenger recited “On that day it will tell its tidings” and asked, “Do you know what its tidings are?” They said, “God and His Messenger know best.” He said: “Its tidings are that it bears witness against every male or female servant concerning what they did on its surface. It says, ‘On such-and-such a day, he did such-and-such.’ These are its tidings.”

T099.04 — Does “tell” take one object, two, or three? [XVI:539–540]
On “where are the two objects of ‘it tells’?”: it is said that both question and answer are open to examination. Ḥaddatha does not take two objects; it takes one. The omitted term explicitly supplied here is the direct object; the expressed term, “its tidings,” is an absolute object, a verbal-noun complement. These are not called “two objects” in the grammarians’ technical usage.

True, when the particular content of the verbal noun is mentioned in this construction, it is made accusative. Some grammarians then call its components a second and third object, as in “I told Zayd that ʿAmr was standing,” and call the verb transitive to three objects. It has been stated and established in its proper place that this is not so: the verb takes one object, while “Zayd standing” is accusative because it occupies the place of the verbal noun. But when the verbal noun is explicitly expressed, as in “I told him a telling and a report,” no one says that the verb takes two objects.

The proof is that, after explaining that “Zayd standing” is accusative in such a construction because it occupies the place of a verbal noun, rather than being a second and third object, Ibn al-Ḥājib says: “It remains to ask how something that is not an action in meaning—the supposed second and third objects—can occupy the position of a verbal noun.” He answers: “It was not a verbal noun insofar as it was ‘Zayd standing,’ but insofar as it was a particular report. The aspect that permits it to be predicated of ‘the report’ when you say, ‘Zayd told me that ʿAmr was departing,’ is the same aspect that permits it to occupy the place of a verbal noun.”

I say: one may answer that ḥaddatha and its sister verbs truly take one object, and that making them take three or two involves figurative extension or incorporation of another verb’s meaning. Al-Mufaṣṣal says: “Ḥaddathtu is treated like aʿlamtu, ‘I informed,’ because it agrees with it in meaning, and is therefore made transitive in the same way.” The author of al-Iqlīd says: “Anbaʾa, nabbaʾa, akhbara, and khabbara originally take one object: anbaʾtu Zaydan bi-kadhā, ‘I informed Zayd of such-and-such.’ The preposition is then omitted and one says anbaʾtuhu kadhā. In revelation, ‘Who informed you of this?’ [66:3] means bi-hādhā, ‘of this’; and ‘Inform My servants that I am the Forgiving, the Merciful’ [15:49]. When these verbs take three objects, this is only because they are treated like aʿlamtu.”

It is therefore clear that the author’s question rests on this analysis. His answer indicates it when he explicitly says: “As though it were said, ‘On that day it tells its tidings: that your Lord has inspired it,’ because one says both ‘I told him such-and-such’ and ‘I told him of such-and-such.’”

T099.05 — Why the hearers are omitted [XVI:540]
On “the aim is to mention its telling the tidings”: the purpose in the verse is the second object, not the first, because the sūrah is directed toward the terror of the Resurrection. That is: a tremendous day on which inanimate things speak.

T099.06 — Abstraction and the inspiration of the earth [XVI:540–541]
On “on that day, by telling that your Lord has inspired it, it tells its tidings”: apparently the bāʾ here resembles that in “If you meet so-and-so, you will meet in him a man of consummate goodness.” The meaning is: by telling that your Lord has inspired it, the earth tells its tidings in their utmost degree. This therefore belongs to abstraction, tajrīd. Hence he says: “On the understanding that its telling that your Lord has inspired it is itself the telling of its tidings.”

Under His words, “When We took from the prophets their covenant,” down to “and We took from them a solemn covenant” [33:7], he says: “By the second He intends precisely the first: by that covenant We took from them a solemn covenant.” The same applies to the example “You gave me every counsel by counselling me about religion”: from counsel concerning religion he abstracts perfect counsel. So too the poet says:
“She granted me all my wishes with a visit,
snatched in secret like the darting of a bird.
Had I been able, I would have bestowed upon the darkness
the blackness of my pupil, to lengthen our night.”

On “it is figurative”: that is, an extended metaphor, as previously under “Be, and it is.” The will to manifest the states within the earth is likened to an instruction conveyed to someone commanded, to display the swiftness with which what is wanted of him is obeyed.

T099.07 — Harshā and the priority of good [XVI:542]
On the line “Take the hollow of Harshā”: Harshā is a pass on the road to Mecca, near al-Juḥfa, with two routes. He addresses his two companions: travel through the hollow of this pass or along its back, since both sides are roads for the camels. This is a proverb for a course equally easy from either side.

It is said that the Bedouin imagined rearrangement here to be permissible. This is wrong: he overlooked the Qurʾān’s subtleties. There is no point in adducing the line here, and it would have been better to omit it, because primary concern attaches to good, while evil is incidental. Under “Whoever disbelieves, his unbelief is against him; and whoever acts righteously, they prepare for themselves—so that He may reward those who believe and do righteous deeds from His bounty; He does not love the unbelievers” [30:44–45], the Qāḍī says: “‘So that He may reward’ gives the purpose of ‘they prepare.’ Limiting the mention to the believer’s reward indicates that this is what is intended in itself.”

T099.08 — The restricted reading and objections to its premises [XVI:542–543]
On “because this follows ‘On that day people will come forth separately’”: he means that “Whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom’s weight of evil will see it” sets out the division of people into two groups, the felicitous and the wretched. That is, the verse is restricted in scope.

Al-Intiṣāf says: “His question rests on two principles. The first is that the unbeliever’s good deeds are nullified by unbelief. This needs examination. If it means that he receives no reward for them, it is correct. But if it means that they do not lessen punishment, that is not conceded. Reports have come concerning Ḥātim, that God lightens his punishment for his generosity, and concerning Abū Ṭālib and others. Such deeds therefore have an effect in lessening punishment.

“The second is that avoiding major sins necessarily expiates minor ones. This conflicts with the doctrine of the People of the Sunna. Minor sins are expiated by one of two things: repentance, or God’s willing forgiveness. This question therefore falls away according to us.”

The Imām says: “One may say that, although the unbeliever’s good deeds are nullified by his unbelief, the weighing of deeds is recognized according to your own position. To the extent of those good deeds, punishment for his unbelief is reduced. The same applies on the other side. This therefore does not impair the verse’s generality.”

T099.09 — Two possible scopes and the author’s doctrinal intention [XVI:543–544]
I say: the verse admits two meanings. One paired clause may intend the felicitous and the other the wretched, since the relative expression is repeated. Or each clause may intend generality: whoever among believers and unbelievers does an atom’s weight of good will see it, and whoever among believers and unbelievers does an atom’s weight of evil will see it.

The author’s words follow the first interpretation, as does what Muḥyī al-Sunna [al-Baghawī] and the Imām transmit from Muḥammad ibn Kaʿb al-Quraẓī: whoever does an atom’s weight of good while an unbeliever sees its reward in this world—in himself, his family, and his property—until he meets the hereafter with no good awaiting him there. Whoever does an atom’s weight of evil while a believer has it expiated in this world—in himself, his family, and his property—until he reaches the hereafter with no evil awaiting him there.

But the author’s intention is to include the person committing a major sin among the unbelievers and the wretched, because that person’s good deeds are nullified by it and he therefore sees only evil; just as the minor sins of one avoiding major sins are expiated through that avoidance and he sees only good. This is known from his question.

On the second interpretation comes what al-Wāḥidī transmits from Muqātil: whoever does an atom’s weight of good in this world will see it on the Day of Resurrection and rejoice in it. Likewise with evil: he sees it in his record, and it grieves him. Muḥyī al-Sunna and the Imām transmit from Ibn ʿAbbās: no believer or unbeliever does anything, good or evil, without God showing it to him. The believer’s evil deeds are forgiven and he is rewarded for his good deeds; the unbeliever’s good deeds are rejected and he is punished for his evil deeds.

T099.10 — Wording, meaning, and style favour generality [XVI:544–545]
This latter possibility is supported by the wording, the meaning, and the style.

As for the wording: “Whoever does,” as already stated, elaborates the preceding “People will come forth separately, to be shown their deeds,” so the two must agree. “Their deeds” is a plural in a possessive construction and conveys comprehensiveness and inclusion of all. “People will come forth” is qualified by “separately,” indicating that they take diverse routes to settle in their stations in Paradise and the Fire according to their differing deeds. Hence Paradise has ascending degrees and the Fire descending levels.

As for the meaning: the passage explains the exhaustive display of deeds and their recompense, as in His saying, “We shall set up the just scales for the Day of Resurrection, and no soul will be wronged in anything. Even if it is the weight of a mustard seed, We shall bring it; and We suffice as reckoners” [21:47].

As for the style: this is one of the comprehensive statements containing the benefits of religion, both its foundations and its branches. We have transmitted from al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Abū Hurayra, that God’s Messenger was asked about donkeys and said, “Nothing has been revealed to me concerning them except this comprehensive, unique verse,” and recited it. “About donkeys” means about alms on donkeys. Fādhdha, “unique,” means singular in its meaning: a man fadhdha from his companions when he separated from them.

Imām Aḥmad transmits from Ṣaʿṣaʿa ibn Muʿāwiya, al-Farazdaq’s uncle, that he came to the Prophet, who recited the verse. He said, “That is enough for me; I do not mind hearing nothing else.” In al-Ḥaqāʾiq: a sage was asked, “Admonish us,” and recited the verse. The questioner said, “Then the admonition has reached its utmost.”

T099.11 — The closing report and another transmitted measure [XVI:545]
On “whoever recites Sūrat ‘When the earth is shaken’ four times”: we have transmitted from al-Tirmidhī, from Ibn ʿAbbās, who said that God’s Messenger said: “Whoever recites ‘When the earth is shaken,’ it is reckoned for him as half the Qurʾān.”

End of the sūrah.

AL-QĀRIʿAH (101) — THE STRIKING CALAMITY
Complete first-pass translation of both authorial layers • C06 • 8 November 2026

SOURCE AND STATUS
Kashshāf: Dār al-Kitāb al-ʿArabī, 1407 AH, IV:789–791, OpenITI Shamela0023627-ara1; compared with the embedded base in Ṭībī and the Turkish edition, VI:1396–1398. Ṭībī: Futūḥ al-Ghayb, Dubai, 1434/2013, XVI:554–557, OpenITI Sham19Y0020891-ara1; printed XVI:556 selectively checked. Complete first draft of both authorial layers, excluding duplicated base text and modern editorial footnotes. Reports remain attributed as in the authors; independent authentication and final scholarly review are pending.

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K101.01 — Heading and verses [IV:789]
Meccan; eleven verses. [The DKA heading additionally places its revelation after Quraysh.]
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

The striking calamity! (1) What is the striking calamity? (2) And what will make you know what the striking calamity is? (3) On the day when people will be like scattered moths, (4) and the mountains like carded wool. (5) As for one whose scales are heavy, (6) he will be in a life of contentment. (7) But as for one whose scales are light, (8) his mother will be an abyss. (9) And what will make you know what it is? (10) A blazing fire. (11)

K101.02 — The day, the moths, and the mountains [IV:789–790]
The adverbial “on the day” is accusative through an omitted verb indicated by al-qāriʿa: “It strikes on the day when people will be like scattered moths.” He likens them to moths in their multitude, dispersal, weakness, abasement, and flight toward the summoner from every direction, as moths fly toward a fire. Jarīr says:
“Al-Farazdaq, so far as I have known, and his people
are like moths that swarm the fire of one warming himself.”

Among their proverbs are “weaker than a moth,” “more abased,” and “more ignorant.” They are called farāsh because they spread out and disperse. He likens the mountains to ʿihn, wool dyed in different colours, because the mountains are variously coloured; and to carded wool because their parts separate. Ibn Masʿūd read ka-l-ṣūf, “like wool.”

K101.03 — Heavy and light scales [IV:790]
Mawāzīn is the plural of mawzūn, something weighed: the deed that possesses weight and importance with God. Or it is the plural of mīzān, a scale; their heaviness is their outweighing.

An example is Abū Bakr’s report in his testament to ʿUmar, God be pleased with them both: “The scales of those whose scales are heavy on the Day of Resurrection are heavy only because they followed the truth and because of their weight in this world. A scale into which nothing but good deeds is placed rightly becomes heavy. The scales of those whose scales are light are light only because they followed falsehood and because of their lightness in this world. A scale into which nothing but evil deeds is placed rightly becomes light.”

K101.04 — “His mother will be an abyss” [IV:790]
“His mother will be an abyss” derives from their expression when invoking destruction upon a man: hawat ummuhu, “May his mother fall!” For when he hawā—falls and perishes—his mother falls through bereavement and grief. The poet says:
“May his mother fall! What a man the morning rouses to set out,
and what a man the night brings back when he returns!”

It is thus as though He said: “As for one whose scales are light, he has perished.”

It is also said that Hāwiya is one of the names of the Fire. It is as though it were the deep Fire, so called because its people fall within it to a great depth, as in the report, “He falls in it for seventy autumns.” That is, his abode is the Fire. An abode is metaphorically called a mother because the mother is the child’s refuge and place of recourse.

From Qatāda: “His mother will be an abyss” means that the crown of his head will fall into the bottom of Jahannam, because he is cast into it headfirst.

K101.05 — The pronoun and the pausal hāʾ [IV:790–791]
In hiya-h, the pronoun refers to the calamity indicated by “his mother will be an abyss” on the first interpretation; or it refers to Hāwiya. The final hāʾ is for pausing. When the reciter connects the reading, he omits it. It is also said that one ought not to connect here, lest connection cause its omission, since it is written in the codex. Retaining it in connected recitation has also been permitted.

K101.06 — Closing report [IV:791]
From God’s Messenger: “Whoever recites Sūrat al-Qāriʿah, God will make his scale heavy through it on the Day of Resurrection.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T101.01 — Jarīr’s comparison [XVI:554–555]
Meccan; ten verses. In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful. [The source’s count is retained; the verse text above uses the eleven-verse numbering.]

On the line beginning “Al-Farazdaq”: mā ʿalimtu means “what I have known,” and is parenthetical. He satirizes al-Farazdaq and his people: they are weak, abased, and ignorant, like moths which ghashīna—that is, came in the covering darkness of night—to the fire at which the poet, Jarīr, warms himself. It is also said that ghashīna means “plunged into.” Another explanation takes mā in mā ʿalimtu as introducing a verbal noun, with a period of time understood: throughout the duration of my knowledge of them, al-Farazdaq and his people have been weak.

T101.02 — The testament and the elegy [XVI:555–556]
On “an example is Abū Bakr’s report”: the author of Jāmiʿ al-Uṣūl transmits the report from Razīn al-ʿAbdarī. We have given it in full under al-Aʿrāf.

On the line “May his mother fall!”: its author is Kaʿb ibn Saʿd al-Ghanawī, lamenting his brother. Yabʿathu means rouses from sleep; al-ghādī is one who sets out in the morning and is circumstantial. Hawat ummuhu is an imprecation whose occurrence is not intended; rather, it expresses wonder and praise. That is: what a man the morning sends forth from him when he sets out, and what a man the night returns from him when he comes back! “From him” is omitted in both places because the discourse indicates it, just as it is omitted in “The clarified butter: two minas for a dirham.” This involves abstraction, tajrīd: the morning sends forth from him a raider, and the night a man returning with booty.

T101.03 — Seventy autumns [XVI:556]
On “seventy autumns”: according to some, autumn stands for the year because fruits and crops develop in this season. The final portion of a period is used to designate the whole.

T101.04 — Destruction, life, and the parallel expressions [XVI:556]
On “on the first interpretation”: that is, if “his mother will be an abyss” is explained as an imprecation, from their saying “May his mother fall!” The pronoun is made to refer to calamity because, when a person falls and perishes and his mother becomes bereaved and disgraced, a calamity has befallen him. On the second interpretation, “his mother” means his abode, and Hāwiya is one of the names of the Fire.

The first interpretation is the more evident, because “his mother will be an abyss” stands opposite “he will be in a life of contentment.” Destruction corresponds more aptly to ʿaysh, since this is the life peculiar to animate creatures. Just as the second paired clause is intensified by what follows it, the first is intensified by figurative predication.

Al-Rāghib says: “ʿAysh is life peculiar to animate creatures, and is narrower than ḥayāt, ‘life,’ because ḥayāt is said of animate creatures, the Creator Most High, and the angel. From it is derived maʿīsha, that by which one lives. God Most High says, ‘We have apportioned among them their livelihood in the life of this world’ [43:32]. Of the people of Paradise He says, ‘He will be in a life of contentment.’ And the Prophet says in the report, ‘There is no life but the life of the hereafter.’”

T101.05 — The stopping point [XVI:557]
On “the hāʾ is for pausing; when the reciter connects, he omits it”: al-Murshid says that mā hiya-h is a sufficient stopping point. Abū Ḥātim calls it a good stopping point. He then explains it with “a blazing fire.” God knows best.

End of the sūrah.

K101-U1: The poetic imprecation is rendered literally in the base text, with its actual force of astonished praise explained in Ṭībī’s layer. The embedded base has yathūb, “returns,” where DKA has yaʾūb; the sense is the same.
T101-U2: Printed XVI:556 confirms khuzyā, “disgraced,” against the electronic transcription’s defective خِزْيا form. The season explanation and the author’s preference for the first interpretation are retained without editorial reconciliation.

SŪRAT AL-TAKĀTHUR (102): RIVALRY IN INCREASE
Complete first-draft translation of al-Zamakhsharī and al-Ṭībī
Checkpoint C05 • 27 October 2026

Coverage: all eight verses, the complete Kashshāf chapter, and all distinct authorial commentary in Futūḥ al-Ghayb XVI, 558–564. The repeated Kashshāf base text in Ṭībī's edition is represented in its own layer. Modern editorial footnotes are excluded from the authorial translation. Sources: K-DKA IV, 791–793; T-Dubai XVI, 558–564, through working transcriptions. This is a complete first draft; critical review is not complete. Transmitted reports retain their source's attributions without a claim of independent authentication.

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K102.01 — Heading and verses
Meccan; eight verses. [K-DKA adds: revealed after al-Kawthar.]
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.
“Rivalry in increase has distracted you, until you visited the graves. No indeed! You will come to know. Again, no indeed! You will come to know. No indeed! If only you knew with knowledge of certainty! You will surely see the blazing Fire. Then you will surely see it with the very certainty of sight. Then that day you will surely be questioned about the pleasures you enjoyed.”

K102.02 — Rivalry and the graves
Alhāhu ʿan kadhā and aqhāhu mean that something occupied him and distracted him from something else. Takāthur is competing in abundance and boasting of it: one group says, “We are more numerous,” and the other says, “We are more numerous.” It is reported that Banū ʿAbd Manāf and Banū Sahm boasted to one another about which was greater in number. Banū ʿAbd Manāf outnumbered them. Banū Sahm said: “Aggression destroyed us in the age of ignorance; count against us both the living and the dead.” Banū Sahm then outnumbered them.
The meaning is: you competed over the number of the living, and once you exhausted their number, you turned to the graves and competed over the dead. Their proceeding to mention the dead is expressed as visiting the graves, in mockery of them. It is also said that they actually visited the graves, saying, “This is so-and-so's grave; this is so-and-so's grave,” while boasting to one another.
The meaning is that this distracted you—something that does not concern you and yields you no benefit in this world or the next—from what does concern you: the affairs of religion, which are more important and more deserving of attention than anything else.
Or He means: rivalry in wealth and children distracted you until you died and were buried. You spent your lives seeking this world, racing toward it and throwing yourselves upon it, until death came while you cared for nothing else. It distracted you from what had a greater claim upon you: striving for your final outcome and working for your hereafter. Visiting the graves is an expression for death. A poet said:
“This year no intimate who has tasted a shared attachment
will secure ten [nights of exclusive companionship], unless he visits the grave.”
Another said:
“Abū Mālik visited the graves,
and became the meanest of their visitors.”
Ibn ʿAbbās read a-alhākum, as a question whose purpose is to elicit acknowledgment.

K102.03 — Repeated warning
Kallā, “no indeed,” rebukes and calls attention to the fact that someone concerned for his own welfare ought not to make this world his entire concern while neglecting his religion. “You will come to know” warns them so that they may fear and awaken from heedlessness. Repetition emphasizes the rebuke and warning directed at them. Thumma, “again/then,” indicates that the second warning is more emphatic and severe than the first, as when you tell someone you are advising, “I tell you, and then I tell you again: do not do it.”
The meaning is: you will know the error of your present course when you see before you the terror of meeting God. This admonition is counsel and mercy for you. He then repeats the admonition and says, “If only you knew,” with the consequence left unstated: if you knew what lies before you with knowledge of something certain—as you know those matters of which you are assured and to whose knowledge you have devoted your attention—you would do something beyond description or comprehension. But you are astray and ignorant.

K102.04 — Seeing the Fire; readings; certainty
He then says, “You will surely see the blazing Fire,” explaining what He had warned them against and threatened them with. The magnifying effect of clarifying something after leaving it unspecified has already been discussed. This is the response to an omitted oath. The oath emphasizes the threat and establishes that what they are threatened with admits no doubt. He repeats it, coordinating it with thumma, to intensify the threat and increase the terror.
La-tarʾunna is also read, with a hamza [in place of the wāw]; it is considered objectionable. If you ask why, when changing a wāw bearing ḍamma into hamza is a regular analogy, I answer: that applies to a wāw whose ḍamma is permanent. Here it is incidental, introduced to avoid the meeting of two vowelless letters. La-turawunna and la-turawunnahā are also read as passives: “you will surely be made to see,” and “you will surely be made to see it.”
“With the very certainty of sight” (ʿayna al-yaqīn): a seeing that is certainty itself and its pure essence. Seeing may mean knowledge and visual perception.

K102.05 — The enjoyment about which people are questioned
“About the pleasures you enjoyed”: about the amusement and comfortable enjoyment whose pleasures distracted you from religion and its obligations.
If you ask what enjoyment a person is questioned and reproached about, since everyone has some enjoyment, I answer: it is the enjoyment of someone who devotes all his concern to exhausting the pleasures of life, living only to eat fine food, wear soft clothes, and pass his time in amusement and entertainment. He pays no heed to knowledge and action and does not undertake their difficulties.
But someone who enjoys God's blessings and provisions—which He created only for His servants—and draws strength from them to study knowledge and perform good works, while rising to the duty of gratitude, is far removed from this. God's Messenger indicated this in the report that he and his Companions ate dates and drank water afterward, and he said: “Praise belongs to God, who fed us, gave us drink, and made us Muslims.”
God's Messenger is reported to have said: “Whoever recites ‘Rivalry in increase has distracted you,’ God will not call him to account for the enjoyment He granted him in the abode of this world, and he will be given a reward as though he had recited a thousand verses.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T102.01 — Outnumbering; the irony of visiting graves (XVI, 558–559)
On “Banū Sahm outnumbered them”: they defeated them through greater numbers, from the expression kāthartuhu fa-kathartuhu, “I competed with him in numbers and outnumbered him.” Takāthur is striving to increase one's wealth and numbers.
On “you turned to the graves and competed over the dead”: according to this, “the graves” is an indirect expression for moving from mentioning the living to mentioning the dead in mutual boasting. It is mockery because visiting graves was prescribed to recall death, reject love of this world, and abandon ostentation and boasting. These people reversed this: they made visiting graves a cause of greater hardness of heart, immersion in worldly love, and boasting of greater numbers.
We have transmitted from Muslim, Abū Dāwūd, and al-Nasāʾī, from Burayda, that God's Messenger said: “I forbade you to visit graves; now visit them.” Abū Dāwūd's version reads: “Visit them, for they remind you of the hereafter.”
On “or He means: rivalry in wealth and children distracted you until you died”: the three interpretations thus reduce to ‘visiting’ meaning either the transition from mentioning one group to mentioning another, actual visiting, or death. “Spending” is a circumstantial expression attached to “distracted you”; “from what had a greater claim upon you” is also governed by “distracted you.”

T102.02 — The verse about a shared attachment (559–560)
On “This year no intimate…”: al-Fāʾiq says: “A woman's ḍamd is her joining together and taking two lovers.” Abū Dhuʾayb said:
“You wish to join me and Khālid as your lovers—
can two swords, woe to you, be joined in one sheath?”
The author of the quoted verse is Miqdād ibn Ḥassān al-Zubayrī. It is preceded by:
“I have found a shared attachment a grievous thing.”
In the age of ignorance a woman would take a lover besides her husband; that is ḍamd.
On “ten” (ʿashran): ten nights. A reading with kasra on the ʿayn, ʿishran, is also transmitted, meaning companionship. Muʿāshara means association, as does taʿāshur; the corresponding noun is ʿishra. Khalīl means husband. The meaning is that a husband will not secure exclusive companionship with a woman for ten nights unless he dies. “Who has tasted a shared attachment” describes the intimate. [The compressed verse and its explanation remain difficult; the alternatives are retained rather than conflated.]

T102.03 — Kallā, stopping points, and the two senses (560–561)
On “kallā rebukes and calls attention”: it rejects the preceding discourse and calls attention to what the following discourse indicates. He thus takes both its meanings into consideration. The Imām [Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī] says: “Kallā is connected with what precedes by rejection and denial: the matter is not as these people imagine, that true happiness lies in abundant numbers, wealth, and children. It is connected with what follows in the sense, ‘Truly, you will come to know’—but at a time when the transgressor becomes repentant, the unbeliever becomes a Muslim, and the covetous person becomes an ascetic.” The author's words intimate both meanings.
Al-Kawāshī says: “The stop at ‘the graves’ is complete if kallā calls attention. If it rebukes, the stop is at kallā.”
If you ask whether the author's position entails using a shared expression simultaneously in both its different meanings, I answer: it does not. The intention is that, if one begins with kallā, a new statement begins there. An implied question is supplied: what is the recompense of these heedless people, and what is said of them? The answer is: truly, they will know the outcome of their state when they see the blazing Fire. The discourse thus conveys rebuke by its meaning.
If one stops at kallā, the implied question occurs after it: what will be done to these rejected people who have been rebuked? The answer is: they will know what will be done to them when they see the blazing Fire. The discourse thus entails calling attention by its meaning. The author of al-Murshid says: “‘Until you visited the graves’ is a complete stop. Begin with kallā in the sense of threat and warning.”
On “if you knew what lies before you”: it is said that ‘knowledge’ here means knowing the thing itself, not knowing it by its description.

T102.04 — The hamza reading and the canonical readers (562)
On “that applies to a wāw whose ḍamma is permanent”: al-Zajjāj says: “The reading is la-tarawunna, with ḍamma on the wāw and without hamza. The wāw receives ḍamma because it and the nūn are both vowelless. Some have pronounced it with hamza, but the grammarians dislike this because its ḍamma is not permanent: it was given a vowel to avoid two vowelless letters meeting. They change a wāw with permanent ḍamma into hamza, as in adʾur, the plural of dār, ‘house’; adwur is also permissible.”
On “la-turawunna is also read”: Ibn ʿĀmir and al-Kisāʾī read with ḍamma on the tāʾ; the others with fatḥa. Among the seven readers there is no disagreement that la-tarawunnahā has fatḥa on the tāʾ.

T102.05 — Knowledge, sight, and the degrees of certainty (562–563)
On “‘the very certainty of sight’: a seeing that is certainty itself”: it is said that ʿayna al-yaqīn is accusative as a verbal-noun expression. ʿAyn here means the thing itself, as when you say, “Zayd himself came”—nafsuhu or ʿaynuhu. The correct position is that seeing here means visual perception rather than knowledge.
I say: this is what he intended by “seeing may mean knowledge and visual perception,” treating the coordination as explanatory. The Qāḍī says: “‘ʿAyn al-yaqīn’ means a seeing that is certainty itself, for knowledge through direct witnessing is the highest degree of certainty.”
Our shaykh, Shaykh al-Islām—may his inner being be sanctified—says in al-ʿAwārif: “ʿIlm al-yaqīn, knowledge of certainty, is reached through reflection and inference. ʿAyn al-yaqīn, the eye of certainty, is reached through unveilings and gifts. Ḥaqq al-yaqīn, the reality of certainty, comes through truly separating oneself from the stain of clay when the herald of union arrives.” Al-Junayd says: “The reality of certainty is that the servant becomes fully established in it: he witnesses unseen things just as he witnesses visible things, with direct vision.”

T102.06 — Who is questioned about enjoyment? (563–564)
On “the enjoyment of someone who devotes all his concern to exhausting pleasures”: the Qāḍī says: “The address, ‘Then that day you will surely be questioned about the pleasures you enjoyed,’ concerns specifically anyone whose worldly life distracted him from his religion, not the believers, because of the contextual indication and the many texts such as ‘Say: Who has forbidden God's adornment, which He has brought forth for His servants, and the good things of provision?’ (7:32), and ‘Eat of the good things’ (23:51). It is also said to concern unbelievers specifically; or to be general, since everyone is questioned about his gratitude.”
I say: the latter is supported by what we have transmitted from Muslim, al-Tirmidhī, and Ibn Mājah, from Abū Hurayra. God's Messenger went out and encountered Abū Bakr and ʿUmar. He asked: “What brought you out of your homes?” They said: “Hunger.” He said: “By Him in whose hand is my soul, the same thing brought me out.” They came to the house of a man of the Anṣār, who brought them a cluster containing unripe, dried, and fresh dates, and slaughtered an animal for them. They ate from the sheep and the date-cluster and drank. When they had eaten and drunk their fill, God's Messenger said to them: “By Him in whose hand is my soul, you will surely be questioned about this enjoyment on the Day of Resurrection.” The report is abbreviated here.
Al-Wāḥidī transmits from Muqātil: “He means the unbelievers of Mecca. In this world they lived amid goodness and blessings. On the Day of Resurrection they will be questioned about gratitude for what they enjoyed. They did not thank the Lord of blessings, since they worshipped others and associated partners with Him; then they will be punished. This is al-Ḥasan's position.”
I say: this is supported by the fact that the address from the beginning of the sūrah is to those competing and boasting in abundance, who, as explained above, are unbelievers. Since preoccupation with worldly enjoyment is a characteristic of the heedless, and a believer must avoid base qualities, God's Messenger spoke sternly when he said, “You will be questioned about this enjoyment on the Day of Resurrection.” This does not mean that he was interpreting the verse by what he said.
Complete.

AL-ʿAṢR (103) — TIME
Complete first-pass translation of both authorial layers • C05 • 27 October 2026

SOURCE AND STATUS
Kashshāf: Dār al-Kitāb al-ʿArabī, 1407 AH, IV:793–794, OpenITI Shamela0023627-ara1; checked against the embedded base text in Ṭībī. Ṭībī: Futūḥ al-Ghayb, Dubai, 1434/2013, XVI:565–567, OpenITI Sham19Y0020891-ara1. Both layers are translated through their endings. Modern editorial footnotes are excluded. Reports retain their authors’ attribution without independent authentication. Review details and uncertainties are recorded in the C05 ledger; this is not a final critical edition.

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K103.01 — Heading and verses [IV:793]
Meccan; three verses. [The DKA edition’s heading additionally places its revelation after al-Sharḥ.]
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

By time! (1) Surely the human being is in loss, (2) except those who believe, do righteous deeds, counsel one another to the truth, and counsel one another to patience. (3)

K103.02 — The oath and the exception [IV:793–794]
He swears by the afternoon prayer because of its excellence. Evidence is His saying, “and the middle prayer—the afternoon prayer,” in Ḥafṣa’s codex [cf. 2:238], and the Prophet’s saying, “Whoever misses the afternoon prayer is as though he had been deprived of his family and property.” Moreover, the obligation to perform it is more demanding because people rush into their trading and earning toward the end of the day and are occupied with their livelihoods.

Or He swears by the late afternoon, just as He swears by the morning brightness, because both contain signs of divine power. Or He swears by time because of the many kinds of wonders in its passage.

“The human being” denotes the species. Khusr means khusrān, loss, just as kufr is used for kufrān. The meaning is that people lose in their trading, except the righteous alone: they purchase the hereafter with this world, and so profit and attain felicity. Everyone else trades in the opposite way and falls into loss and wretchedness.

“And counsel one another to the truth”: to the established reality that cannot rightfully be denied. It encompasses all good: affirming God’s oneness and obeying Him, following His books and messengers, renouncing attachment to this world, and desiring the hereafter.

“And counsel one another to patience”: in refraining from acts of disobedience, performing acts of obedience, and bearing the trials with which God tests His servants.

K103.03 — Closing report [IV:794]
From God’s Messenger: “Whoever recites the sūrah ‘By time,’ God forgives him, and he is among those who counsel one another to the truth and counsel one another to patience.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T103.01 — “As though deprived of his family and property” [XVI:565]
Meccan; three verses. In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

On “as though he had been deprived of his family and property,” al-Nihāya says: “Wutira means diminished. You say watartuhu when you diminish someone, as though making him solitary after he had been many. It is also said to derive from watr, an injury: the person who misses the afternoon prayer is likened to someone whose intimate companion has been killed or whose family and property have been taken from him.

“The report is transmitted with ‘family’ both accusative and nominative. Those who put it in the accusative make it the second object of wutira, and understand within the verb an unexpressed passive subject referring to the person who missed the prayer. Those who put it in the nominative posit no such pronoun, but make ‘family’ the passive subject, since they are the ones struck by the loss and taken away. Whoever attributes the deprivation to the man puts both nouns in the accusative; whoever attributes it to family and property puts them in the nominative.”

T103.02 — Rushing, time, and mutual counsel [XVI:566]
On “because they rush” (li-tahāfut): it means falling down piece by piece. Tahāfut al-farāsh fī l-nār means the moths’ falling into the fire.

On “or He swears by time,” al-Zajjāj says: “Al-ʿaṣr means time; al-ʿaṣr also means day, and al-ʿaṣr means night.” Ḥumayd ibn Thawr says:
“The two times—day and night—do not delay,
when they seek something, in overtaking what they aim at.”

On “‘and counsel one another to the truth’: to the established reality,” and what follows, al-Rāghib says: “Waṣiyya is giving another person advance instructions about something to be done, accompanied by admonition and sincere advice. It derives from their expression arḍ wāṣiya: land whose vegetation is continuous. One says awṣāhu and waṣṣāhu; a people tawāṣaw when they counsel one another.” One says qaddamtu ilayhi bi-kadhā when one commands someone before the time comes for the action to be needed.

T103.03 — The four requirements of deliverance [XVI:567]
The Imām [Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī] says: “The verse contains a severe threat, for it judges all people to be in loss except whoever possesses faith, righteous action, mutual counsel to the truth, and mutual counsel to patience. This indicates that deliverance depends on these things together. Just as the morally responsible person must acquire what concerns his own self, he must also, regarding others, call to religion, give sincere advice, command right and forbid wrong, and love for another what he loves for himself. Mutual counsel is then repeated so that the first includes calling to God and the second remaining steadfast in it.”

End of the sūrah.

TEXTUAL NOTE
T103-U1: XVI:566 reads أرض واصية, “land whose vegetation is continuous”; the electronic text’s أوض is corrected from the scan. This is a transcription correction, not a translation of the modern editor’s apparatus.

AL-HUMAZAH (104) — THE BACKBITER
Complete first-pass translation of both authorial layers • C06 • 8 November 2026

SOURCE AND STATUS
Kashshāf: Dār al-Kitāb al-ʿArabī, 1407 AH, IV:794–796, OpenITI Shamela0023627-ara1; compared with the embedded base in Ṭībī and the Turkish edition, VI:1408–1412. Ṭībī: Futūḥ al-Ghayb, Dubai, 1434/2013, XVI:568–576, OpenITI Sham19Y0020891-ara1; printed XVI:572, 574, and 576 selectively checked. Both authorial layers are translated through their endings. Modern editorial footnotes are excluded except where a source-reading issue is explicitly recorded in the translator’s notes. Repeated base text is not duplicated as commentary. Reports remain attributed; final scholarly review is pending.

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K104.01 — Heading and verses [IV:794]
Meccan; nine verses. [The DKA heading additionally places its revelation after al-Qiyāmah.]
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

Woe to every backbiter and slanderer, (1) who amasses wealth and counts it over, (2) thinking that his wealth has made him immortal. (3) No indeed! He will surely be cast into the Crusher. (4) And what will make you know what the Crusher is? (5) God’s kindled fire, (6) which rises over the hearts. (7) It is closed over them, (8) amid extended pillars. (9)

K104.02 — Backbiting, ridicule, and a general warning [IV:794–795]
Hamz means breaking, like hazm; lamz means piercing. One says lamazahu and lahazahu, “he stabbed him.” What is intended is breaking people’s reputations, diminishing them, backbiting them, and attacking them. The pattern fuʿala indicates that this is a habit to which the person has become addicted. Comparable forms are luʿana, a habitual curser, and ḍuḥaka, a habitual laugher. The poet says:
“And when I am absent, you are the backbiter, the slanderer.”

The reading waylun li-l-humazati l-lumaza, “woe to the backbiter, the slanderer,” is also transmitted. Another reading has humza lumza with the mīm quiescent: this is the laughing-stock who produces outlandish acts and comical displays, and is laughed at and abused.

It is said to have been revealed concerning al-Akhnas ibn Sharīq, whose habit was backbiting and defamation; or concerning Umayya ibn Khalaf; or al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra and his backbiting and disparaging God’s Messenger. The occasion may be particular while the threat is general, so as to include everyone who undertakes this ugly conduct, and to operate as an indirect allusion to the person concerning whom it was revealed. That is more restraining and more wounding to him.

K104.03 — Accumulation and counting [IV:795]
“Who” is a substitute for “every,” or accusative in a construction of blame. Jammaʿa is also read with the consonant doubled, matching ʿaddadahu, “counts it over.” Another explanation takes ʿaddadahu to mean that he makes it a reserve against the events of time.

Wa-ʿadadahu is also read: he gathers the wealth, keeps track of its amount, and counts it; or he gathers his wealth and the people who support him. This derives from saying that someone possesses ʿadad and ʿudad when he has a plentiful number of helpers and the resources to sustain them. It is also said that wa-ʿadadahu means wa-ʿaddahu, “and counted it,” with the assimilation undone, like ḍaninū for ḍannū, “they were miserly.”

K104.04 — The illusion of immortality [IV:795–796]
Akhladahu and khalladahu have the same meaning. Wealth has lengthened his hopes and filled him with distant aspirations, until, through excessive heedlessness and long hopes, he thinks that wealth has left him immortal in this world, never to die. Or, in erecting buildings secured with stone and brick, planting trees, and cultivating the earth, he acts as someone who thinks his wealth has kept him alive.

Or it is an indirect allusion to righteous action: that is what makes its possessor abide eternally in bliss, whereas wealth has made no one abide there eternally.

It is related that al-Akhnas possessed four thousand dinars; another account says ten thousand. From al-Ḥasan: he visited a wealthy man who was ill. The man said, “What do you say of thousands which I have neither used to ransom myself from a base person nor bestowed upon an honourable one?” He asked, “But what did you keep them for?” The man said, “For the hostility of the times, the ruler’s harshness, the calamities of life, and fear of poverty.” He replied, “Then you will leave them to someone who will not praise you, and come before One who will not excuse you.”

K104.05 — The Crusher and the hearts [IV:796]
“No indeed!” restrains him from his supposition. La-yunbadhānni is also read: he and his wealth will surely be cast away. Another reading has la-yunbadhunna with ḍamma on the dhāl: he and his supporters will be cast away. La-yanbidhannahu, “he/it will surely cast him,” is also read.

“Into the Crusher”: into the fire whose nature is to crush everything thrown into it. Of a voracious man one says, “He is a crusher.” Al-ḥāṭima, “the crushing one,” is also read. The meaning is that it enters their interiors until it reaches their breasts and rises over their afʾida, the centres of their hearts. Nothing in the human body is more delicate than the fuʾād or more acutely pained by the slightest hurt touching it. How, then, when Jahannam’s fire rises over it and takes possession of it?

The hearts may also be singled out because they are the seats of unbelief, corrupt beliefs, and evil intentions. The fire’s rising over them means that it overtops, overcomes, and encompasses them. Or, figuratively, it examines the mines from which what necessitates it is drawn.

K104.06 — Closed doors and extended pillars [IV:796]
“Closed”: shut over them. The poet says:
“My she-camel longs for the mountains of Mecca,
while before her the gates of Ṣanʿāʾ are closed.”

Fī ʿumud is read with two ḍammas; ʿumd with the mīm quiescent; and ʿamad with two fatḥas. The meaning is that their despair of escape and certainty of everlasting imprisonment are reinforced: the doors are closed upon them and pillars stretched across the doors, fastening upon fastening.

Or the meaning is that it is closed over them while they are secured in extended pillars, like the stocks in which thieves are confined. God, protect us from the Fire, O best of those in whom refuge is sought!

K104.07 — Closing report [IV:796]
From God’s Messenger: “Whoever recites Sūrat al-Humazah, God gives him ten good deeds for every person who mocked Muḥammad and his companions.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T104.01 — Breaking reputations and the correspondence of punishment [XVI:568–569]
Meccan; nine verses. In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

On “hamz means breaking”: according to some, hamz is like squeezing with the hand. One says, “I squeezed the thing in my palm”; from this comes hamz in letters. Hamz of a person is backbiting him. One says hāmiz, hammāz, and humaza of a man.

On “diminishing them,” al-Jawharī says: “Ghaḍḍa minhu yaghuḍḍu, with ḍamma in the imperfect, means that he lowered and diminished his standing.” Others relate to it lowering the gaze and voice, and restraining blame.

On “the pattern fuʿala indicates that this is a habit,” al-Intiṣāf says: “How fine is the correspondence of humaza and lumaza with ḥuṭama! Having marked him with this description and with a form indicating rootedness and firmness, He threatens him with this corresponding attribute, so that deed and recompense balance one another.”

I say: there is another subtle correspondence. Hamz includes breaking reputations; ḥaṭm includes breaking ribs. Nabdh, casting away, conveys contempt and belittlement, since the man imagined himself to belong among the honoured. Under His saying, ‘We seized him and his troops and cast them into the sea’ [28:40], he says: “To express contempt for them and belittle their number, He likens them to little pebbles which someone takes in his palm and throws into the sea.” Al-Wāḥidī transmits from Muqātil: “It crushes bones and consumes flesh until it assails the hearts.”

T104.02 — The poetic example and strange utterances [XVI:569–570]
On “And when I am absent, you are the backbiter, the slanderer”: it is said that the opening is:
“You proffer affection for me, falsely, when you meet me.”

Al-Zajjāj cites these words of Ziyād al-Aʿjam:
“When I meet you, you bare your teeth in a smile despite your resentment;
when I am absent, you are the backbiter, the slanderer.”

Ibn al-Sikkīt says: “Kashr means smiling. Kashara al-rajulu, iftarra, and ibtasama all mean that his teeth become visible.”

On “outlandish acts,” al-Asās says: “Among figurative uses is ‘So-and-so is devoted to awābid of speech,’ meaning its strange expressions, and to awābid of poetry, those poems whose excellence is unmatched.”

T104.03 — General language and indirect rebuke [XVI:570–571]
On “the occasion may be particular while the threat is general”: the Imām transmits from al-Farrāʾ: “An expression’s being general does not contradict a particular person’s being intended. For instance, someone says to you, ‘I have never visited you,’ and you say, ‘Everyone who has not visited me, I will not visit.’ In legal theory this is called restricting a general expression by a contextual indication of customary usage.”

On “to operate as an indirect allusion to the person concerning whom it was revealed”: if that person is al-Akhnas, Umayya, or al-Walīd, and the wording is made general as an indirect rebuke, this is more restraining and wounding. His name has not been explicitly stated so that he would put on the leopard’s hide against the person confronting him. Instead it prompts him to reflect on his own state: has he entered this general category before everyone else by backbiting the best of creation and diminishing his due? Al-Asās says: “Nakaytu fī l-ʿaduwwi nikāyatan means I inflicted many wounds upon the enemy. One says, ‘So-and-so inflicts little hurt but complains at length.’”

T104.04 — The relative clause and the unassimilated form [XVI:571–572]
On “or accusative in a construction of blame”: it is said that it may be an adjective of kull, “every,” because kull is definite. Thus under “Every soul—with it a driver and a witness” [50:21], he says that “with it a driver” is circumstantial and accusative in position, referring to kull, which becomes definite through being annexed to something treated as definite.

On ḍaninū: that is, in the poet’s words:
“Gently, reproacher! Have you learned from my character
that I am generous to people even when they are miserly?”

His saying “It is also said that wa-ʿadadahu means wa-ʿaddahu” is coordinated with “wa-ʿadadahu: he gathers the wealth and keeps track of its amount.” On this latter analysis, it is the object of an omitted verb, as in:
“I fed her straw and cold water.”

T104.05 — Two main readings, with three resulting explanations [XVI:572–573]
On “or he acts”: this is coordinated with “he thinks.” “Or it is an indirect allusion” is coordinated, in sense, with the whole explanation beginning “wealth has lengthened his hopes.” Hence the expression changes. There are thus two principal interpretations which yield three explanations.

The analysis is this: “he thinks” is circumstantial, referring to the pronoun in “amasses.” The supposition concerns either immortality in this world or everlasting bliss, as with the speaker who said, “If I am returned to my Lord, I shall certainly find a better place of return than this” [18:36], and al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil, who said, “I shall certainly be given wealth and children” [19:77]. On the first interpretation, the supposition is either literal—as intended by “he thinks that wealth has left him immortal in this world”—or figurative, as intended by “or, in erecting buildings, he acts,” just as God says, “Do you build on every elevation a monument, amusing yourselves, and take strong structures as though you would live for ever?” [26:128–129]. On the second interpretation, the verse contains an indirect allusion.

It is appropriate on the first interpretation to make “who” a substitute for “every.” The meaning is: woe to the man who amassed wealth and counted it, then entertained long hopes and fell into delusion, thinking his wealth had left him immortal in this world. On the second interpretation, it should be accusative for blame. The meaning is: woe to the impious slanderer—I mean the one whom accumulating wealth and relying on men emboldened to slander and impiety, while he also thinks his wealth will make him live eternally in bliss. “No indeed! He will surely be cast into the Crusher.” What makes its possessor abide in the lasting bliss of Paradise is righteous action, as in His saying, “The day when neither wealth nor sons avail, except one who comes to God with a sound heart” [26:88–89]. The two interpretations thus unfold what was folded together in “who is a substitute for every, or accusative in a construction of blame.” God knows best.

T104.06 — Ransoming one’s honour and the rebuke [XVI:573–574]
On “I have not used them to ransom myself from a base person”: I did not make my wealth a ransom for my honour, to escape his harm. He cites:
“I protect my honour with my wealth and do not stain it;
may God not bless wealth once honour is lost!”

On “the hostility of the times,” al-Asās says: “Nabā ʿannī fulān means he parted from me. Between us is nabwa, estrangement. He complains of the times’ estrangement and harshness.”

On “‘No indeed!’ restrains him from his supposition,” the Imām says: “It is not as he imagined, that wealth and numbers make one immortal; rather, knowledge and righteousness do. ʿAlī, God be pleased with him, said: ‘The hoarders of wealth have died while still alive, but the learned remain as long as time remains.’ Or kallā means ‘truly’: he will surely be cast away. The lām answers an oath, indicating the presence of an oath in kallā. Casting away conveys humiliation and contempt, since he imagined himself among the honoured.”

T104.07 — The fuʾād and the fire’s discernment [XVI:574–575]
On “nothing in the human body is more delicate than the fuʾād,” al-Rāghib says: “Fuʾād is like qalb, ‘heart,’ but it is called fuʾād when the sense of tafaʾʾud, burning, is considered in it. Faʾadtu al-laḥma means I roasted the meat; laḥm faʾīd is roasted meat. Singling out the hearts in ‘which rises over the hearts’ calls attention to the extreme effect upon them.”

On “or, figuratively, it examines the mines from which what necessitates it is drawn”: choosing the word “mines” hints at the reverse of the Prophet’s saying, “People are mines, like mines of gold and silver.” Since these people’s hearts are the settled seats of the pollution and filth of corrupt beliefs that necessitate the Fire, and the commencement of each person’s burning is fixed according to what he deserves, it is said that the Fire figuratively examines the mines of what necessitates it.

In al-Taysīr, Abū Saʿīd says: “It knows how much punishment each of them deserves because of the unbelief and corrupt beliefs in his heart.” This comes from saying iṭṭalaʿa fulānun ʿalā amrinā: he became acquainted with our affair and knew it. God has made the fire such that it burns each person according to his deserts, neither adding nor subtracting, as though it had ascertained the amount he deserves. He says: “Since it may be described as enraged and as calling whoever turned his back and withdrew, it may also be described in this way.”

T104.08 — Closure and the readings of “pillars” [XVI:575]
On “muʾṣada: closed,” al-Rāghib says: “A waṣīda is a chamber made in a mountain for storing property. One says awṣadtu al-bāba and āṣadtuhu: I closed the door and secured it. God Most High says, ‘Over them is a closed fire’ [90:20]; it is also read with hamza.”

On “fī ʿumud is read”: Abū Bakr, Ḥamza, and al-Kisāʾī read it with two ḍammas; the others with two fatḥas.

On “pillars are stretched across the doors”: it is said that, on this interpretation, fī ʿumud is circumstantial, referring to the pronoun in muʾṣada—namely the one referring to the doors. On the interpretation “secured in pillars,” it is circumstantial, referring to the pronoun in ʿalayhim, “over them.”

T104.09 — Stocks, the particular occasion, and the general scope [XVI:576]
On “like the stocks,” al-Jawharī says: “The miqṭara is the falaq: a piece of wood with holes into which prisoners’ legs are inserted.”

I say: the first interpretation suits the report that the verse was revealed concerning al-Akhnas ibn Sharīq, Umayya ibn Khalaf, or al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra and his backbiting God’s Messenger. Having explained that the Crusher is the fire which examines the mines of what necessitates it, God follows this with “It is closed over them.” That is, the fire has ascertained these people’s deserts for backbiting the best of humankind, and is therefore closed and shut over them. Their despair of escape and certainty of everlasting imprisonment are thus reinforced.

The second interpretation suits taking “every backbiter and slanderer” generally. This is indicated by his saying, “the laughing-stock who produces outlandish acts and comical displays,” because he attacks people’s reputations like a thief stealing their property. On this interpretation, their remaining for ever in the Fire would follow.

End of the sūrah.

TEXTUAL NOTES
K104-U1: The third variant of “will be cast” is printed as an active verb with an object pronoun; the agent is not explained in the Arabic passage. The English preserves that indeterminacy rather than importing the Turkish comparator’s supplied subject, “wealth.”
T104-U1: The discussion of wa-ʿadadahu moves between a verbal analysis and an accusative nominal analysis with an omitted verb. The cold-water example illustrates an understood “gave to drink”; the compact source wording and both analyses are retained.
T104-U2: XVI:574 prints وأقر بدء إحراق كل أحد على قدر استحقاقه. “The commencement of each person’s burning is fixed according to what he deserves” is a provisional construal of this difficult phrase. The scan confirms the wording; further witnesses are needed.

The translation follows the printed main text and retains this unresolved variant explicitly; it does not silently attribute a settled doctrine of everlasting punishment for every slanderer to Ṭībī.

AL-FĪL (105) — THE ELEPHANT
Complete first-pass translation of both authorial layers • C05 • 27 October 2026

SOURCE AND STATUS
Kashshāf: Dār al-Kitāb al-ʿArabī, 1407 AH, IV:797–800, OpenITI Shamela0023627-ara1. Ṭībī: Futūḥ al-Ghayb, Dubai, 1434/2013, XVI:577–584, OpenITI Sham19Y0020891-ara1. Both layers are translated through the final report and gloss. These historical narratives and reports are the authors’ transmitted material, not independently authenticated conclusions. Modern editorial footnotes are excluded; unresolved readings remain flagged.

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K105.01 — Heading and verses [IV:797]
Meccan; five verses. [The DKA heading additionally places its revelation after al-Kāfirūn.]
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

Have you not seen how your Lord dealt with the People of the Elephant? (1) Did He not bring their scheme to nothing, (2) and send against them birds in flocks, (3) pelting them with stones of sijjīl, (4) and make them like eaten crop-stalks? (5)

K105.02 — Abraha’s expedition [IV:797]
It is related that Abraha ibn al-Ṣabbāḥ al-Ashram, ruler of Yemen on behalf of Aṣḥama the Negus, built a church in Ṣanʿāʾ, named it al-Qullays, and intended to divert the pilgrims to it. A man of Kināna went and “sat” in it at night, angering him. Another account says an Arab travelling party lit a fire, which the wind carried to the church and burned it. Abraha swore to demolish the Kaʿba and set out with the Abyssinians, bringing his enormous, powerful elephant Maḥmūd and twelve other elephants. Eight are also reported; another account says a thousand, while [another has] the elephant alone. [The transmitted conjunction is awkward; the alternatives are not harmonised.]

When he reached al-Mughammas, ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib went out and offered him a third of Tihāma’s wealth to turn back. He refused, arranged his army, and put the elephant in front. Whenever they directed it toward the sanctuary it knelt and would not move; toward Yemen or any other direction it trotted. God sent black birds—also said to be green, or white—each carrying a stone in its beak and two in its feet, larger than a lentil and smaller than a chickpea. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have seen about a qafīz-measure of them at Umm Hāniʾ’s, striped red like Ẓafār agate.

The stone struck a man’s head and emerged from his rear, and each bore the name of the person it would strike. They fled and perished along every road and watering place. Abraha sickened; his fingertips and limbs fell away, and he did not die until his chest split open over his heart. His minister Abū Yaksūm escaped, with his bird circling above him, until he reached the Negus and told him the story. When he finished, the stone fell on him and he collapsed dead before him.

K105.03 — ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib and the prayer [IV:797–798]
It is said that Abraha was the grandfather of the Negus who lived in the Messenger’s time, [Abraha’s rule being] forty years [before the prophetic mission]; twenty-three years is also reported. [The bracketed temporal clarification follows Ṭībī’s explanation below.] ʿĀʾisha says: “I saw the elephant’s driver and keeper, blind and crippled, begging for food.”

The narrative also relates that Abraha took two hundred camels belonging to ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib. He went to recover them, and his imposing appearance impressed Abraha, for he was large and handsome. Abraha was told, “This is Quraysh’s chief and the lord of Mecca’s caravan, who feeds people on the plain and wild animals on the mountain-tops.” When he stated his request, Abraha said, “You have fallen in my estimation. I have come to destroy the House that is your religion and your fathers’ religion, your protection and your ancient honour, yet a few confiscated camels distract you from it!” He replied, “I am the lord of the camels; the House has a Lord who will protect it.” He returned to the House’s door, grasped its ring, and said:
“O God, a man protects his household: protect Your people!
Let not their cross and their power soon overcome Your power.
If You leave them with our Kaʿba, then [do] whatever seems good to You.
Lord, I hope for none against them but You;
Lord, protect Your sanctuary from them.”

K105.04 — The birds and “Have you not seen?” [IV:798–799]
As he prayed he turned and saw birds approaching from Yemen. “By God,” he said, “these are strange birds, neither seabirds nor birds of Tihāma.” The narrative also says that Mecca’s people took possession of the invaders’ property, and ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib amassed a great fortune from their jewels and gold, which made him wealthy. Asked about the birds, Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī said that Mecca’s pigeons descend from them. It is said that they arrived in the evening and attacked next morning. ʿIkrima says anyone struck developed smallpox, the first smallpox to appear.

Alam tar is also read with a quiescent rāʾ, emphatically displaying the effect of the jussive governor. The meaning is: you have seen the effects of God’s act upon the Abyssinians and heard reports of it through widespread transmission (tawātur), so these take the place of direct witnessing. Kayfa, “how,” is accusative through “your Lord dealt,” not through “have you not seen,” because of its interrogative meaning.

“To nothing” (fī taḍlīl): into waste and nullity. Ḍallala kaydahu means making a scheme stray and fail; compare “The unbelievers’ scheming is only in vain” [40:25]. Imruʾ al-Qays was called “the errant king” because he squandered his father’s kingdom. First they plotted against the House by building al-Qullays, intending to supersede it by directing pilgrims there; He frustrated their scheme by burning it. They then plotted to destroy the House; He frustrated that by sending the birds.

K105.05 — Flocks, sijjīl, and eaten stalks [IV:799–800]
Abābīl means bands, singular ibbāla. Their proverb says, “A handful upon a great bundle.” Ibbāla is the great bundle: the birds’ tightly gathered band is likened to it. It is also said that abābīl, like ʿabādīd and shamāṭīṭ, has no singular.

Abū Ḥanīfa read yarmīhim, “he pelts them”: God, or the birds, for ṭayr is a masculine collective noun and is made feminine only according to its meaning.

Sijjīl appears to be a proper name for the register containing the unbelievers’ punishment, just as sijjīn names the register of their deeds: stones forming part of the recorded punishment. It derives from isjāl, sending, because punishment is described as sent: “He sent against them birds”; “We sent against them the flood” [7:133]. Ibn ʿAbbās explains it as clay baked like bricks. It is also said to be Arabicised from Persian sang-gil. Another explanation is “from His severe punishment,” citing Ibn Muqbil’s verse:
“A severe striking (sijjīlan), enjoined by champions upon one another.”
But the actual word is sijjīnan: the poem is a well-known nūn-rhyming poem in his collection.

They are likened to crop leaves when eaten—that is, infested by worms—or to straw eaten and excreted by animals. The expression observes Qurʾānic propriety, like “They both ate food” [5:75]. Or it means that the grain was eaten, leaving the stalks empty.

From God’s Messenger: “Whoever recites Sūrat al-Fīl, God spares him throughout his life from being swallowed by the earth and from bodily transformation.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T105.01 — Names and vocabulary of the expedition [XVI:577–578]
Meccan; five verses. In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

On al-Ashram: sharm means cutting the nose-tip or a she-camel’s vulva. It is said he was called al-Ashram because his father struck him with a spear and split his nose and forehead.

On “he sat in it at night”: an indirect expression meaning he relieved himself. Al-Mughammas is said to be a place between Mecca and Minā. On “he arranged his army,” al-Jawharī says: “ʿAbbaytu l-jaysha taʿbiyatan, taʿbiʾatan and taʿbīʾan means I stationed it in its positions; Abū Zayd gives ʿabaʾtuhu with hamza.”

On “Abraha sickened”: dawā, with short final alif, means illness; dawiya, with kasra, means became ill. It is also explained as “sickened from disease.” On “his limbs”: irb means a limb, as in prostrating on seven ārāb. On “a bird circling”: taḥlīq means rising high in flight.

T105.02 — Chronology and appearance [XVI:579]
“Who lived in the Prophet’s time” distinguishes which Negus is meant. The author of al-Jāmiʿ says: “Negus is the title of Abyssinia’s king. The one who became Muslim and believed in the Prophet was Aṣḥama. He became Muslim before the conquest, died before it, and the Prophet prayed over him.” [His wording is retained.]

“Forty years” means before the prophetic mission. It is a second predicate of the first kāna: he existed and ruled forty years before the mission. This account is closer than twenty-three years, since, by the agreement of transmitters, the Prophet was born in the Elephant’s year and commissioned forty years later. The Negus accepted Islam in the fifth year after the mission. Ibn al-Jawzī reports that the Messenger was born on Monday, ten days into Rabīʿ al-Awwal in the Elephant’s year; Ibn Isḥāq gives twelve days. Ibn Qutayba says: “They agree that God’s Messenger was born in the Elephant’s year.”

“Concerning them” means concerning the camels and recovering them. On “his appearance impressed him,” al-Asās says: “Jahartuhu, ijtahartuhu and istajhartuhu: I saw him as imposing in appearance. Jaharanī fulān: his beauty and bearing struck me with awe.”

T105.03 — The prayer’s wording [XVI:580–581]
Dhawd denotes between three and ten camels. Though the camels were very numerous, Abraha seemingly diminishes their number to belittle the request and deter him from making it in that situation.

On the verses beginning “Lāhumma”: its original form is Allāhumma. Riḥālaka is transmitted, and also ḥilālaka, plural of ḥilla, a place where people settle. Ḥilāl, with kasra, also means people settled together as neighbours: the sanctuary’s inhabitants. Al-Asās gives “I settled among the people” and “I settled in the dwelling”; it is their maḥalla and ḥilla; a tribe described as ḥilla or ḥilāl is settled in one place.

On “their cross”: one says the Byzantines came with crosses. Maḥāla and miḥāl mean resourceful contrivance, as in “Man fails, but resourcefulness does not.” Miḥāl is also said to mean punishment or strength, as in “He is mighty in power” [13:13].

In “fa-amrun mā,” mā is either additional for reinforcement or relative: whatever benefit seems good to You. Al-Nihāya explains ghaduwwan, with dotted ghayn: “Ghaduww is the original form of ghad, the day following today; its final root letter was dropped. The full form occurs only in verse, as in:
‘People are only like dwellings and their inhabitants:
inhabited the day they settle, and tomorrow desolate.’
ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib did not mean the particular next day, but a near time.”

T105.04 — A great fortune [XVI:581]
Al-jawr, with fatḥa on jīm, quiescent wāw and rāʾ, occurs in a copy collated against the author’s handwriting: abundant wealth, so called because its accumulation exceeds the limit. Al-ḥawz, with ḥāʾ and zāy, is also transmitted. Al-Jawharī says: “Ḥawz is gathering; whoever brings something into his possession has ḥāzahu ḥawzan wa-ḥiyāzatan, and iḥtāzahu.” Al-juʾr also occurs. Al-Jawharī says: “Ghayth juʾr is copious, abundant rain; juʾar, like nughar, is also said.” They recite:
“Let no heavy, thunder-roaring downpour water it.”
ʿAzf here is the rumble of thunder.

T105.05 — Seeing how, and the prelude to prophecy [XVI:582]
On “you have seen the effects of God’s act,” the Qāḍī says: “‘Have you not seen?’ addresses God’s Messenger. Though he did not witness the event, he saw its effects and heard widely transmitted reports, as though he had seen it. ‘How He dealt’ is said rather than ‘what He did’ because the point is to recall its indications of God’s perfect knowledge and power and His Prophet’s dignity and honour: it belongs to the anticipatory signs of prophecy.”

The Imām says: “Things have entities and modes. Their modes are what theologians call ‘the aspect establishing the proof’ (wajh al-dalīl). Desert of praise is discerned by seeing modes, not merely entities. Hence: ‘Have they not looked at the sky above them, how We built it?’ [50:6]. This event undoubtedly laid the foundation for his prophethood and anticipated his mission.” Irhāṣ derives from rahṣ, the bottom course of a wall: something resembling a miracle precedes the prophetic claim, such as clouds shading the Messenger and stones and clods speaking to him.

Ḥazāʾiq means groups. Al-Asās gives ḥizqa, ḥazīqa and ḥazīq for a group, and “they followed one another like bands of locusts.”

T105.06 — Bundles, scattered bands, and the disputed verse [XVI:583–584]
On “a handful upon a great bundle,” al-Maydānī says: “Ibbāla is a bundle of firewood; ḍighth a handful of mixed fresh and dry grass. Ībāla is also transmitted, and some lighten it to ibāla. Its meaning is one affliction upon another.”

On ʿabādīd and shamāṭīṭ, al-Jawharī says: “ʿAbādīd are groups of people going in every direction. Shamāṭīṭ are scattered pieces; horses arriving shamāṭīṭ arrive dispersed in separate groups.”

On isjāl, sending, al-Asās says: “This is musjal: released, unrestricted; one may take it or leave it. Asjaltu l-bahīmata maʿa ummihā means I let the young animal go with its mother.”

On “His severe punishment,” al-Zajjāj says: “When Arabs describe something disagreeable as sijjīl they mean severity; the term is not applied otherwise. Ibn Muqbil says:
‘And foot-soldiers striking the bright blades openly,
a severe striking, enjoined by champions upon one another.’”

In his book’s marginal note: “Abū ʿUbayda cited it thus in his Majāz; Ibn Muqbil’s poetry has sijjīnan, and that is correct. Rijla is the collective of rājil, foot-soldier. A thing’s ḍāḥiya is its exposed side. Sijjīnan qualifies ḍarban.” Another transmission instead has “the bright blades from various sides” (al-bīḍ ʿan ʿurḍ). Al-bīḍ means swords. ʿUrḍ, with ḍamma, means the middle or side of something. Thus: many foot-soldiers strike with swords in battle from different sides, striking fiercely as champions enjoin upon one another.

T105.07 — Qurʾānic propriety and the empty stalks [XVI:584]
On “like ‘they both ate food’” [5:75]: in the two verses animal dung and human waste are expressed indirectly, observing propriety. Their dismemberment is likened to the dispersal of particles of dung, at once preserving propriety and displaying the ugliness of their state and outcome.

On “its grain was eaten, leaving it empty”: devoid of good. The meaning is stalks whose grain has been eaten, like saying “So-and-so is beautiful” meaning beautiful of face: what is understood is omitted. This is al-Ḥasan’s interpretation.

End of the sūrah.

TEXTUAL AND REVIEW NOTES

QURAYSH (106)
Complete first-pass translation of both authorial layers • C05 • 27 October 2026

SOURCE AND STATUS
Kashshāf: Dār al-Kitāb al-ʿArabī, 1407 AH, IV:800–803, OpenITI Shamela0023627-ara1, compared with the embedded Kashshāf in Ṭībī. Ṭībī: Futūḥ al-Ghayb, Dubai, 1434/2013, XVI:585–590, OpenITI Sham19Y0020891-ara1. Both layers are translated to the end. Modern editorial footnotes are excluded. Reports are translated as attributed by the authors, not independently authenticated. First draft; see the C05 review ledger and textual notes.

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K106.01 — Heading, verses, and the governing construction [IV:800–801]
Meccan; four verses. [The DKA heading additionally places its revelation after al-Tīn.]
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

For the accustomed security of Quraysh—(1) their accustomed winter and summer journey—(2) let them worship the Lord of this House, (3) who has fed them against hunger and secured them against fear. (4)

“For the accustomed security of Quraysh” (li-īlāfi Quraysh) depends grammatically on “let them worship”: He commands them to worship Him because He has accustomed them to the two journeys. If you ask why fāʾ then enters, I reply: because the statement carries a conditional meaning. Its sense is: if for nothing else, let them worship Him for their accustomed security. God’s blessings upon them are innumerable; if they do not worship Him for His other blessings, let them worship Him for this one conspicuous blessing.

It is also said to mean: “Marvel at Quraysh’s accustomed security.” Another interpretation connects it to what precedes: “He made them like eaten stalks for the accustomed security of Quraysh.” This resembles enjambment (taḍmīn) in poetry: the meaning of one verse depends upon the preceding verse in such a way that it cannot stand correctly without it. In Ubayy’s codex the two are one sūrah, with no division. It is related of ʿUmar that he recited them both in the second unit of the sunset prayer, having recited “By the fig” in the first.

The meaning is that He destroyed the Abyssinians who intended to attack them, so that people would hear of this, stand in greater awe of them, and accord them increased respect. Thus security would be established for their two journeys and no one would dare attack them.

K106.02 — The journeys and variant forms [IV:801]
Quraysh had two journeys: they travelled to Yemen in winter and Syria in summer, obtaining provisions and trading. They were secure on both journeys because they were the people of God’s sanctuary and custodians of His House; no one molested them, whereas other people were seized and raided.

Īlāf comes from ālaftu l-makāna, ūlifuhu īlāfan: I became accustomed to a place; hence I am muʾlif. The poet says:
“Among those accustomed to the [herbage / easy journey], not those grazing arāk.”
[The DKA transcription reads al-rahw, “easy travelling”; Ṭībī’s printed base reads al-zahw and explains both forms below.]

The reading li-ilāfi Quraysh also occurs, meaning “because of Quraysh’s becoming accustomed.” It is said that one says aliftuhu ilfan wa-ilāfan. Abū Jaʿfar reads li-ilfi Quraysh. Both forms occur in the poet’s words:
“You claim that your brothers are Quraysh;
they have ilf, while you have no ilāf.”

K106.03 — The name Quraysh and the syntax of “journey” [IV:802]
ʿIkrima reads li-yaʾlafa Qurayshun, ilfahum riḥlata l-shitāʾi wa-l-ṣayf. Quraysh are the descendants of al-Naḍr ibn Kināna. They were named with the diminutive of al-qirsh, a great sea creature that attacks ships and can be overcome only with fire. Muʿāwiya is reported to have asked Ibn ʿAbbās, “Why was Quraysh so named?” He replied, “After a sea creature that eats but is not eaten and overcomes but is not overcome,” and recited:
“Quraysh is the creature dwelling in the sea;
after it Quraysh was named Quraysh.”

The diminutive conveys magnification. Another explanation derives it from qarsh, earning, because they earned through trade and travelling through the lands.

Īlāf is first stated without qualification, then replaced by an appositional expression specifying the two journeys, to magnify this accustomed security and remind them of the greatness of its blessing. Riḥla is accusative as the object of īlāfihim, just as yatīman is made accusative by iṭʿām [90:14–15]. The two journeys of winter and summer are meant. The singular is used because confusion is impossible, as in:
“Eat into part of your belly [rather than fill your bellies].”

K106.04 — Hunger, fear, and the concluding report [IV:803]
Ruḥla is also read, with ḍamma: the destination to which one travels. The indefiniteness of “hunger” and “fear” indicates their severity. Through the two journeys He fed them against the severe hunger they had previously suffered and secured them against great fear: fear of the People of the Elephant, or fear of being seized in their own country and on their routes.

It is also said that hardship had afflicted them until they ate carrion and burnt bones; and that He secured them against fear of leprosy, so that it does not afflict them in their country. All this, it is said, came through Abraham’s prayer. Among novel interpretations is: “He secured them against fear” that the caliphate might belong to others. Min khawf is also read with concealment of the nūn [ikhfāʾ].

From God’s Messenger: “Whoever recites the sūrah ‘For the accustomed security of Quraysh,’ God gives him ten good deeds for every person who circumambulates the Kaʿba and stays there in devotional retreat.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T106.01 — The fāʾ and the implied condition [XVI:585–586]
Meccan; four verses. In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

On “why, then, does fāʾ enter?”: the fāʾ [in the question fa-lima] indicates objection. That is: if li-īlāf depends on fa-l-yaʿbudū, why does the fāʾ of succession stand between the governing expression and what it governs? He answers that fāʾ introduces the consequence of an omitted condition. This must be supplied, because if one reconstructs simply “let them worship Him for the accustomed security of Quraysh,” the fāʾ remains without anything to which it relates.

It may also be understood as reinforcement, with fāʾ marking succession: “For Quraysh’s accustomed security, let them worship Him—so let them worship.” Likewise His saying “so let them rejoice” [10:58]. Its permissibility has already been transmitted from al-Zubayr, from al-Zajjāj. His saying “and your Lord—magnify Him” [74:3] follows this pattern. He says: “Fāʾ enters because of the conditional meaning, as though it said, ‘Whatever happens, do not cease to magnify Him.’”

On “the meaning is: immā lā, let them worship Him”: the author is reported to have said, “The Arabs say ‘Do this, immā lā,’ meaning ‘If you will not do something else, do this.’ Mā is additional, compensating for the omitted kāna. They pronounce lā with imāla because it stands in place of the verb, like balā, both taking the verb’s place. One also says, ‘Give me this, immā lā.’”

T106.02 — Linking the two sūrahs [XVI:586]
On “He made them like eaten stalks for the accustomed security of Quraysh,” al-Zajjāj says: “The meaning is: God destroyed the People of the Elephant so that Quraysh and their accustomed winter and summer journeys might remain.”

On “in the second [unit] of the sunset prayer”: that is, in the second rakʿa; in the first rakʿa he recited Sūrat “By the fig.” This apparently indicates that they are one sūrah. [This is Ṭībī’s inference, retained as his statement.]

T106.03 — The camel verse and the verbal noun [XVI:587]
On “among those accustomed”: one says ālaftu l-makāna, ūlifuhu īlāfan when one becomes accustomed to a place; hence muʾlif. [The cited wording is] al-zahw ghayra l-awārik. Zahw denotes being short of ripeness; zahw also means herbage and dates beginning to colour. One says zahat al-ibilu zahwan when camels travel for a night or more after watering. Zahawtuhā, “I drove them,” is also used: the verb is both transitive and intransitive. Zāhiya camels do not graze saline plants. Some transmit al-rahw with rāʾ: easy travelling. One says, “The horses came at an easy pace.” Awārik is the plural of ārika: camels eating arāk. Al-Jawharī says: “Arakat means that she stayed among the arāk, which belongs to the saline vegetation; she is ārika, plural awārik.”

On “that is, because of Quraysh’s becoming accustomed”: according to this interpretation, ilāf is the verbal noun of fāʿala, and means muʾālafa, like ḍāraba, muḍārabatan and ḍirāban.

T106.04 — Two poems [XVI:588–589]
On “it is also said”: this indicates a verbal noun of faʿala, like kataba kitāban.

After the verse beginning “You claim” comes:
“Those people were secured against hunger and fear,
whereas the sons of Asad have hungered and feared.”

Its author is Musāwir ibn Hind, satirising Banū Asad. He says: you are not of Quraysh, nor are Quraysh of you; your claim to their brotherhood is false, for they were fed against hunger and secured against fear, whereas you were not. The author says: “This belongs to the verses with subtle meanings: the first hemistich reports their claim, while the second argues against them and binds them to its consequence.”

On the verse beginning “Quraysh is the creature”: al-Wāḥidī and Muḥyī l-Sunna transmit the following as al-Jumaḥī’s:
“Quraysh is the creature dwelling in the sea;
after it Quraysh was named Quraysh.
It eats the lean and the fat, and never leaves
a feather to anything with two wings.
So too, throughout the lands, the tribe Quraysh
devours the lands in vigorous fashion.
In the latter age they shall have a prophet
who multiplies killing and scratching wounds among them.”

T106.05 — The object and the destination [XVI:589]
On “just as yatīman is made accusative by iṭʿām” [90:14–15], Abū l-Baqāʾ says: “Yatīman is the object of iṭʿām. Some Basrans hold that when the verbal noun governs an object, it contains a pronoun like the pronoun within the active participle.”

On “the destination to which one travels,” al-Kawāshī says: “Originally riḥla meant travelling on a riding camel; it then came to be used for every kind of travel.”

End of the sūrah [XVI:590; no further unique gloss follows the final Kashshāf block].

AL-MĀʿŪN (107) — SMALL ACTS OF ASSISTANCE
Complete first-pass translation of both authorial layers • C05 • 27 October 2026

SOURCE AND STATUS
Kashshāf: DKA, 1407 AH, IV:803–806, OpenITI Shamela0023627-ara1. Ṭībī: Dubai, 1434/2013, XVI:591–599, OpenITI Sham19Y0020891-ara1. Both authorial layers are translated through their endings. The different headings are retained explicitly. Modern editorial notes are excluded. Claims about faith and unbelief are the authors’ arguments, not independent judgments on individuals. Complete first draft; final review pending.

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K107.01 — Heading, verses, and readings [IV:803]
The DKA heading describes the first three verses as Meccan and the remainder as Medinan, seven verses in all, and places revelation after al-Takāthur. Ṭībī’s embedded Kashshāf heading says: Meccan, also said to be Medinan; seven verses.
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

Have you seen the one who denies recompense? (1) That is the one who violently repulses the orphan (2) and does not urge feeding the needy. (3) So woe to those who pray, (4) yet are heedless of their prayer, (5) who make a display, (6) and withhold assistance. (7)

A-rayta is also read with the hamza omitted. It is not the preferred form, since dropping that hamza is peculiar to the imperfect; raytu is not established Arabic usage. What makes it easier here is the initial interrogative particle. Compare:
“Friend, have you seen or heard of a herdsman
returning to the udder what he gathered in the milking bowl?”

K107.02 — The orphan and signs of denial [IV:804]
Ibn Masʿūd read a-raʾaytaka, adding the address-marker, as in “Tell me of this one whom You have honoured above me” [17:62]. The meaning is: do you know who denies recompense? If not, that denier is the one who pushes the orphan away violently, with cruelty and injury, rejecting him harshly with rebuke. Yadaʿu is also read: abandons and treats coldly. “Does not urge”: does not encourage his household to give the needy food.

Withholding kindness and daring to injure the weak are made the sign of denying recompense. Had he believed in recompense and been certain of the threat, he would have feared God and His punishment and not done this. His doing it reveals denial. How severe these words, how fearful the situation, how forceful the warning against sin and its fitness to indicate weak faith and a slack bond of certainty!

“So woe to those who pray” is then connected: if this is so, woe to those who neglect prayer from indifference, until they miss it or its time passes; or who fail to perform it as the Messenger and the early generations did, but peck through it without humility and submission or avoiding disliked acts—playing with beard and clothes, excessive yawning and looking about. One finishes without knowing how many units he prayed or which sūrahs he recited, like the prayer of so many you see, habitually displaying their deeds and withholding their wealth’s dues.

Their neglect of prayer, religion’s pillar and the distinction between faith and unbelief; their ostentation, a branch of associating partners with God; and their refusal of alms, prayer’s counterpart and Islam’s bridge, are all the more fitting signs that they deny recompense. How many bearing Islam’s name—even scholars—you see like this! What a calamity!

K107.03 — Another construction; neglect of prayer versus lapses in it [IV:804–805]
Alternatively fa-dhālika is conjoined to “the one who denies,” either joining one person to another or one attribute to another. The answer to a-raʾayta is omitted, indicated by what follows: tell me what you say about one who denies recompense and one who harms the orphan and does not feed the needy—is his conduct good? Then: “woe to those who pray,” meaning, once his wrongdoing is known, woe to them. Their description replaces their pronoun, because along with denial and the other attributed acts they neglected prayer, made a display, and failed to purify their property by alms.

If you ask how the plural “those who pray” replaces the pronoun of the singular “one who denies,” I reply: the meaning is plural, because the class is intended.

What distinguishes “heedless of their prayer” (ʿan) from “heedless in their prayer” (fī)? ʿAn means neglecting and abandoning it, paying it little attention: the conduct of hypocrites or brazenly sinful Muslims. Fī means lapses occurring during it through Satan’s whispering or inward thoughts, from which scarcely any Muslim is free. Even God’s Messenger had lapses during prayer, let alone others; hence jurists include chapters on the prostration for forgetfulness. Anas said, “Praise God that He did not say ‘in their prayer.’” Ibn Masʿūd read lāhūna, “distracted.”

K107.04 — Ostentation and assistance [IV:805–806]
If you ask what ostentation (murāʾāh) means, I reply: it is the reciprocal form from showing. The ostentatious person shows others his deed, and they show him praise and admiration. Displaying a righteous deed does not make someone ostentatious if it is obligatory. Obligations should be public and manifest, as in the Prophet’s saying, “There is no concealment in God’s obligations”: they are Islam’s signs and religion’s rites. Their omission deserves blame and abhorrence, so suspicion must be removed by making performance visible.

A voluntary deed should be concealed: leaving it incurs neither blame nor suspicion. Showing it so that others follow the example is good. Ostentation consists specifically in showing it so that eyes see and people praise one’s righteousness. Someone saw a man prolonging a prostration of thanksgiving in the mosque and said, “How fine this would be in your house!” He said so because he discerned display and desire for reputation. Avoiding ostentation is difficult except for those trained in sincerity. Hence the Messenger said, “Ostentation is more hidden than a black ant’s movement on black haircloth in a dark night.”

Al-māʿūn means obligatory alms. Al-Rāʿī says:
“A people remaining in Islam, who have not withheld
their alms nor neglected declaring God’s oneness.”

Ibn Masʿūd explains it as things ordinarily lent among people: an axe, cooking pot, bucket, fire-kindler and the like. ʿĀʾisha says: water, fire and salt. Withholding these may be forbidden when urgently needed, and contrary to decent conduct even when necessity is absent.

From God’s Messenger: “Whoever recites Sūrat ‘Have you seen,’ God forgives him if he pays the obligatory alms.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T107.01 — The heading, reading, and poetic illustration [XVI:591–592]
Ṭībī’s own heading: Medinan; six verses. [This differs from the embedded base text and is preserved, not silently corrected.]
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

A-rayta is al-Kisāʾī’s reading. The initial interrogative makes a second hamza burdensome, hence its omission. The verse beginning “Friend” resembles Abū l-Ṭayyib’s:
“Youth once gone cannot be recovered;
no passing day can be brought back.”
Ṣāḥ originally means yā ṣāḥib, shortened by truncation. Qary is gathering water in a basin. An ʿulba is a wooden milking bowl, plural ʿulab or ʿilāb. The poet asks whether his friend ever saw or heard of a herdsman returning to the udder milk already drawn and gathered in the bowl.

On adding the address-marker: the kāf reinforces the address already expressed by tāʾ. Al-Rāghib says: “Ḥaḍḍ is urging, like ḥathth, except that ḥathth may concern travel and driving onward, while ḥaḍḍ does not. Its origin is urging onto the ḥaḍīḍ, the ground’s lower level.”

T107.02 — Culpable heedlessness and hurried prayer [XVI:593–594]
Al-Rāghib says: “Sahw is an error from inattention, of two kinds. One arises from causes a person himself brought about: someone drinks wine and commits wrongdoing without intending it. The other does not arise from his own causes: an insane person insults someone. The second is pardoned; the first is accountable. The first kind is what God condemns in ‘woe to those who pray, heedless of their prayer.’”

“Or fail to perform it” is conjoined to “neglect prayer.” Thus neglect means delaying prayer beyond its time through indifference, or inattentively omitting its constituent acts, forms, proprieties and stillness. Hence “they peck through it like a bird pecking grain.” Abū Dāwūd and al-Nasāʾī transmit from ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Shibl: the Messenger forbade pecking like a crow, spreading oneself like a predatory beast, and claiming a habitual place as a camel does.

Al-Bukhārī and al-Nasāʾī transmit from Zayd ibn Wahb: Ḥudhayfa saw a man praying deficiently and asked how long he had prayed this way. “Forty years.” “You have not prayed for forty years. If you died praying thus, you would die outside Muḥammad’s original way.” He added, “A person may pray briefly yet completely and well.”

T107.03 — The escalating argument [XVI:594–595]
“Ostentation” and “withholding alms” are nominative, conjoined to “their neglect,” the subject of yakūna; “a sign” is the predicate, understood similarly for the coordinated terms, as in:
“We with what we have, and you with what you have,
are content, though judgment differs.”

These things are signs of denying recompense because “woe to those who pray” follows as consequence from cause and result from condition, with an escalation: do you recognise the denier? He repulses the orphan and withholds his good. Do you recognise something still greater and more dreadful? Abandoning prayer and alms and displaying one’s deeds are greater, because worship is the intrinsic purpose of the world’s creation. On this account māʿūn must mean withholding alms, completing the mention of prayer rather than introducing a further escalation. Denial of recompense thus fundamentally nullifies the wisdom in creating heaven and earth, legislating worship, and urging all other good deeds. God protect us from it.

The Imām says: “Denying resurrection is like the root of every kind of unbelief and sin. God makes harming the weak and withholding kindness its sign: belief in recompense and certainty of the threat would prevent them. Denial of resurrection therefore produces the sin.”

T107.04 — Who are “those who pray”? [XVI:595–596]
On joining persons or attributes: in the first earlier construction fāʾ answers an omitted condition, “If you do not know, then recognise him as that denier.” If a particular person is intended, “the one” denotes someone known; if an attribute, it can also denote the class. Hence the differing identifications: Muqātil gives al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil; al-Suddī and Muqātil give al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra; Ibn ʿAbbās gives a hypocrite. These occur in al-Maʿālim.

Al-Kawāshī says: “Do not pause after ‘the needy’ if ‘the one’ denotes the class and the praying people belong to the same statement. What a-raʾayta relates to is then omitted: what do you say of one who denies truth, repulses the orphan and injures the needy—has he acted well? Woe to them! ‘Those who pray’ replaces ‘them.’”

I say: this shows that on the first interpretation “woe to those who pray” is separate from the preceding statement as regards its referent. The denier is an unbeliever such as al-Walīd or al-ʿĀṣ; the praying people are Muslims. Making injury and withheld kindness signs of denial signals their severity, so believers avoid such conduct, characteristic of unbelievers denying Judgment Day. Hence the author’s exclamations about the severe words and fearful situation, and their indication of weak faith.

His saying “he then connected ‘woe to those who pray,’ as though saying ‘if the matter is thus’” also indicates distinct referents: he mentions “the matter,” without saying an explicit noun replaces a pronoun. By contrast, on the final interpretation he expressly says “woe to them.” There the praying people are the deniers, since he says they were heedless of prayer “alongside their denial.” The Imām says: “On this interpretation the verse shows an unbeliever receiving additional punishment for prohibited acts and omitted religious obligations. This supports al-Shāfiʿī’s position that unbelievers are addressed by the subsidiary ordinances of revealed law.”

T107.05 — Objections to the distinction between ʿan and fī [XVI:597]
The Imām says: “ʿAṭāʾ transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās: had God said ‘heedless in their prayer,’ believers would be more exposed to this threat. Instead He says ‘heedless of their prayer’: not remembering it and being disengaged from it. This interpretation is weak: heedlessness of prayer cannot mean abandoning prayer, since ‘those who pray’ affirms their prayer; moreover, abandoning it through heedlessness is neither hypocrisy nor unbelief.

“The first objection may be answered: He calls them praying people regarding the outward prayer, and says they have entirely forgotten prayer regarding its meaning, as in ‘When they stand for prayer, they stand lazily, showing themselves to people, and remember God only a little’ [4:142].”

I say: “those who pray” may mean those whose proper role is to discharge gratitude owed for God’s blessings. He therefore attributes prayer to them—“their prayer”—indicating an established duty upon the morally responsible. They should not pass from maintaining its pillars, forms and customary practices into inattention, much less abandonment. This rests on unbelievers’ being addressed by subsidiary ordinances.

The Imām says: “The second objection is answered by understanding forgetfulness of prayer as remaining forgetful of God throughout every part. This proceeds only from a hypocrite who thinks prayer useless. A Muslim who believes its benefits cannot fail throughout all its parts to recall religion, reward or punishment. He may indeed lapse during some parts. Thus inattention in prayer is a believer’s act, and inattention of prayer an unbeliever’s.” [The argumentative claim is translated as his, without turning it into a diagnostic rule.]

T107.06 — Public obligations, the poem, and lending [XVI:598–599]
“No concealment” is also transmitted as “no gharar.” Al-Nihāya explains Wāʾil ibn Ḥujr’s report: God’s obligations are not covered and hidden; they are displayed, announced and voiced publicly.

The poem “a people remaining in Islam” alludes to apostates who withheld alms: we are not apostates, so do not treat us like them.

On things ordinarily passed between borrowers, al-Jawharī says: “Iʿtawarū l-shayʾ means they passed it among themselves; taʿawwarūhu and taʿāwarūhu mean the same.”

End of the sūrah.

REVIEW FLAGS
T107-U1: The six-verse/Medinan heading differs from the base’s seven-verse alternatives. It is preserved as transmitted, not adopted as a settled verse count.
T107-U2: The elaborate argument on ʿan/fī and legal responsibility is fully retained. Printed XVI:597 was inspected; further comparison of the quoted authorities is pending.

AL-KAWTHAR (108) — ABUNDANT GOOD
Complete first-pass translation of both authorial layers • C05 • 27 October 2026

SOURCE AND STATUS
Kashshāf: Dār al-Kitāb al-ʿArabī, 1407 AH, IV:806–808, OpenITI Shamela0023627-ara1, collated with the embedded base text in Ṭībī. Ṭībī: Futūḥ al-Ghayb, Dubai, 1434/2013, XVI:600–605, OpenITI Sham19Y0020891-ara1, with the omitted report wording restored from printed XVI:603. Both authorial layers extend through their closing reports. Modern editorial footnotes are excluded. Report attribution is preserved without independent authentication. This is a complete first draft, with unresolved readings explicitly marked below.

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K108.01 — Heading, verses, and the meaning of al-kawthar [IV:806]
Meccan; three verses. [The DKA heading additionally places its revelation after al-ʿĀdiyāt.]
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

We have indeed given you abundant good. (1) So pray to your Lord and sacrifice. (2) Surely it is the one who hates you who is cut off. (3)

In the recitation of God’s Messenger: innā anṭaynāka, with nūn [instead of the ʿayn in aʿṭaynāka]. In his saying: “And give the middling portion” (wa-anṭū l-thabaja).

Al-kawthar has the pattern fawʿal, from kathra, abundance: it means what is exceedingly abundant. A Bedouin woman whose son had returned from a journey was asked, “What did your son bring back?” She replied, “He returned with kawthar.” The poet says:
“You are abundant in good, son of Marwān, and wholesome;
your father, the son of noble women, was abundant in good.”

K108.02 — The river and its first visitors [IV:807]
It is also said that al-Kawthar is a river in Paradise. From the Prophet: when this sūrah was revealed, he recited it and said, “Do you know what al-Kawthar is? It is a river in Paradise that my Lord has promised me, containing abundant good.” Its description is transmitted as follows: “Sweeter than honey, whiter than milk, colder than snow, and softer than butter; its banks are chrysolite, and its vessels are silver, as numerous as the stars of heaven.”

It is also transmitted: “Whoever drinks from it never thirsts again. The first to reach it are the poor Emigrants: dirty in clothing, dishevelled in hair, who are not given pampered women in marriage and for whom the doors of the entrance halls are not opened.” One of them dies with his need still turning within his breast; were he to adjure God, God would fulfil his oath.

From Ibn ʿAbbās: he interpreted al-kawthar as abundant good. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr said to him, “Some people say it is a river in Paradise!” He replied, “That is part of the abundant good.” [The attribution differs in the report that Ṭībī quotes below; both authors’ formulations are retained.]

K108.03 — Prayer, sacrifice, and the double gift [IV:807]
Naḥr means slaughtering sacrificial camels. According to ʿAṭiyya, the prayer is the dawn prayer at Jamʿ [Muzdalifa], and the slaughter is at Minā. It is also said to mean the festival prayer and sacrifice. Another interpretation takes prayer generically and naḥr as placing the right hand over the left.

The meaning is: you have been given limitless good in both abodes, such as no one else has been given, and the giver of all this is I, the God of the worlds. Thus the two exalted grounds for being envied without ill will have come together for you: receiving the noblest and most abundant gift from the most generous giver and greatest benefactor. So worship your Lord, who has honoured you through His giving, ennobled you, and protected you from dependence on the favours of created beings, thereby defying your people who worship other than God. When you slaughter, slaughter for His sake and in His name, opposing their slaughter for idols.

K108.04 — The one truly cut off [IV:807–808]
It is the person among your people who hates you because you oppose them who is cut off, not you. Every believer born until the Day of Resurrection is among your children and descendants. Your remembrance is raised on pulpits and minarets and on the tongue of every scholar and person who remembers God until the end of time: God is mentioned first and you second. In the hereafter you have what description cannot encompass. Someone such as you is not called cut off. The one cut off is your hater, forgotten in this world and the hereafter; if mentioned, he is mentioned with a curse.

They used to say, “Muḥammad is a ṣunbūr: when he dies, his remembrance dies.” It is said that the verse was revealed concerning al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil, who called him al-abtar. Al-abtar means someone without offspring; hence a tailless donkey is called al-ḥimār al-abtar.

K108.05 — Closing report [IV:808]
From God’s Messenger: “Whoever recites Sūrat al-Kawthar, God gives him drink from every river in Paradise, and ten good deeds are recorded for him for every offering the servants have offered on the Day of Sacrifice or will offer.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T108.01 — The dialectal verb and the poem [XVI:600–601]
Meccan; three verses. In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

On “and give the middling portion,” al-Nihāya says: “This is the language of Yemen. He wrote to Wāʾil: ‘Anṭū l-thabaja,’ meaning: give the middle quality as alms, neither the choicest property nor its poorest. The feminine ending was attached because the word shifted from noun to adjective.”

On “son of noble women”: women of the choicest kind; the ʿaqīla of anything is its noblest part. A kawthar among men is one abundant in good and giving. The verse is by al-Kumayt.

T108.02 — Reports concerning the river [XVI:601]
On “it is a river in Paradise”: we transmit in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, from Ibn ʿAbbās, who said concerning al-kawthar: “It is abundant good.” Ibn Jubayr was asked, “But people maintain that it is a river in Paradise?” Saʿīd replied, “The river in Paradise is part of the good that God Most High gave him.”

From Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal, al-Tirmidhī, Ibn Mājah and al-Dārimī, from Ibn ʿUmar: God’s Messenger said, “Al-Kawthar is a river in Paradise. Its banks are gold, and its course runs over pearls and rubies. Its soil is more fragrant than musk, and its water sweeter than honey and whiter than snow.”

In ʿĀʾisha’s report: “Its banks are hollowed pearls, and its vessels as numerous as the stars of heaven.” Al-Bukhārī transmitted it.

T108.03 — Those denied entry at the doors [XVI:602]
On “for whom the doors of the entrance halls are not opened”: the report is transmitted by al-Tirmidhī from Thawbān. God’s Messenger said: “My pool is as extensive as the distance between Aden and ʿAmmān of al-Balqāʾ. Its water is whiter than snow and sweeter than honey, and its cups are as numerous as the stars of heaven. Whoever drinks from it never thirsts afterward. The first people to reach me there are the poor Emigrants, dishevelled in hair and dirty in clothing, who do not marry pampered women and for whom the doors of the entrance halls are not opened.”

Al-Tirmidhī says: ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz said, “I have married a pampered woman, Fāṭima daughter of ʿAbd al-Malik, and the doors of the entrance halls have been opened for me. Therefore I shall not wash my head until it becomes dishevelled, nor the garment next to my body until it becomes dirty.”

In al-Jāmiʿ: “Sudad is the plural of sudda, which here means door.” In al-Nihāya: “A sudda is like an awning over a doorway, protecting it from rain. It is also said to be the open space before the door, or the door itself: that is, doors are not opened for them. In Abū l-Dardāʾ’s report, he came to Muʿāwiya’s door and was not admitted. He said, ‘Whoever frequents the ruler’s entrance is made to stand and sit.’”

I say: the more fitting interpretation is to take the genitive construction ‘doors of the sudad’ as explanatory, making it an indirect expression for the doors of kings and great men, with sudda understood as the awning or forecourt.

T108.04 — The report concerning al-Rubayyiʿ [XVI:602–603]
On “were he to adjure God, God would fulfil his oath”: he said this in the report concerning al-Rubayyiʿ. We transmit from al-Bukhārī, Muslim, Abū Dāwūd and al-Nasāʾī, from Anas ibn Mālik: his paternal aunt al-Rubayyiʿ broke a girl’s incisor. They sought a pardon from her people, but they refused. They offered compensation, but they refused. They came to God’s Messenger and insisted on retaliation. God’s Messenger ordered retaliation.

Anas ibn al-Naḍr said, “Messenger of God, will al-Rubayyiʿ’s incisor be broken? No! By Him who sent you with the truth, her incisor shall not be broken.” God’s Messenger said, “Anas, does not God’s Book prescribe retaliation?” Then the people were satisfied and pardoned her. God’s Messenger said, “Among God’s servants are those who, were they to adjure God, He would fulfil their oath.” It means: were such a person to ask God, He would answer him. Adjuration here means an appeal for favour (istiʿṭāf).

T108.05 — The Giver and the gift [XVI:603]
On “the giver of all this is I, the God of the worlds”: this signals a preference for Ibn ʿAbbās’s interpretation, that al-kawthar means abundant good. It also draws out what the plural pronoun conveys of greatness and majesty, for its speaker is none other than the God of the worlds; and [the text is difficult here] “even if the gift were not great, the Giver is great.”

Because of these two correspondences, “So pray to your Lord” follows, with an explicit noun replacing a pronoun. That is: since both gift and Giver are great, perform the greatest bodily and financial acts of worship that you can. Slaughter is preferred as the expression so as to incorporate the meaning of cutting the soul off from immediate pleasures. “Surely it is the one who hates you who is cut off” is then joined to this, completing the good news. The Imām says: “Having given him good news of immense blessings, and knowing that their completion consists in the subjugation of enemies, He said, ‘Surely it is the one who hates you who is cut off.’”

Al-Sulamī transmits from Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq: “We have given you a light in your heart that guided you to Me and cut you off from everything besides Me.” From al-Qāsim: “Your hater is the one cut off from the good things of both abodes.” God knows best.

T108.06 — Minarets, indirect expression, and ṣunbūr [XVI:604]
On “and the manār,” al-Nihāya says: “Manār is the plural of manāra: the marker between two boundaries. Hence Abū Hurayra’s report, ‘Islam has waymarks and manār’: signs and ordinances by which it is recognised.” It is also said that manāʾir is the plural of the manāra from which the call to prayer is made. Its original form is manāwir, since it derives from nūr, light; hamza replaces wāw. A root letter may be treated like an added one, as in maṣāʾib, originally maṣāwib.

On “someone such as you is not called cut off”: this resembles “someone such as you is not miserly” as an indirect expression (kināya). It means: someone with your attribute—every believer born until the end of time being his child—is not called cut off.

On ṣunbūr, al-Nihāya says: “A person cut off, without offspring. Originally ṣunbūr is a palm shoot growing on the trunk rather than in the ground. It is also said to be a solitary palm whose lower part is slender. They meant that when he was uprooted his remembrance would cease, just as the trace of a ṣunbūr disappears, because he has no offspring.”

T108.07 — “Or will offer” [XVI:605]
On “or will offer”: according to some, “or” distinguishes the kinds [past and future offerings].

End of the sūrah.

SŪRAT AL-KĀFIRŪN (109): THE UNBELIEVERS
Complete first-draft translation of al-Zamakhsharī and al-Ṭībī
Checkpoint C05 • 27 October 2026

Coverage: all six verses, the complete Kashshāf chapter, and all distinct authorial commentary in Futūḥ al-Ghayb XVI, 606–612. Repeated Kashshāf base text is represented in its own layer below. Modern editorial footnotes are not part of the authorial translation. Base sources: K-DKA IV, 808–809; T-Dubai XVI, 606–612, through the working transcriptions. A complete first draft; final critical review remains open. Conflicting positions and transmitted reports are preserved as the authors present them, without independent authentication or endorsement.

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K109.01 — Heading and verses
Meccan; six verses. [K-DKA adds: revealed after al-Māʿūn.] This sūrah and Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ are called al-Muqashqishatān: the two that clear one of hypocrisy.
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.
“Say: O unbelievers! I do not worship what you worship, nor are you worshippers of what I worship. Nor am I a worshipper of what you have worshipped, nor are you worshippers of what I worship. To you your religion, and to me my religion.”

K109.02 — Those addressed and the occasion of revelation
Those addressed are particular unbelievers whom God knew would not believe. It is reported that a group of Quraysh said: “Muḥammad, come: follow our religion and we will follow yours. You worship our gods for a year and we will worship your God for a year.” He replied: “God forbid that I associate anything else with God!” They said: “Then touch some of our gods in reverence; we will believe you and worship your God.” The sūrah was then revealed. In the morning he went to the Sacred Mosque, where the leaders of Quraysh were gathered, stood before them, and recited it to them. They lost hope.

K109.03 — Future and past; the use of mā
“I do not worship” intends worship in the future, because lā is used only with an imperfect verb having a future meaning, just as mā is used only with an imperfect having a present meaning. Do you not see that lan emphatically negates what lā negates? Al-Khalīl said that lan originally consists of lā an. The meaning is: in the future I will not do what you ask of me, namely worship your gods; nor will you do in the future what I ask of you, namely worship my God.
“Nor am I a worshipper of what you have worshipped” means: at no time in the past was I a worshipper of what you worshipped then. You never knew me to worship an idol in the age of ignorance; how, then, can this be expected of me in Islam? “Nor are you worshippers of what I worship” means: at no time have you worshipped that which I now worship.
If you ask why He did not say “what I worshipped” (mā ʿabadtu), as He said “what you worshipped” (mā ʿabadtum), I answer: they used to worship idols before the prophetic mission, whereas he was not worshipping God at that time. [Ṭībī's sustained objection to this statement is translated below.]
If you ask why mā, “what,” is used rather than man, “who,” I answer: the intended consideration is the description, as though He said, “I do not worship the false, and you do not worship the true.” It is also said that mā forms a verbal noun: I do not perform your worship, and you do not perform my worship.

K109.04 — “To you your religion” and the closing report
“To you your religion, and to me my religion”: to you your association of partners with God, and to me my affirmation of His oneness. The meaning is: I am a prophet sent to you to call you to truth and salvation. If you will not accept what I bring or follow me, leave me alone without injury and do not invite me to associate partners with God.
God's Messenger is reported to have said: “Whoever recites Sūrat al-Kāfirūn, it is as though he recited a quarter of the Qurʾān. The rebellious devils withdraw far from him, he is cleared of associating partners with God, and he is spared the greatest terror.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T109.01 — Grammar of the proposed bargain; istilām (XVI, 606–607)
On “and we will follow”: according to one explanation, this is coordinated with the syntactic position of “then follow,” because if that expression were an imperfect verb, it would be jussive as the response to “come” (halumma). “We will worship,” and what follows, explains the proposal.
On “then touch in reverence” (fa-stalim): that is, kiss. One says istalama al-ḥajara, meaning that he touched the Stone with his hand; the expression was then extended to every kind of contact.

T109.02 — Why “what I worship” rather than “what I worshipped”? (607–608)
On “why did He not say…?”: “Nor are you worshippers of what I worship” is paired with “nor am I a worshipper of what you have worshipped.” Why, then, is the second altered to “what I worship,” when “what I worshipped” would appear to be expected, corresponding to “what you worshipped” in the first?
On “he was not worshipping God at that time”: al-Intiṣāf says: “This statement is mistaken both in its underlying principle and in its consequence. As for the principle, the Qadarī holds that before his mission the Prophet was not following the religion of a previous prophet, because that would be a blemish in his standing and discourage people from following him. They also hold that all people are obliged, by the requirements of reason, to reflect upon God's signs and the proofs of His oneness and knowledge of Him, and that reflection is obligatory through reason rather than revealed instruction. That is worship before the mission, and they must not suppose that he failed to perform it. Their own principle thus entails that he worshipped God before his mission.
“Al-Zamakhsharī preserved his principle concerning the absence of adherence to an earlier prophet, but failed to draw the consequence of his other principle: the rational obligation of worship. The truth is that he worshipped before revelation and devoted himself to religious observance in the cave of Ḥirāʾ. If the use of ‘I worship’ is because the worship intended by the verse had not occurred in the past, the matter concerning ‘what I worshipped’ should be understood as the full body of actual worship known only through revealed law, not the mere affirmation of God's oneness and knowledge of Him, which were always established for him before the mission.
“As for its imperfect form, this vividly presents his worship to the hearer's mind and fixes it there. Compare: ‘Have you not seen that God sent down water from the sky, and the earth becomes green?’ (22:63). The expected form is ‘became’; it is altered for the reason just stated.”
I say: it may be understood as continuity in both past and future, as indicated by the parallel expressions. Compare: “Those who recite God's Book and established prayer” (35:29), where a past verb is coordinated with an imperfect. The correct position is that before the mission he practiced worship according to a revealed law.

T109.03 — Earlier revealed law before and after the mission (608–609)
Ibn al-Jawzī transmits in al-Wafāʾ from Imām Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal: “To say that God's Messenger followed his people's religion is an evil statement. Did he not refrain from eating what was slaughtered on the sacrificial stones?” Abū al-Wafāʾ ʿAlī ibn ʿAqīl said: “Before his mission God's Messenger practiced as religion whatever he knew to be soundly established of Abraham's revealed law. After his mission, did he practice according to the law of those before him? There are two transmitted positions.
“The first is that he practiced whatever was soundly established of the laws of those before him through revelation to him, not through their own accounts, transmissions, or revealed books. Abū al-Ḥasan al-Tamīmī preferred this position; it is also the position of Abū Ḥanīfa's followers.
“The second is that he was bound by none of the earlier laws, but only by what was revealed to him of his own law. This is the position of the Muʿtazila and the Ashʿarīs. Al-Shāfiʿī's followers have two views corresponding to these two transmitted positions.
“Those who say he practiced according to an earlier law differ over which law it was. Some say Moses' law, apart from what was abrogated in our law. The apparent sense of Aḥmad's words is that he practiced everything soundly established as the law of an earlier prophet, unless its abrogation was established, because God says: ‘Those are the ones God guided; follow, then, their guidance’ (6:90).
“Ibn Qutayba said: The Arabs continued to observe remnants of Ishmael's religion. These included pilgrimage to the House, circumcision, making divorce final when it reached three, the husband's right to take his wife back after one or two divorces, a blood-money payment of one hundred camels for a life, washing after major ritual impurity, and the prohibition of marriage to those forbidden through blood relationship or marriage affinity. God's Messenger thus shared their belief in God and their practice of these religious ordinances. As for ‘You did not know what the Book was, nor faith’ (42:52), this means the ordinances of faith, not faith in the sense of acknowledging God.” Here ends Ibn al-Jawzī's discussion.

T109.04 — Ṭībī's objection to the distribution of past and future (609)
I say: the author's purpose in venturing this objectionable statement was to remove repetition from the discourse by distinguishing the two times, future and past. He made the first pair refer to the future and the latter pair to the past; hence the question arose against him. The sounder explanation is that the discourse does not concern God's Messenger's past worship or what he worshipped in earlier times. It concerns the future, as the occasion of revelation attests. “What I worship” therefore remains in its apparent sense. As for the past form “what you worshipped,” it intensifies his repudiation of them and their worship, and is thus understood beyond the immediately apparent temporal sense.

T109.05 — Four explanations that avoid repetition (610)
The Imām [Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī] says: “There are two positions concerning the passage. The first is that it contains no repetition, and this has several explanations.
“First: the opening pair concerns the future, since lā enters only upon an imperfect verb with future meaning: in the future I will not do what you ask of me, worshipping your gods; nor will you do in the future what I ask of you, worshipping my God. Then He says, ‘Nor am I a worshipper of what you worshipped’: I am not at present worshipping your objects of worship, nor are you at present worshipping my object of worship.
“Second: reverse this, making the first pair concern the present and the second the future. This is the position of al-Zajjāj, al-Wāḥidī, and Muḥyī al-Sunna. Al-Wāḥidī says: ‘Mā is used instead of man to correspond to “what you worship,” making the second expression conform to the first.’ Al-Zajjāj and Muḥyī al-Sunna say: ‘This addresses those whom God already knew would not believe.’
“Third: Abū Muslim's position. The first two statements concern the object of worship, and mā means ‘that which’: I do not worship idols, and you do not worship God. In the latter two, mā forms a verbal noun: I do not worship with a worship like yours, founded on doubt; nor do you worship with a worship like mine, founded on certainty.
“Fourth: understand the first pair as denying worship on the particular consideration they proposed, and the second as a general denial in every respect. I do not worship what you worship in the hope that you will worship God, nor do you worship in the hope that I will worship your idol. He then says: I will not worship your idol for any purpose whatsoever, in any manner whatsoever; similarly, you do not worship God for any purpose. It is like someone inviting another to commit injustice to obtain pleasure, who replies: ‘I do not commit injustice for pleasure; indeed, I do not commit injustice at all, whether for pleasure or anything else.’”

T109.06 — Two explanations accepting repetition; Ṭībī's preference (611)
“The second position accepts that repetition occurs, for two reasons. First, repetition conveys emphasis, and the greater the need for emphasis, the more fitting the repetition. No situation needs emphasis more than this one: they returned to him repeatedly with this proposal and entertained hopes because they saw his eagerness for their faith.”
Muḥyī al-Sunna says: “Most scholars say that the Qurʾān was revealed in the Arabs' language and according to their ways of addressing one another. Their usages include repetition for emphasis and explanation, just as they include abbreviation for lightness and concision.”
I say: this is the explanation we have chosen because it fits the situation. Next in preference is the fourth explanation under the first position.
“Second: they stated the proposal twice: you worship our gods for a month and we will worship your God for a month; you worship our gods for a year and we will worship your God for a year. The response therefore repeats itself in conformity with their speech. There is a kind of mockery in this: someone who repeats the same statement for a corrupt purpose is answered with repeated rejection of that statement, in disdain.” Muḥyī al-Sunna transmits this explanation more briefly from al-Qutaybī.

T109.07 — Kafāf; the disagreement over abrogation (611–612)
On “leave me alone without injury” (fa-daʿūnī kafāfan): al-Nihāya says: “Kafāf is what does not exceed a thing and is proportionate to the need for it. It is accusative as a circumstantial expression. It is also said that the meaning is: with their harm held back from me. Or: you do not injure me, and I do not injure you—you refrain from me, and I refrain from you.”
On this understanding, “To you your religion, and to me my religion” conveys mutual disengagement, each party allowing the other to remain upon its religion; it would then be abrogated by the verse commanding fighting.
The Qāḍī says: “‘To me my religion’ means the religion I follow and will not abandon. It neither grants permission for unbelief nor prohibits fighting, and therefore is not abrogated.” Dīn has also been explained as reckoning, recompense, supplication, and worship.

T109.08 — The quarter of the Qurʾān (612)
On “it is as though he recited a quarter of the Qurʾān”: we have transmitted from al-Tirmidhī, from Ibn ʿAbbās and Anas, that God's Messenger said: “Whoever recites ‘Say: O unbelievers,’ it is equivalent for him to a quarter of the Qurʾān.”
The sūrah is complete.

AL-NAṢR (110) — DIVINE HELP
Complete first-pass translation of both authorial layers • C05 • 27 October 2026

SOURCE AND STATUS
Kashshāf: Dār al-Kitāb al-ʿArabī, 1407 AH, IV:810–813, OpenITI Shamela0023627-ara1. Ṭībī: Futūḥ al-Ghayb, Dubai, 1434/2013, XVI:613–621, OpenITI Sham19Y0020891-ara1, with the missing Qurʾānic quotation restored from the scan of printed XVI:615. Both layers are translated through the closing report and final gloss. Modern editorial notes are excluded. Historical and transmitted assertions remain attributed to the author concerned; conflicting chronologies are not silently harmonised. Further review remains necessary.

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K110.01 — Heading and verses [IV:810]
Revealed at Minā during the Farewell Pilgrimage, it is counted as Medinan. It is the last sūrah revealed, and has three verses. [The DKA heading also places it after al-Tawba.]
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

When God’s help and the conquest come, (1) and you see people entering God’s religion in multitudes, (2) glorify your Lord with praise and ask His forgiveness: He has ever turned mercifully toward the repentant. (3)

K110.02 — Help and conquest [IV:810]
“When” (idhā) is accusative through sabbiḥ, “glorify,” and concerns the future. Announcing this before its occurrence is one of the signs of prophethood. It is reported that the sūrah was revealed during the days of tashrīq at Minā in the Farewell Pilgrimage.

If you ask how help differs from conquest, so that the latter is conjoined to the former, I reply: naṣr is succour and giving mastery over an enemy. Hence naṣara llāhu l-arḍa means God watered the earth with rain. Fatḥ is the conquest of countries. The meaning is help for God’s Messenger against the Arabs, or against Quraysh, and the conquest of Mecca. It is also said to mean God’s help for believers generally and His opening the polytheists’ countries to them.

Mecca was conquered ten days into Ramaḍān in the eighth year. With God’s Messenger were ten thousand Emigrants, Helpers, and members of Arab tribes. He stayed fifteen nights, then went out against Hawāzin. On entering Mecca he stood at the Kaʿba’s door and said: “There is no god but God alone, without partner. He fulfilled His promise, helped His servant, and routed the confederates alone.” Then: “People of Mecca, what do you think I shall do with you?” They replied, “Good: you are a noble brother and son of a noble brother.” He said, “Go: you are released.” God’s Messenger thus freed them, although God had forcibly delivered their persons into his power and they were his booty. Therefore Mecca’s people were called “the released.” They then pledged allegiance to him in Islam.

K110.03 — Entering in multitudes [IV:810–811]
“Into God’s religion”: into the religion of Islam, for no other religion is attributed to Him: “Whoever seeks a religion other than Islam, it will not be accepted from him” [3:85]. “In multitudes”: dense groups. An entire tribe would enter after people had previously entered singly or in pairs.

Jābir ibn ʿAbd Allāh once wept. Asked why, he said, “I heard God’s Messenger say: ‘People entered God’s religion in multitudes and will leave it in multitudes.’” It is also said that “people” means the Yemenis. Abū Hurayra says: when the sūrah was revealed, God’s Messenger said, “God is greatest! God’s help and the conquest have come, and the people of Yemen have come, a people tender of heart. Faith is Yemeni, understanding is Yemeni, and wisdom is Yemeni.” He also said, “I find your Lord’s relief coming from the direction of Yemen.” [The DKA transcription has nafīr; Ṭībī’s base and gloss have nafas.]

Al-Ḥasan says: when God’s Messenger conquered Mecca, the Arabs turned to one another and said, “Now that he has overcome the people of the sanctuary, we have no strength against him. God had protected them from the People of the Elephant and everyone who intended to attack them.” So they entered Islam in multitudes without fighting.

Ibn ʿAbbās read “God’s conquest and help.” Yudkhalūna, “are made to enter,” is also read in the passive. If you ask the grammatical position of “entering,” I reply: accusative, either as a circumstantial clause, with raʾayta meaning “saw” or “recognised,” or as its second object, with raʾayta meaning “knew.”

K110.04 — Glorification and asking forgiveness [IV:811–812]
“Glorify your Lord with praise”: say “Glory be to God,” praising Him. That is: marvel that God has made possible what never occurred to you or anyone else—that someone should overcome the people of the sanctuary—and praise Him for His action. Or remember Him, glorifying and praising, increasing your worship and praise as His beneficence to you increases. Or perform prayer for Him. Umm Hāniʾ relates that when the Kaʿba’s door was opened, he prayed eight units of the forenoon prayer. ʿĀʾisha relates that before his death he frequently said, “Glory be to You, O God, and praise be Yours; I ask Your forgiveness and turn to You.”

The command to seek forgiveness alongside glorification completes the command concerning religion’s foundation: combining obedience with guarding against disobedience. His being commanded thus, despite his protection from sin, is a gracious aid to his community. Asking forgiveness also entails humility before God and abasement of the self, and is therefore worship in itself. From the Prophet: “I seek forgiveness a hundred times during a day and night.”

K110.05 — The announcement of his departure [IV:812]
It is related that when God’s Messenger recited the sūrah to his Companions, they rejoiced, but al-ʿAbbās wept. “What makes you weep, uncle?” he asked. “Your death has been announced to you.” He replied, “It is as you say.” He then lived two years, during which he was not seen laughing in gladness. It is also said that Ibn ʿAbbās made this remark, and God’s Messenger said, “This boy has been given much knowledge.”

It is related that when the sūrah was revealed, God’s Messenger preached: “God gave a servant a choice between this world and meeting Him, and he chose meeting God.” Abū Bakr understood and said, “May we ransom you with ourselves, our property, our fathers and our children.”

From Ibn ʿAbbās: ʿUmar used to bring him close and admit him with the veterans of Badr. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān asked, “Do you admit this youth with us, when among our sons are his equals?” He replied, “He is one whose standing you know.” Ibn ʿAbbās says: one day he admitted them, and me with them, and asked about “When God’s help comes.” I think he asked only because of me. Some said, “God commands His Prophet, when He grants him conquest, to ask forgiveness and repent.” I said, “Rather, his death has been announced to him.” ʿUmar said, “I understand it only as you do,” then, “How can you blame me concerning him after what you have seen?”

The Prophet called Fāṭima and said, “Daughter, my death has been announced to me.” She wept. He said, “Do not weep: you will be the first of my household to join me.” Ibn Masʿūd says this sūrah is called the Sūrah of Farewell.

“He has ever turned mercifully”: in past ages, ever since creating morally responsible beings, He has turned toward them when they sought forgiveness. Every seeker of forgiveness should therefore expect the same.

K110.06 — Closing report [IV:813]
From God’s Messenger: “Whoever recites the sūrah ‘When God’s help comes’ receives the reward of someone who was present with Muḥammad on the day Mecca was conquered.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T110.01 — A chronological objection [XVI:613–614]
Medinan; three verses. In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

On “or against Quraysh, and the conquest of Mecca,” the Qāḍī [al-Bayḍāwī] says: “It is said to mean God’s help generally and the conquest of Mecca and other countries for them. What is obtained is figuratively described as coming, suggesting that decreed events proceed from eternity toward their appointed times, approaching gradually. That is: help has drawn near its time, so await its arrival and prepare to give thanks.”

I say: this and the author’s statement require examination, because Mecca’s conquest preceded the sūrah’s revelation. We transmit from Muslim, from ʿUbayd Allāh ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUtba: Ibn ʿAbbās asked me, “Do you know the last complete sūrah revealed?” I said, “Yes: ‘When God’s help and the conquest come.’” He said, “Correct.” The author’s own words indicate this: he dates Mecca’s conquest to ten days into Ramaḍān of year eight, whereas it is said that the sūrah descended during the days of tashrīq at Minā in the Farewell Pilgrimage. That pilgrimage was in year ten: he remained nine years without performing pilgrimage, then was permitted it in the tenth.

T110.02 — Jābir’s report and Yemeni faith [XVI:614–616]
On Jābir’s weeping: Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal transmitted this report from him; al-Dārimī transmitted it from Abū Hurayra.

On “faith is Yemeni”: al-Bukhārī, Muslim and al-Tirmidhī transmit from Abū Hurayra that God’s Messenger said, “The people of Yemen have come to you: they are more tender in feeling and softer of heart. Faith is Yemeni, and wisdom is Yemeni.” Another transmission has “understanding is Yemeni.”

Al-Nihāya says: “He said this because faith began in Mecca, which belongs to Tihāma, and Tihāma is part of Yemen; hence the expression ‘the Yemeni Kaʿba.’ It is also said that he spoke at Tabūk, with Mecca and Medina then between him and Yemen: he pointed toward Yemen, intending Mecca and Medina. Or he meant the Helpers, who were Yemenis: they supported faith and the believers and sheltered them, so faith was ascribed to them,” as in “those who had established themselves in the abode and in faith” [59:9]. According to another authority, wisdom means the Sunna and religious understanding, as in “and teaches them the Book and wisdom” [62:2].

“Understanding is Yemeni” is also transmitted. This praises the Yemenis for hastening to faith and accepting it well. I say: perhaps “understanding” (fiqh) means what al-Ḥasan meant in the report we transmit from al-Dārimī, from ʿImrān. I once said to al-Ḥasan about something he said, “Abū Saʿīd, that is not what the jurists say.” He replied, “Woe to you! Have you ever seen a person of understanding? Such a person renounces attachment to this world, desires the hereafter, sees clearly in his religion, and perseveres in worshipping his Lord.”

T110.03 — Relief from Yemen and the Arabs’ words [XVI:616]
On “I find your Lord’s nafas from Yemen,” al-Nihāya says: “Nafas is borrowed from the air drawn inward by breathing, which cools and moderates the inward heat; or from the breath of wind one inhales and finds refreshing; or from a garden’s breath, its pleasant scents, through which distress is relieved. One says, ‘You have breathing space in your affair,’ and ‘Act while you have breathing space in your life’: breadth and room.”

On ammā idh ẓafira, “now that he has prevailed”: ammā is transmitted both lightened and doubled. The doubled form is preferable, for ammā distinguishes cases: before he prevailed over the sanctuary’s people we hoped to overcome him; now that he has prevailed we have no strength against him.

T110.04 — Wonder or worship? [XVI:617–618]
On “say ‘Glory be to God,’ praising Him; that is, marvel”: the bāʾ in “with your Lord’s praise” introduces a circumstance. Say the glorification while engaged in praise. On this interpretation the mention of glorification does not aim at remembrance as such. He says: “Originally God is glorified upon seeing something wondrous among His works; usage then broadened until it was applied to anything that occasions wonder.” Al-Intiṣāf says: “The command here has the meaning of a statement: an imperative in an expression of wonder is not intended as a command. The point is that this event is such as to arouse wonder.”

On “or remember Him, glorifying and praising”: on this interpretation glorification implies remembrance by semantic inclusion (taḍmīn), which is why he expresses glorification as a circumstance. “With your Lord’s praise” is a circumstance nested within that circumstance, since the incorporated meaning is most often expressed circumstantially. The Qāḍī says: “Extol God through the attributes of majesty while praising Him for the attributes of beneficence.”

I say: this interpretation is preferable and more coherent. In Sūrat al-Fatḥ it was explained that God made Mecca’s conquest a ground for forgiveness because it occasioned the command that His Messenger attend to his own particular concern after expending his utmost effort in conveying the message and struggling against religion’s enemies: turning to worship and piety and preparing to travel to the exalted stations and join the Highest Companion. His saying “God gave a servant a choice between this world and meeting Him, and he chose meeting God” points to this. Hence his uncle al-ʿAbbās wept when the sūrah was recited and said, “Your death has been announced to you.”

This is also what his cousin, the learned authority of the community, understood when he answered those elders, saying that the Prophet’s death had been announced; ʿUmar confirmed him.

T110.05 — Connecting passages by meaning [XVI:618–619]
Muḥyī l-Sunna transmits from Muḥammad ibn Jarīr that “so that God may forgive your earlier and later sin” [48:2] refers back to “When God’s help and the conquest come” through “and ask His forgiveness”: ask forgiveness so that God may forgive you. What is meant is that this explanation of purpose depends on something understood after “We have granted you a manifest conquest”: “[so glorify your Lord with praise and ask His forgiveness,] so that He may forgive you.” Both sūrahs refer to one event and one situation. It does not mean that “so that God may forgive you” is directly governed by that very wording “ask His forgiveness.” That would disrupt the miraculous composition, beyond human powers and capacities. How could it, when “We have granted you” was revealed before Mecca’s conquest, after the Messenger’s return from al-Ḥudaybiya, while Sūrat al-Naṣr was revealed two years after the conquest? In Sūrat Hūd we have already set out a rule for bringing together portions of one narrative occurring in different contexts and forms.

If you ask: the narrative’s unity indicates this implied expression, but what of Muḥyī l-Sunna’s further transmission from al-Ḥusayn ibn al-Faḍl, that “so that God may forgive you” refers back to “ask forgiveness for your sin and for believing men and women” [47:19]—ask forgiveness so that God may forgive you and “admit believing men and women to gardens beneath which rivers flow” [48:5]?

I reply: this strengthens our preference for a connection in meaning. If you make it a direct verbal connection, you find yourself in a trackless wilderness, stumbling blindly. Notice how he joins “so that He may admit the believers” to “so that God may forgive you.” The former explains “He sent tranquillity into the believers’ hearts” [48:4], itself explained by “so that they might increase in faith”; “and punish the hypocritical men and women” is conjoined to it. As the author says: “Among its consequences is that He calmed the believers’ hearts,” continuing through “so that they deserve reward, and He rewards them and punishes the unbelievers and hypocrites.”

This accords with what we transmit from Muslim and al-Tirmidhī, from Anas. When “We have granted you a manifest conquest” through “a mighty triumph” [48:1–5] was revealed on the return from al-Ḥudaybiya, they were affected by sorrow and dejection, and he had slaughtered the sacrificial animals there. God’s Messenger said, “A verse has been revealed to me that is dearer to me than the entire world.” Al-Tirmidhī’s version adds: they said, “May it bring you joy and benefit, Messenger of God! God has made clear what He will do with you; what will He do with us?” Then “so that He may admit believing men and women to gardens beneath which rivers flow” was revealed.

Perhaps the speaker considered that if God’s Messenger asks forgiveness for his sin and the believers’ sin, God must forgive him and answer his prayer for his community, as in: “Had they, after wronging themselves, come to you and asked God’s forgiveness, and the Messenger asked forgiveness for them, they would have found God ever relenting, merciful” [4:64]. He therefore connected the passages in meaning. For this subtle reason he preferred “refers back” and “is referred back” to “is grammatically dependent.” God knows best.

T110.06 — Completing obedience with caution [XVI:620]
On “eight units of the forenoon prayer”: we transmit the report in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī. On “he frequently said before his death”: al-Bukhārī and Muslim transmitted it.

On “the command to ask forgiveness alongside glorification completes”: in the rhetorical discipline, completion (takmīl) consists in bringing a statement that appears incomplete, then completing it with another. Here glorification commands obedience; acts of obedience are incomplete without guarding against sins. The Qāḍī says: “Ask His forgiveness, humbling yourself and regarding your deeds as insufficient, and remedying what escaped you through attending to another. It is also said: ask forgiveness for your community. Putting glorification, then praise, before seeking forgiveness follows the descent from the Creator to creation.”

On “I seek forgiveness a hundred times during a day [and night]”: al-Bukhārī and al-Tirmidhī transmitted it from Abū Hurayra.

T110.07 — Ibn ʿAbbās and Fāṭima [XVI:621]
On Ibn ʿAbbās’s report that ʿUmar brought him close: Imām Aḥmad, al-Bukhārī and al-Tirmidhī transmitted it. “Brought him close” means gave him precedence, treated him on a level with the elders, and permitted him to enter his presence.

On his calling Fāṭima: this is an abridgment of al-Dārimī’s transmission from Ibn ʿAbbās.

End of the sūrah.

AL-MASAD / TABBAT (111) — THE TWISTED ROPE
Complete first-pass translation of both authorial layers • C05 • 27 October 2026

SOURCE AND STATUS
Kashshāf: DKA, 1407 AH, IV:813–817, OpenITI Shamela0023627-ara1. Ṭībī: Dubai, 1434/2013, XVI:622–631, OpenITI Sham19Y0020891-ara1. Both authorial layers are translated in full through their endings. Editorial footnotes are excluded; transmitted assertions retain their attribution. First draft, not final review. The scan of printed XVI:629 was consulted for the camel-hide verse and gloss.

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K111.01 — Heading, verses, and destruction [IV:813]
Meccan; five verses. [The DKA heading places it after al-Fātiḥa.]
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

May Abū Lahab’s hands perish—and perish he did! (1) His wealth and what he earned have availed him nothing. (2) He shall burn in a flaming fire, (3) and his wife, the carrier of firewood, (4) with a rope of twisted fibre about her neck. (5)

Tabāb means destruction. Hence their question, “Is she young, or tābba?”—worn out by decrepitude and old age. His hands perish because, according to a report, he picked up a stone to throw at God’s Messenger; “and he perished” means his whole person perished. Or his hands are declared ruined with the destruction of his entire person intended, as in “because of what your two hands sent ahead” [22:10]. “And he perished” then means this occurred and was accomplished, as in:
“He requited me—may God requite him with the worst requital—
with the requital of howling dogs; and He has done so.”

K111.02 — The warning on al-Ṣafā and his byname [IV:814]
Ibn Masʿūd’s reading “and he has indeed perished” supports this. It is related that when “Warn your nearest kinsfolk” [26:214] was revealed, he climbed al-Ṣafā and cried, “Alarm!” People gathered from every direction. He said, “Sons of ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, sons of Fihr: if I told you there was cavalry at this mountain’s foot, would you believe me?” “Yes.” “Then I warn you before the Hour.” Abū Lahab said, “Ruin to you! Is this why you summoned us?” The sūrah was revealed.

If you ask why he is given his kunya, when this is an honour, I reply: there are three explanations. First, he may have been known by his kunya rather than his name. A man may be known by one rather than the other; hence either may follow the other as explanatory apposition. Since the intention was to make him notorious by a curse that would remain his mark, the better-known designation was used. This is supported by the reading yadā Abū Lahab, like “ʿAlī ibn Abū Ṭālib” and “Muʿāwiya ibn Abū Sufyān”: nothing is altered in the familiar name, lest the listener become confused. Fulayta ibn Qāsim, Mecca’s ruler, had two sons, one called ʿAbdi-llāh with the genitive, the other ʿAbda-llāh with the accusative. There was a man in Mecca known only as ʿAbdi-llāh, with kasra on the dāl.

Second, his name was ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā, so the kunya was used instead. Third, he belonged to the people of the Fire and would end in a flaming fire, so his condition suited his kunya, “Father of Flame,” making it fitting. One calls someone Abū Lahab as one calls a wicked person “Father of Evil” and a good person “Father of Good,” or as the Messenger called al-Muhallab’s father Abū Ṣufra because of yellow colouring in his face. It is also said that his cheeks glowed and shone, which gave him the name; using it may therefore mock both him and his pride in it.

Abī Lahb is also read with quiescent hāʾ: an alteration peculiar to proper names, like Shums ibn Mālik with ḍamma.

K111.03 — Wealth and earnings [IV:814–815]
Mā aghnā is either an interrogative expressing denial, with mā accusative, or a negation. Wa-mā kasaba is nominative; mā is either relative, “what he earned,” or converts the clause into a verbal noun, “his earning.” His wealth and what he earned through it did not benefit him: capital and profits; or livestock and their offspring and benefits—he owned abundant breeding stock; or inherited wealth and what he acquired himself; or long-held and newly acquired property.

Ibn ʿAbbās explains “what he earned” as his children. Abū Lahab’s sons are said to have brought a dispute before him and begun fighting. He rose to separate them; one pushed him and he fell. Angry, he said, “Take this foul earning away from me!” Hence the Prophet’s saying, “The best of what a man eats is from his earning, and his child is from his earning.” Al-Ḍaḥḥāk explains it as his wealth and evil action, meaning his schemes of hostility against the Messenger. Qatāda takes it as the deeds he thought amounted to something, as in “We turned to whatever deeds they had done” [25:23]. It is related that he said, “If my nephew’s claims are true, I shall ransom myself from it with my wealth and children.”

K111.04 — His wife and the firewood [IV:815]
Sa-yaṣlā is read with fatḥa on yāʾ and also with ḍamma, in both lightened and doubled forms. Sīn expresses threat: it will inevitably occur, however delayed. His wife is Umm Jamīl daughter of Ḥarb, Abū Sufyān’s sister. She carried bundles of thorns, prickles and saʿdān and scattered them at night on the Messenger’s path. Or she went about carrying tales. A malicious gossip who corrupts relations is said to “carry firewood among them”: he kindles their hostility and causes evil. The poet says:
“A fair woman never found upon a blameworthy course,
nor walking among the tribe with damp firewood.”
The wood is called damp to suggest smoke, an additional evil.

“His wife” is nominative, conjoined to the pronoun in sa-yaṣlā: he and his wife will burn; “about her neck” then expresses a circumstance. Or “his wife” is the subject and “about her neck” its predicate. “Carrier of firewood” is also read accusative as abuse. I prefer this reading: whoever loves reviling Umm Jamīl has approached the Messenger through something fine (jamīl). Ḥammālatun li-l-ḥaṭab and ḥammālatan li-l-ḥaṭab are also read, with tanwīn and nominative or accusative. Wa-murayʾatuhu, “and his little wife,” is read as a diminutive.

K111.05 — The rope, the taunt, and punishment [IV:815–817]
Masad is tightly twisted cord, whether palm fibre, leather, or another material. The poet says, “And a rope tightly twisted from she-camels.” A man mamsūd in build is firmly knit. Thus she has a twisted rope about her neck, carrying and tying up the thorns like wood-gatherers. This debases and belittles her, depicting her as one of the serving-women who gather wood, to anger both her and her husband despite their house of honour and their wealth and prosperity.

Someone taunted al-Faḍl ibn al-ʿAbbās ibn ʿUtba ibn Abī Lahab with “the carrier of firewood.” He replied:
“Why have you turned to insulting and diminishing me?
What reproach is there in the carrier of firewood?
A radiant woman, whose broad blaze shone in glory,
offspring of an elder of profound ancestral distinction.”

Or the meaning is that in Hell she will appear as she did when carrying the thorn-bundle: always bearing a bundle of hellfire wood from zaqqūm or ḍarīʿ, with a rope twisted from the Fire’s chains about her neck. Every criminal is punished in a manner corresponding to his crime.

From God’s Messenger: “Whoever recites Sūrat Tabbat, I hope God will not place him and Abū Lahab together in one abode.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T111.01 — Loss and old age [XVI:622]
Meccan; five verses. In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

On “tabāb means destruction,” al-Rāghib says: “Tabb and tabāb mean persistence in loss. One says tabban lahu and tabba lahu; tabbabtuhu means I said that to him. Because persistence is included, istatabba li-fulān means something became established for him. ‘Abū Lahab’s hands perished’ means they persisted in loss; ‘they added only destruction to them’ [11:101] means causing loss.” On “becoming aged”: ʿajizat and ʿajjazat describe a woman becoming old, just as tathayyabat means becoming previously married.

T111.02 — Hands, the whole person, and certainty [XVI:623–624]
On “his entire person is meant,” compare:
“A man whose hands begrudge another man
a benefit from someone else is surely miserly.”
That is, he begrudges him. Al-Jawharī: “‘This is what your hands have committed’ means what you committed.”

On “‘and he perished’: it happened”: according to one explanation tabba is an imprecation in the first interpretation and a statement in the second; tabbat is an imprecation in either. The Imām says the first can mean the destruction of his deeds, the second of himself: a man strives for his own welfare and that of his work, and God declares him deprived of both.

I say: the composition supports this. What follows explains it: “His wealth and earnings availed him nothing” points to his deeds’ destruction, and “He shall burn in a flaming fire” to his own. The past tense first signals certainty, as God’s announcements of future events do; the future tense second depicts the coming state before the listener’s eyes. Ibn Masʿūd’s “he has indeed perished” supports this, since qad confirms, as in the poet’s “and He has done so.” The poem means: he requited me like howling dogs—“running dogs” is also transmitted—may God give him His worst requital, and He has already done it.

T111.03 — The cry of warning [XVI:624]
The report upon “Warn your nearest kinsfolk” [26:214] is transmitted by al-Bukhārī, Muslim, Imām Aḥmad and al-Tirmidhī from Ibn ʿAbbās: the Prophet climbed al-Ṣafā, calling the branches of Quraysh, “Sons of Fihr! Sons of ʿAdī!” They gathered; anyone unable to come sent someone to discover the matter. Abū Lahab and Quraysh came. He said, “If I told you cavalry in the valley intended to raid you, would you believe me?” “Yes: we have experienced only truthfulness from you.” “Then I warn you before a severe punishment.” Abū Lahab said, “Ruin to you all day! Is this why you gathered us?” The sūrah descended.

Al-Nihāya explains yā ṣabāḥāh: “A cry for aid, originally the alarm for a raid, since raids commonly occurred in the morning: morning has come, so prepare.” Safḥ is the mountain’s lower slope, where water pours down.

T111.04 — Names, readings, and al-Muhallab [XVI:625–626]
On preserving the name unchanged, al-Intiṣāf says: “This indicates that the nominative precedes the other case forms. They preserve its familiar shape; the name became known thus. His name ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā was avoided in favour of his kunya because it was disliked.”

Fulayta has fatḥa on fāʾ and kasra on lām; Fukayta with kāf and the diminutive is also transmitted. The report that the Messenger named al-Muhallab’s father Abū Ṣufra is not mentioned in Jāmiʿ al-uṣūl. Al-Muhallab is Abū Saʿīd al-Muhallab ibn Abī Ṣufra; his father’s name was Ẓālim ibn Surrāq ibn Ṣubayḥ al-Azdī. Al-Muhallab fought the celebrated wars against the Khārijites and died in 83 at Marw al-Rūdh under ʿAbd al-Malik ibn Marwān. He belongs to the first generation of Basran Successors and saw ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb.

“It is also said he was so named” subdivides the third explanation; it is not a fourth. The kunya is preferred either because it uniquely identifies him and his name would confuse; or both identify him equally and the kunya was chosen though the name was possible; or for a subtle point: to signify that he belongs to Hell, either simply or with mockery. The author of al-Miftāḥ indicates the first explanation and the first version of the third.

Ibn Kathīr reads Lahb with quiescent hāʾ; the others with fatḥa. Abū l-Baqāʾ calls them two dialectal forms. Mā’s accusative position is as an absolute object: “What benefit?” Abū l-Baqāʾ gives the two interpretations and says it cannot mean “that which.” The author is reported to have said: māl is general; Bedouins use it of camels, while their landed notables use it of an estate.

T111.05 — Livestock, earnings, and the reading [XVI:627]
On sābiyāʾ [the transcription also writes sāyibāʾ], al-Nihāya says: “The offspring and abundance of livestock; one says a household has sābiyāʾ, plural sawābī. Originally it is the membrane from which the newborn emerges, or the afterbirth.” Some say it is a diptote naming the offspring. Tālid means long-held wealth, opposite to newly acquired ṭārif. Abū Dāwūd transmitted “the best of what a man eats” from ʿĀʾisha. The reading sa-yaṣlā with fatḥa is the well-known one; the reading with ḍamma is noncanonical.

T111.06 — The woman and damp wood [XVI:628]
“Never hunted” means never found: she is likened to an oryx and its description applied to her. Lāʾima means something blameworthy: she was never found pursuing a blameworthy quality. The poet praises her honour. Al-khaṭar al-raṭb is also transmitted: damp wood used to make an enclosure. The meaning is that she did not go among people carrying tales and instilling hostility.

On smoke as an additional evil: he did not merely make it wood, but damp wood, an intensifying completion (īghāl and tatmīm) for hyperbole. Imruʾ al-Qays says:
“I bore a Rudaynī spear whose point was like
the brilliance of a flame unmixed with smoke.”
ʿĀṣim reads ḥammālata l-ḥaṭab accusative; the others nominative.

T111.07 — The twisted camel-hide rope [XVI:629]
The verse “And a rope tightly twisted from she-camels” continues, according to al-Zajjāj:
“Reddish-white, choice, with marrow approaching maturity.”
Al-Muṭliʿ has “neither old she-camels nor young ones of the ḥiqqa age.” Umirra means twisted. Ayāniq is the plural of aynuq, itself a plural of nāqa: the cord is made from their hides. Ṣuhb qualifies ayāniq. An aṣhab camel has white mingled with red. Rāhiq is borrowed from a boy approaching maturity. Anyāb is the plural of nāb [an old she-camel]. The rope is made neither from a very young nor an old animal’s hide, but from a strong young adult’s.

On “firmly knit,” al-Jawharī gives “a girl of well-knit build.” Mawāhin is plural of māhina: mahna, with fatḥa on mīm, is service; māhin is a servant.

T111.08 — Anger, the poem, and final syntax [XVI:630–631]
Maʿiḍtu and imtaʿaḍtu mean I became angry and found something distressing. In “what did you intend toward insulting me,” aradta includes the meaning of turning toward, and therefore takes ilā. A shādikha is a horse’s broad white blaze extending from forelock to nose without touching the eyes, a feature of noble horses. “The elder” is taken as ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib—but this is not correct, since she is Ḥarb’s daughter and Abū Sufyān’s sister, as stated.

On the interpretation that her appearance in Hell matches her former appearance: “and his wife, carrier of firewood” is then a circumstantial clause referring to the pronoun in sa-yaṣlā, or “his wife” is conjoined to that pronoun. On the earlier interpretation it cannot be a circumstantial clause, but one sentence is conjoined to another. Abū l-Baqāʾ says: “Imraʾatuhu has two analyses: a subject with ḥammāla as predicate; or conjoined to the pronoun in sa-yaṣlā. On the latter, ḥammāla is either an adjective for the preceding noun or stands for ‘while she is a carrier.’”

End of the sūrah.

PART K — AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ'S AL-KASHSHĀF

[K112.01 | T XVI 632; K IV 817]
Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ. Meccan; it is also said to be Medinan. It has four verses.
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.
“Say: He is God, One. God, the One to whom all turn in need (al-Ṣamad). He neither begot nor was begotten, and no one is equal to Him.” [112:1–4]
[REVIEW K112-A: The separate four-volume transcription additionally prints “revealed after al-Nās” in its heading and repeats the basmala. These are absent from the corresponding Dubai base-text heading. Their authorial status is unestablished; they are recorded rather than silently inserted.]

[K112.02 | T XVI 632–633; K IV 817]
Huwa [He/it] is the pronoun of the matter (ḍamīr al-shaʾn), and “God is One” is that matter, as when you say, “It is: Zayd is departing.” It is as though one had said: the matter is this—namely, that God is one, with no second beside Him.
If you ask, “What is the syntactic position of huwa?”, I answer: nominative, as the subject (mubtadaʾ); its predicate is the sentence.
If you ask, “A sentence functioning as a predicate must contain something referring back to the subject. Where, then, is that reference?”, I answer: this sentence has the same status as the single predicate in your statement “Zayd is your servant-boy”: in meaning, it is the subject itself. For “God is One” is precisely the matter expressed by huwa. This is not the case with “Zayd—his father is departing,” since Zayd and the sentence denote two different meanings; something connecting them is therefore indispensable.
Ibn ʿAbbās reports: Quraysh said, “Muḥammad, describe to us your Lord, to whom you summon us,” and the sūrah was revealed. That is: the one whose description you have asked me for is God. Aḥad is a substitution (badal) for “God”; alternatively, [it is construed with an understood subject] “He is One.” It has the meaning of wāḥid, and its original form is waḥad.

[K112.03 | T XVI 634–635; K IV 817–818]
ʿAbd Allāh and Ubayy read “Huwa Allāhu aḥad,” without “Say.” In the Prophet's ﷺ reading it is “Allāhu aḥad,” without “Say: He”; and he said, “Whoever recites ‘Allāhu aḥad’—it is equivalent to the Qurʾān.” Al-Aʿmash read “Qul huwa Allāhu al-wāḥid.” It has also been read “aḥadu llāhu,” without tanwīn: the tanwīn is dropped because it meets the lām of the definite article. Comparable to this is:
“Nor remembering God, except a little.”
The sounder usage is to retain the tanwīn, giving it a kasra because of the meeting of two unvocalized consonants.
Al-Ṣamad is on the pattern faʿal with the meaning of a passive participle (mafʿūl), from ṣamada ilayhi, “he directed himself towards him.” It is the master to whom people turn with their needs.
[REVIEW K112-B: The report about equivalence is translated as given; Ṭībī's correction to “one third” follows in T112.12. Its presence here is not a fresh authentication.]

[K112.04 | T XVI 636; K IV 818]
The meaning is: He is the God whom you know, and whom you acknowledge as the creator of the heavens and the earth and your own creator. He is one, alone in divinity, with no partner in it. He is the one to whom every creature turns; they cannot dispense with Him, whereas He has no need of them.
“He did not beget,” because He shares no genus with anything, such that He might have a consort of His own genus and the two might produce offspring. His statement “How could He have a child, when He has no consort?” [6:101] indicates this meaning. “Nor was He begotten,” because everything begotten is originated and a body; He, however, is pre-eternal (qadīm), with no beginning to His existence, and is not a body. No one equals Him: that is, no one resembles Him or is like Him. It is also possible to take this from suitability between spouses (kafāʾa) in marriage, thereby denying Him a consort.

[K112.05 | T XVI 636, 638; K IV 818]
They asked him to describe Him to them, and what encompasses His attributes was revealed to him. “He is God” points them to the creator and originator of things. Implicit in that is His description as powerful and knowing, since creation requires power and knowledge, occurring as it does with the utmost precision, coherence and order. This also describes Him as living, hearing and seeing. “One” describes His oneness and denies partners. “Al-Ṣamad” describes Him as exclusively the one who is needed; if He is exclusively needed [and not needy], He is self-sufficient. His being self-sufficient together with His being knowing entails that He is just and does not perform evil acts: He knows the evil of the evil act, and knows that He has no need of it. “Nor was He begotten” describes pre-eternity and primordiality. “He did not beget” denies likeness and a shared genus. “No one is equal to Him” confirms this and settles the judgment definitively.

[K112.06 | T XVI 638–639; K IV 818–819]
If you ask, “In eloquent Arabic, an adjunct (ẓarf) that is supplementary (laghw), rather than indispensable to the predication (mustaqirr), is postponed, not placed first—Sībawayh explicitly says so in his book. Why, then, is it placed first in the most eloquent and most fully Arabic of speech?”, I answer: this utterance is directed precisely at denying equivalence to the essence of the Creator—glory be to Him. This adjunct [lahu, ‘to Him’] is where that meaning converges and is centred. It is therefore the most important and most pertinent element, the one most entitled and most suited to precedence.
Kufuʾan has been read with ḍamma on both kāf and fāʾ, and also with ḍamma or kasra on the kāf together with an unvocalized fāʾ [kufʾan, kifʾan].

[K112.07 | T XVI 640–642; K IV 819]
If you ask, “Why is this sūrah the equivalent of the whole Qurʾān, despite its shortness and the closeness of its two ends?”, I answer:
“It is for some great cause that whoever becomes a master becomes one.”
That is solely because it encompasses God's attributes, His justice and His oneness. For anyone who acknowledges its excellence and believes the Messenger of God's ﷺ statement about it, this is sufficient proof that the science of divine oneness (ʿilm al-tawḥīd) has an exalted standing with God. How could it be otherwise? Knowledge follows its object: it is ennobled by the nobility of what is known, and lowered by its lowliness. The object of this science is God Most High and His attributes, and what is and is not admissible concerning Him. What, then, do you think of the nobility of its rank, the grandeur of its position, its eminence over every other science, and its winning the prize ahead of them? Whoever disparages it does so because of the weakness of his knowledge of its object, the smallness of his reverence for Him, his lack of fear of Him, and his remoteness from considering his own ultimate fate.
O God, gather us among those who know You, act for You, affirm Your justice and Your oneness, and fear Your threat.
It is called “The Sūrah of the Foundation,” because it encompasses the foundations of religion. Ubayy and Anas transmitted from the Prophet ﷺ: “The seven heavens and the seven earths were founded upon ‘Say: He is God, One.’” That is: they were created solely to serve as signs of God's oneness and of the knowledge of His attributes expressed by this sūrah.
It is related from the Messenger of God ﷺ that he heard a man reciting “Say: He is God, One,” and said, “It has become assured.” He was asked, “Messenger of God, what has become assured?” He answered, “Paradise has become assured for him.”

PART T — AL-ṬĪBĪ'S ADDITIONAL COMMENTARY

[T112.01 | XVI 632–633 | on K112.02]
Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ. Meccan; it is also said to be Medinan. Four verses.
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.
His words, “The one whose description you asked me for is God, and aḥad is a substitution.” Abū al-Baqāʾ says: “Huwa is a subject meaning ‘the one asked about,’ for they said, ‘Is your Lord made of copper or gold?’ On this interpretation, Allāh may be the predicate of the subject, with aḥad a substitution, or the predicate of an omitted subject. Allāh may also be a substitution, with aḥad the predicate. The hamza of aḥad replaces wāw, since it means al-wāḥid.” Substitution of hamza for a wāw carrying fatḥa is rare. It is also said that the hamza is original, like the hamza in aḥad used for generality.

[T112.02 | XVI 633–634 | on K112.02]
His words, “It has the meaning of wāḥid.” There are two possibilities. First, this may attach to the second interpretation: huwa answers their request, “Describe your Lord to us,” and is the pronoun referring to the one asked about. In that case, a distinction must be drawn between wāḥid and aḥad. He says in al-Aḥzāb: “Aḥad originally means waḥad, that is, al-wāḥid; then it was assigned to general negation, applying equally to masculine and feminine, one and more than one.”
The author of al-Nihāya transmits from al-Azharī: “The difference between al-wāḥid and al-aḥad is that aḥad was formed to negate whatever number is mentioned with it: you say, ‘No one came to me.’ Wāḥid is a name formed for the beginning of number: you say, ‘One person came to me,’ but do not say [in this affirmative construction], ‘Aḥad came to me.’ Al-wāḥid is unique in essence, without likeness or counterpart; al-aḥad is unique in meaning. It is also said: al-wāḥid is that which is not divisible, is not doubled, does not admit division, and has neither counterpart nor likeness. Only God Most High combines these two descriptions.”
Al-Azharī says in Tafsīr asmāʾ Allāh al-ḥusnā: “Al-aḥad is among the attributes God has reserved to Himself: nothing shares it with Him, and nothing other than God is described as al-aḥad. One does not say ‘an aḥad man’ or ‘an aḥad dirham’; one says ‘a wāḥid man.’”
Knowing this, we say: when they said, “Describe to us your Lord, to whom you summon us,” they were told: the one asked about is God, one, unique in essence, without likeness or counterpart. The discourse thus serves to distinguish, and the description differentiates. Although it entails magnification, [when] huwa is taken as the pronoun of the matter, the descriptions serve magnification alone. For this is the beginning of the Messenger's ﷺ mission, guiding the people and drawing attention to an object of worship of immense dignity and overwhelming dominion. It is as though one had said: Say, Muḥammad, the matter and affair is that God is One, with no second beside Him. Allāh indicates all the attributes of perfection; aḥad, all the attributes of majesty. Accordingly, it is appropriate to say “One, with no second beside Him,” because aḥad indicates the negation of whatever is mentioned with it.
The second possibility is that [his explanation] attaches to both interpretations together: either huwa is the pronoun of the matter, or huwa means the one asked about. There would then be no distinction between aḥad and wāḥid. Al-Jawharī says, “Al-aḥad means al-wāḥid, and is the beginning of number.” The author of al-Nihāya says, “Al-wāḥid is the solitary one who has always been alone, with no other beside Him.”

The transition following وإن استلزم التعظيم remains syntactically awkward; bracketed “when” makes the understood construction explicit.]

[T112.03 | XVI 634 | on K112.03]
His words, “It was equivalent to the Qurʾān.” It has been said: its recitation was equivalent to reciting the Qurʾān. The report is cited as evidence for this reading. Perhaps the meaning is that “Say: He” functions as an introduction and preparation for “God is One.” This works only if the pronoun is taken as the pronoun of the matter.

[T112.04 | XVI 635 | on K112.03]
His words, “Nor remembering God, except a little.” Its opening is:
“I found him not returning to goodwill under reproach.”
That is: I reminded him. “Nor remembering” is intended with tanwīn; it was dropped because two unvocalized consonants met. Allāh consequently remains accusative, not genitive through annexation (iḍāfa). Dhākir is genitive, coordinated with mustaʿtib: “nor remembering.” That is: I reminded him of the affection formerly between us, but he was found not to turn back, through reproach, from the ugliness of what he had done.
His words, “The sounder usage is tanwīn”: this is the well-known reading.
His words, “The master to whom people turn with their needs.” Al-Zajjāj cited al-Asadī:
“Early came the death-announcer with news of the two best of Banū Asad:
ʿAmr ibn Masʿūd and the master to whom all turn.”
Al-Ṣamad: that is, the one to whom everything turns—the one who created all things, and whom nothing can do without. Muḥyī al-Sunna transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, al-Ḥasan and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr: “Al-Ṣamad is the one with no interior cavity.” Al-Shaʿbī said: “The one who neither eats nor drinks.”

[T112.05 | XVI 636 | on K112.03–04]
Al-Rāghib: “That which has no interior cavity is of two kinds: what is below the human being, such as inanimate things; and what is above, namely the Creator, exalted and holy. The purpose of al-Ṣamad is to indicate that He differs from those to whom they ascribed divinity. Something of this kind is indicated by ‘His mother was a woman of truth; they both used to eat food’ [5:75].”
His words, “His statement ‘How could He have a child, when He has no consort?’ indicates this meaning”: these are coordinated with “because He shares no genus.” That is, “He did not beget” either indirectly signifies (kināya) His transcendence above belonging to a genus—for whoever shares a genus with anything takes a consort of his genus, and whoever takes a consort produces offspring—or [the reasoning proceeds] in reverse: how could He have a child when He has taken no consort? Generation occurs only between two partners of one genus. He transcends having a congener, so a consort cannot properly be ascribed to Him, and neither can generation. He states this in explaining the verse in al-Anʿām.

[T112.06 | XVI 636–637 | on K112.05]
His words, “Thus His statement ‘He is God.’” The fāʾ introduces a detailed exposition; the general statement being expounded is “what encompasses His attributes.” Since Allāh names the essence—and in the Opening of the Book he established the impossibility of its being a descriptive epithet—it nevertheless has in each context a meaning appropriate to that context. The particular character of the polytheists' question required its explanation as “the Creator,” because of His statement, “If you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will certainly say: God” [31:25]. Thus Allāh here, as an answer, points them to the one who creates things. You know that knowledge and power are what make creatorship possible: these two attributes are therefore included under it. This is what “implicit in that is His description as powerful and knowing” indicates. He cannot be powerful and knowing without being knowing, living, hearing and seeing. Then these descriptions were followed by the meaning of oneness in “One.” Since uniqueness excludes recourse to another, He was affirmed to possess ṣamadiyya, so that recourse would be to Him.
Once this established an essence entailing the attributes of creatorship, knowledge, power, life and divinity, their perfection was to be explained, together with their difference from creatures' attributes in past and future. At this point it was said: “He neither begot nor was begotten, and no one is equal to Him.”

[T112.07 | XVI 637–638 | continuation]
Ḥujjat al-Islām gives a concise discussion of them:
“‘Aḥad’: He is the wāḥid from whom partnership is excluded, and the aḥad in whom there is no composition. Wāḥid negates partner and likeness; aḥad negates multiplicity within His essence. Al-Ṣamad is the self-sufficient one whom others need. He is aḥad in essence and wāḥid in attributes: for if He had a partner in His dominion, He would not be self-sufficient and needed by others; rather, He would depend for His subsistence and existence on the components of His composition. Thus ṣamadiyya proves both wāḥidiyya and aḥadiyya.
“‘He did not beget’ proves that His continuing existence is not like human existence, whose species persists through generation and reproduction; rather, His existence continues eternally, without beginning or end. ‘Nor was He begotten’ proves that His existence is not like the existence of the human soul, which comes into being after nonexistence and then remains forever, either in a lofty Garden that does not perish or in an abyss that does not end.
“‘No one is equal to Him’ proves that His true existence is an existence that bestows existence on another without receiving existence from another. Thus ‘He is God, One’ proves His holy, transcendent essence. Ṣamadiyya entails His freedom from need and others' need of Him. ‘Nor was He begotten,’ at the end of the sūrah, removes from Him what characterizes others. There is no clearer path to knowledge of God Most High than removing from Him the attributes of creatures.”
[REVIEW T112-U1: The scan prints “nor was He begotten” in the last sentence, although it is not the sūrah's final clause. It is preserved. The wording about partnership, composition and attributes also needs comparison with the work being quoted.]

[T112.08 | XVI 638 | on K112.05–06]
His words, “Exclusively the one who is needed”: the exception is unfilled (istithnāʾ mufarragh); that is, God is exclusively the one who is needed—relative, that is, to creatures.
His words, “Supplementary, not indispensable to the predication.” An indispensable adjunct (ẓarf mustaqirr) is one required to complete the utterance, as when it functions as the predicate in “There was not in it anyone better than you.” A supplementary adjunct (laghw) is one without which the utterance is complete, as in “No one was better than you in it.” In the first case, the indispensable adjunct is put first because it is intended as the focus. The normal arrangement was abandoned in the verse because it was directed at explaining divine oneness.
Ibn al-Ḥājib says: “It was placed first out of concern for the correspondence of verse endings. Had it merely preceded aḥad, the aim would have been achieved; but it would then have separated the two parts which are [the predicate and] the subject of predication. It was therefore placed before both, and the aim was achieved.”
[REVIEW T112-U2: The printed wording is اللذين هما مسند إليه, apparently incomplete as a description of the two parts. The bracketed supplement conveys the apparent argument but requires checking the quoted source.]

[T112.09 | XVI 639 | on K112.06]
The author of al-Intiṣāf says: “Sībawayh reports hearing a rough Arab recite ‘wa-lam yakun aḥadun kufuwan lahu.’ The uncouth man followed his habitual manner, his coarse disposition missing the subtle meaning that required the adjunct and predicate to precede the subject. The verse's intended aim is to deny equivalence and equality to God's essence. It was therefore preferable to put first the equivalence intended to be denied Him. Once it was brought forward for negation, the adjunct was mentioned with it, to distinguish the holy essence by the denial of equivalence.”
I say: its upshot is that attention to the meaning demanded by the context is more fitting, more justified and prior to attention to wording and verse endings.

[T112.10 | XVI 639 | on K112.06]
His words, “Kufuʾan has been read, with ḍamma on kāf.” Ḥafṣ reads with ḍamma on it and on fāʾ, without hamza [kufuwan]. Ḥamza reads with unvocalized fāʾ and hamza when continuing; when stopping, he replaces it with a wāw bearing fatḥa. The others read with ḍamma on fāʾ and with hamza.
Al-Rāghib: “Kufʾ is [equality] in standing and worth. From it comes kifāʾ for a piece of fabric sewn to another, with which the rear of a tent is covered. One says that a person is another's equal in marriage, combat and the like. From it comes mukāfaʾa: equality and corresponding action. Ikfāʾ is overturning something, as though removing equality; from this comes ikfāʾ in poetry.”

[T112.11 | XVI 640 | on K112.07]
His words, “The equivalent of the whole Qurʾān.” It is transmitted with fatḥa or kasra on the ʿayn. Al-Akhfash says: “ʿIdl, with kasra, means an equivalent. ʿAdl, with fatḥa, is originally the verbal noun in ‘I made this nicely equal [to that]’; you turn it into a noun for an equivalent to distinguish it from a ʿidl, a load of goods.” Al-Farrāʾ says: “ʿAdl, with fatḥa, is what equals something while belonging to a different kind. ʿIdl, with kasra, is its like. You say, ‘I have the ʿidl of your servant-boy, or of your sheep,’ when it is a servant-boy equal to a servant-boy or a sheep equal to a sheep. If you mean its value in something of another kind, you pronounce the ʿayn with fatḥa. Some Arabs sometimes pronounce it with kasra; that was an error on their part.”

[T112.12 | XVI 640–641 | on K112.07]
The correct version is “one third of the Qurʾān.” We have transmitted from al-Bukhārī, Muslim, Mālik, Abū Dāwūd and al-Nasāʾī, from Abū Saʿīd, that a man heard another reciting “Say: He is God, One” repeatedly. In the morning he came to the Prophet ﷺ and mentioned it, apparently regarding it as little. The Messenger of God ﷺ said: “By Him in whose hand is my soul, it is indeed equivalent to one third of the Qurʾān.”
Al-Qāḍī says: “Because this sūrah, short as it is, encompasses all knowledge of divinity and refutes those who deviate concerning it, the report says that it equals one third of the Qurʾān. The Qurʾān's aims are confined to explaining beliefs, rulings and narratives. Whoever equates it with the whole takes into account what is intended for its own sake among these.”
His words, “It is for some cause that whoever becomes a master becomes one”: the opening is:
“I resolved to stay for a morning.”
Mā is an additional particle giving indefiniteness and magnification: that is, it is for a great cause that whoever becomes a master becomes one.

[T112.13 | XVI 641 | on K112.07]
His words, “It suffices as proof for whoever acknowledges.” Man iʿtarafa [whoever acknowledges] is the object of kafā [suffices]. Its subject is what is indicated by “because it encompasses God's attributes.” The pronoun in “its excellence” refers to the sūrah. “Believes” is coordinated with “acknowledges”; “the Messenger of God's ﷺ statement” attaches to “believes.” “That the science of divine oneness” attaches to “as proof,” which is a specification (tamyīz). That is: for whoever acknowledges the sūrah's excellence and believes the Messenger's statement, this suffices as proof that the science of divine oneness has an exalted standing with God.
The Prophet's ﷺ statement intended here is the one he transmits at the end of the sūrah: “The seven heavens were founded …,” to the end. I have not found this report in the recognized source collections.
Al-Tirmidhī, Abū Dāwūd and Ibn Māja transmit from Burayda that the Messenger of God ﷺ heard a man saying: “O God, I ask You by my testimony that You are God—there is no god but You—the One, al-Ṣamad, who neither begot nor was begotten, and to whom no one is equal.” The Prophet ﷺ said: “By Him in whose hand is my soul, he has asked God by His greatest name: when invoked by it, He answers; when asked by it, He gives.”

[T112.14 | XVI 642 | on K112.07]
His words, “He said: It has become assured.” Mālik, Aḥmad, al-Tirmidhī and al-Nasāʾī transmitted the report from Abū Hurayra.

[T112.15 | XVI 642–643 | concluding addition]
A conclusion from the words of Shaykh Faṣīḥ al-Dīn—may God have mercy on him:
“‘God, al-Ṣamad’ is not joined by a conjunction to the preceding sentence, because it confirms and explains its content. The same applies to ‘He did not beget,’ because it confirms the content of ‘God, al-Ṣamad’: the absolutely self-sufficient one, whom everything needs, cannot be a begetter or begotten, since that necessarily entails need.
“‘Nor was He begotten’ is joined to ‘He did not beget,’ because ‘was not begotten’ does not express the meaning of ‘did not beget,’ and thus does not confirm its meaning; rather, the two sentences confirm the content of the preceding sentence. ‘And no one is equal to Him’ is joined [by a conjunction] because its content does not confirm the content of the two preceding sentences: it expresses that He cannot have any equal in anything mentioned concerning essence and attributes. He is therefore one, with no partner—exalted, holy and supremely great.
“The predicate is definite in ‘God, al-Ṣamad’ to negate [the claim of] anyone who asserts and names another as ṣamad. It is indefinite in ‘God, One,’ because they did not call things aḥad in this sense.”

PART K — AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ'S AL-KASHSHĀF

[K113.01 | T XVI 644; K IV 820]
Sūrat al-Falaq. There is disagreement about it [whether Meccan or Medinan]; it has five verses.
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.
“Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of daybreak, from the evil of what He created; from the evil of a darkening one when it enters; from the evil of those who blow upon knots; and from the evil of an envier when he envies.” [113:1–5]
[TEXT VARIANT: The separate four-volume transcription's heading says “Meccan; also said to be Medinan,” adds “revealed after al-Fīl,” and repeats the basmala. Dubai's base heading is translated above.]

[K113.02 | T XVI 644–646; K IV 820]
Al-falaq and al-faraq mean morning, because night is split and parted away from it: faʿal has the meaning of mafʿūl. The proverb says, “It is clearer than the breaking of morning,” or “than the parting of morning.” Related to this is their expression saṭaʿa al-furqān when dawn rises.
It is also said to mean everything God splits open: the earth for plants, mountains for springs, clouds for rain, wombs for children, grain and date-stones, and other things. It is also said to be a valley in Hell or a pit within it, from their use of falaq for a depression in the ground; the plural is fulqān.
It is reported that one of the Companions came to Syria and saw the houses of the protected non-Muslim communities (ahl al-dhimma), their comfortable lives and the worldly plenty granted them. He said, “I do not mind. Is al-falaq not awaiting them?” He was asked, “What is al-falaq?” He answered: “A chamber in Hell which, when opened, makes all the inhabitants of the Fire cry out at the intensity of its heat.”

[K113.03 | T XVI 646; K IV 820]
“From the evil of what He created”: from the evil of His creation. Their evil consists in the disobedience and sins committed by morally responsible living beings, and the harm they do one another through injustice, aggression, killing, striking, abuse and so forth; in what the non-responsible among living beings do by devouring, tearing with the teeth, stinging and biting, such as predatory animals and creeping creatures; and in the kinds of harm God has placed in inanimate things, such as burning in fire and killing in poison.

[K113.04 | T XVI 646–648; K IV 820–821]
Al-ghāsiq is night when its darkness becomes dense, from His statement “… until the darkness of night” [17:78]. Related expressions are “the eye ghasaqat”—filled with tears—and “the wound ghasaqat”—filled with blood. Its wuqūb is the entry of its darkness into everything. One says waqabat al-shams when the sun sets. In the report, when he saw that the sun had set, he said, “This is the time when it becomes lawful”—meaning the sunset prayer.
It is also said to be the moon when full. ʿĀʾisha—may God be pleased with her—reports: the Messenger of God ﷺ took my hand, pointed to the moon, and said, “Seek refuge in God from the evil of this, for it is the darkening one when it enters.” Its wuqūb is its entering eclipse and becoming dark.
Al-ghāsiq may also mean a black snake; its waqb is its striking and piercing. Waqb means a hole, as in waqbat al-tharīd, the hollow in a dish of bread soaked in broth.
Refuge is sought from the evil of night because evil spreads more widely in it and is harder to guard against. Hence their sayings, “Night conceals misfortune better,” and “Night has become treacherous.” When it grows dark, treachery increases. Evil is attributed to night because it accompanies it, occurring within it.
[TEXT VARIANT K113-C1: Dubai p.646 cites the opening of 17:78, “Establish prayer at the sun's decline,” rather than the words containing ghasaq. The independent four-volume transcription and Turkish p.1470 give the relevant latter clause, translated above. Both forms are recorded.]

[K113.05 | T XVI 648–649; K IV 821]
Al-naffāthāt are women, souls, or groups of female sorcerers who tie knots in threads, blow upon them and recite incantations. Nafth is blowing with saliva. This has no effect—unless, indeed, something harmful is administered as food, drink or something to smell, or the bewitched person is brought into contact with it in some way. But God—mighty and majestic—may perform an act at that moment as a test by which those steadfast upon truth are distinguished from the Ḥashwiyya and the ignorant common people. The Ḥashwiyya and the rabble attribute it to the women and their blowing; those steadfast in the firm word pay it no attention and attach no weight to it.
If you ask, “What, then, does seeking refuge from their evil mean?”, I answer: there are three interpretations. First, refuge is sought from their activity, the practice of sorcery, and their sin in doing it. Second, refuge is sought from their leading people astray through their sorcery and deceiving them by their false practices. Third, refuge is sought from whatever evil God causes to befall someone at the moment of their blowing.
[TEXT CORRECTION K113-C2: Both electronic Arabic texts have النفخ من ريق. The Dubai scan p.648 clearly reads النفخ مع ريق, “blowing with saliva”; Turkish p.1470 agrees. The scan governs this translation. Ḥashwiyya is retained as the author's polemical label, not silently converted into a neutral modern school name.]

[K113.06 | T XVI 649–650; K IV 821–822]
They may also mean scheming women, from His statement “Your scheming is indeed great” [12:28], likening their scheming to sorcery and blowing on knots; or women who tempt men by presenting themselves to them and displaying their attractions, as though bewitching them through that.
“When he envies”: when his envy becomes manifest and he acts as it dictates, seeking misfortunes for the person envied. If he does not manifest the effect of what he conceals, no harm from him reaches the person he envies. Rather, he harms himself by grieving at another's happiness. ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz is reported to have said: “I have never seen a wrongdoer who more resembles someone wronged than an envier.”
The envier's evil may also mean his sin and the ugliness of his condition at the time of his envy and his displaying its effect.

[K113.07 | T XVI 650–651; K IV 822]
If you ask, “‘From the evil of what He created’ includes everything from which refuge is sought. What is the point, then, of subsequently seeking refuge from the darkening one, the women who blow and the envier?”, I answer: their evil is singled out from all evil because its operation is hidden and reaches a person from where he does not know, as though he were treacherously struck by it. They say: “The worst enemy is the one who conceals his hostility and plots against you without your perceiving it.”
If you ask, “Why are some of those from whom refuge is sought made definite and others indefinite?”, I answer: al-naffāthāt is made definite because every such blower is evil. PART K — AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ'S AL-KASHSHĀF

[K114.01 | T XVI652; K IV823]
Sūrat al-Nās. There is disagreement about it [whether Meccan or Medinan]; it has six verses.
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.
“Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of humankind, the King of humankind, the God of humankind, from the evil of the whisperer who withdraws, who whispers in the breasts of humankind, from among jinn and humankind.” [114:1–6]
[TEXT VARIANT: The separate four-volume transcription calls it Meccan, also said to be Medinan, adds “revealed after al-Falaq,” and repeats the basmala. Dubai's base heading is translated above.]

[K114.02 | T XVI652; K IV823]
“Qul aʿūdhu” has been read with the hamza omitted and its vowel transferred to the lām. Comparable is “fa-khudh arbaʿata” [2:260].
If you ask, “Why say ‘in the Lord of humankind,’ annexing Lord specifically to them?”, I answer: because refuge is sought from the evil of the one who whispers in human breasts. It is as though one had said: I seek refuge from the evil of the one who whispers to people, in their Lord who controls their affairs, who is their God and the one they worship—just as some dependants, when a calamity befalls them, seek help from their master, the one they serve, and the governor of their affairs.

[K114.03 | T XVI653; K IV823]
If you ask, “What is the relation of ‘King of humankind, God of humankind’ to ‘Lord of humankind’?”, I answer: both are explanatory apposition (ʿaṭf bayān), as in “the conduct of Abū Ḥafṣ, ʿUmar, al-Fārūq.” He is clarified by “King of humankind,” then further clarified by “God of humankind.” For someone other than Him may be called “lord of humankind,” as in “They took their rabbis and monks as lords apart from God” [9:31]; someone may also be called “king of humankind.” But “God of humankind” is exclusive, admitting no partnership, and is therefore made the culmination of the clarification.
If you ask, “Why not be content with explicitly mentioning the annexed noun ‘humankind’ once?”, I answer: because explanatory apposition serves clarification, making it a suitable place for explicit mention rather than substitution by a pronoun.

[K114.04 | T XVI653–654; K IV823–824]
Al-waswās is a noun meaning whispering (waswasa), as zalzāl means shaking (zalzala). The verbal noun, however, is wiswās, with kasra, like zilzāl. What is meant is Satan: he is named with the verbal noun as though he were whispering itself, since that is his craft and the occupation to which he constantly devotes himself. Alternatively, “the possessor of whispering” is intended. Waswasa is a faint sound; hence waswās al-ḥuly, the rustling of jewellery.
Al-khannās is one whose habit is to withdraw. It is a form associating him with khunūs, retreating, like al-ʿawwāj and al-battāt [occupational forms]. For it is related from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr that when a person remembers his Lord, Satan withdraws and turns away; when he is heedless, Satan whispers to him.
“Alladhī yuwaswisu” [who whispers] may occupy a position corresponding to any of the three case vowels: genitive as a description, or nominative or accusative as an expression of invective. It is fitting for the reciter to stop at al-khannās and begin with alladhī yuwaswisu on either of these two interpretations [nominative or accusative].
[REVIEW K114-U1: “These two interpretations” naturally refers to nominative/accusative in the base passage. Ṭībī explicitly glosses it as “description and invective” below; the Turkish translation follows that gloss. The disagreement is retained rather than silently resolved.]

[K114.05 | T XVI655; K IV824]
“From among jinn and humankind” explains “who whispers,” on the basis that Satan is of two kinds, jinn and human, as He says: “devils of humankind and jinn” [6:112]. Abū Dharr—may God be pleased with him—is reported to have asked a man: “Have you sought refuge in God from human Satan?”
Min may also attach to yuwaswisu, denoting a starting point: that is, he whispers in their breasts from the direction of the jinn and from the direction of human beings.
It is also said that “from among jinn and humankind” explains al-nās, and that the name al-nās applies to jinn. They adduce nafar [a group] and rijāl [men] in Sūrat al-Jinn as evidence. I do not accept it as established: jinn are called jinn because they are concealed, and people nās because they are manifest, from īnās, seeing, just as they are called bashar. Even if nās applied to both classes, and that were sound and established, it would not suit the Qurʾān's eloquence and its freedom from affectation.

[K114.06 | T XVI656; K IV824]
Better than that is to understand al-nās as al-nāsī [the forgetful one], like “the day the caller calls” [54:6], and as “from where al-nās departed” [2:199] has been read. It is then explained by “jinn and humankind,” since the two weighty kinds (al-thaqalān) are the two species characterized by forgetting God's due—mighty and majestic is He.
It is related from the Messenger of God ﷺ: “Two sūrahs have been sent down to me the like of which has never been sent down. You will never recite two sūrahs more beloved or more pleasing to God than these”—meaning the two sūrahs of refuge. The two sūrahs of refuge are also called al-muqashqishatān.
[TRANSLATOR'S NOTE: The phonological argument is the omission of final yāʾ in al-nāsī, parallel to al-dāʿī/al-dāʿ. No claim that the ordinary reading means “forgetful” is being added.]

PART T — AL-ṬĪBĪ'S ADDITIONAL COMMENTARY

[T114.01 | XVI652 | on K114.02]
Sūrat al-Nās. Meccan; also said to be Medinan. Six verses.
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.
His words, “Why say ‘in the Lord of humankind’?”: that is, He is the Lord of all worlds, so why single out humankind here? He answers: it is humankind alone who seek help from their Lord, owner and object of worship against the affliction that befalls them.

[T114.02 | XVI652–653 | on K114.02]
His words, “Just as some dependants, when a calamity befalls them, seek help from their master, the one they serve, and the governor of their affairs.” He observes an ascent in the help sought: protection through governance is stronger than through being served, while protection through mastery is weaker than through being served. Likewise, the sense of irresistible dominion in divinity is higher than in ownership, and [that is higher] than in lordship.
Some commentaries say: “Repelling Satan's evil and whispering is through one of three things: either not enabling him to whisper, inasmuch as He is Lord; or enabling him but forcibly preventing him, inasmuch as He is King; or forbidding him to whisper as a rebuke, yet willing it by choice, inasmuch as He is God.”
Alternatively, it may be said: the servant seeks refuge in God from Satan, grounding the request in appropriate attributes in ascending order. He first describes Him—mighty and majestic—as Lord, because the first thing the servant knows of his Lord is that He bestows blessings upon his outward and inward being. He then advances to knowing that He governs him and owns him, and then to knowing that He alone is the object of worship without qualification and that there is no final resort except to Him.
[REVIEW T114-U2: The difficult third alternative reads لكن يريدها اختيارا. “Yet willing it by choice” preserves the wording; the subject and the intended theological distinction between prohibition and willing need checking in the unnamed source. Do not replace it with “the devil chooses it” without evidence.]

[T114.03 | XVI653 | on K114.03]
His words, “Someone may also be called king of humankind.” Al-Rāghib: “A king is one who directs the populace through command and prohibition. This is peculiar to governing rational beings; hence ‘king of humankind’ is said, but not ‘king of things.’”

[T114.04 | XVI653–654 | on K114.04]
His words, “The verbal noun, however, is wiswās.” According to one explanation, by waswās he meant the noun which has the meaning of waswasa, itself the verbal noun. The Maghribī grammarians say: the difference between a verbal noun (maṣdar) and a noun of the verbal action (ism al-maṣdar) is this. The meaning expressed by the real act—the origin of the grammatical verb—may be considered together with the agent's engagement in it, its proceeding from him, and its renewed occurrence. The expression assigned to that meaning, restricted by this qualification, is called a maṣdar. If that qualification is not considered, the expression assigned to the meaning without the stated restriction is an ism al-maṣdar.
His words, “His craft”: another reading is “his livelihood” (ḍayʿatuhu). Al-Nihāya: “A man's ḍayʿa is that from which he earns his living, such as a craft, trade, an industry and so on.”
His words, “Associated with khunūs”: he says “associated” because withdrawing has been made his habit.
His words, “When a person remembers his Lord, he withdraws.” We transmitted in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, as a suspended report (taʿlīqan), from Ibn ʿAbbās: the Messenger of God ﷺ said, “Satan crouches upon the heart of the son of Adam. When he remembers God, he withdraws; when he is heedless, he whispers.”

[T114.05 | XVI654–655 | on K114.04]
His words from “It is fitting for the reciter to stop” to “on either of these two interpretations”: that is, description and invective.
In al-Kawāshī: “A stop at al-khannās is sufficient if you make [what follows] nominative or accusative in blame; it is not permissible if you make it genitive as a description of al-khannās.”
I say: its impermissibility is questionable, considering the verse ending. The author of al-Murshid says: “When you have said ‘the All-Merciful, the Merciful,’ the stop is sufficient because it is the end of a verse. It is not a complete stop, because the genitive expression—I mean ‘Master of the Day of Judgment’—is left without its governor, and the epithet is separated from what it qualifies. Likewise a stop at ‘the straight [path]’ is permissible but not a good stop; it is permitted only because it is the end of the verse.”
[TEXT CORRECTION T114-C1: OpenITI reads وفي عدم الجواز نظرا للفاصلة. The scan p.654 reads وفي عدم الجواز نظر، نظرا للفاصلة: the missing نظر is restored in this translation. “Sufficient,” “complete” and “good” preserve distinct stop categories, not interchangeable praise.]

[T114.06 | XVI655 | on K114.05]
His words, “From the direction of human beings”: for example, he whispers into a Muslim's heart, concerning astrologers and soothsayers, that they know the unseen; and concerning jinn, that they harm and benefit.
In al-Muṭṭalaʿ: “According to one explanation, if the phrase is taken as clarification, ‘from among jinn and humankind’ is a circumstantial expression (ḥāl) relating to the pronoun in ‘who whispers.’”

[T114.07 | XVI655 | on K114.05]
His words, “I do not accept it as established”: that is, I do not establish it, from their saying ḥaqqaqtu al-shayʾa aḥuqquhu, “I established the thing.”
The Imam says: “It is said that ‘from among jinn and humankind’ gives two divisions included under ‘in the breasts of al-nās.’ It is as though the common element shared by jinn and humans were called insān, while the human being were also called insān; the expression insān would thus apply equivocally to both genus and species. The evidence is the report that a group of jinn came and were asked, ‘Who are you?’ They answered, ‘Nās from among the jinn.’ God has also called them rijāl in ‘There were men of humankind who sought refuge with men of the jinn, and they increased them in distress’ [72:6]. Thus it would be permissible to call them nās here. This strained statement—he does not mean that it is weak—for making insān the name of a genus containing both jinn and humans is remote from the language.”
[REVIEW T114-U3: The final sentence is problematic in the print itself: وهذا القول المتعسف لا يريد أنه ضعيف. The phrase لا يريد may be displaced from the next gloss; that is a hypothesis, not an adopted correction. Check the Imam's source before repairing it. [T114.08 | XVI656 | on K114.06]
His words, “Better than that”: that is, better than this strained statement; he does not mean that it is an interpretation possessing merit. It is to take al-nās in “the breasts of al-nās” as “the forgetful one.” It can then be divided into jinn and humans, for they are two classes characterized by forgetting God's due.

[T114.09 | XVI656 | on K114.06]

One says taqashqasha al-marīḍ when the sick person recovers and becomes well.”
