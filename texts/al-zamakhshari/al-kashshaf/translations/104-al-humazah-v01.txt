AL-HUMAZAH (104) — THE BACKBITER
Complete first-pass translation of both authorial layers • C06 • 8 November 2026

AL-ZAMAKHSHARĪ — AL-KASHSHĀF

K104.01 — Heading and verses [IV:794]
Meccan; nine verses. [The DKA heading additionally places its revelation after al-Qiyāmah.]
In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

Woe to every backbiter and slanderer, (1) who amasses wealth and counts it over, (2) thinking that his wealth has made him immortal. (3) No indeed! He will surely be cast into the Crusher. (4) And what will make you know what the Crusher is? (5) God’s kindled fire, (6) which rises over the hearts. (7) It is closed over them, (8) amid extended pillars. (9)

K104.02 — Backbiting, ridicule, and a general warning [IV:794–795]
Hamz means breaking, like hazm; lamz means piercing. One says lamazahu and lahazahu, “he stabbed him.” What is intended is breaking people’s reputations, diminishing them, backbiting them, and attacking them. The pattern fuʿala indicates that this is a habit to which the person has become addicted. Comparable forms are luʿana, a habitual curser, and ḍuḥaka, a habitual laugher. The poet says:
“And when I am absent, you are the backbiter, the slanderer.”

The reading waylun li-l-humazati l-lumaza, “woe to the backbiter, the slanderer,” is also transmitted. Another reading has humza lumza with the mīm quiescent: this is the laughing-stock who produces outlandish acts and comical displays, and is laughed at and abused.

It is said to have been revealed concerning al-Akhnas ibn Sharīq, whose habit was backbiting and defamation; or concerning Umayya ibn Khalaf; or al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra and his backbiting and disparaging God’s Messenger. The occasion may be particular while the threat is general, so as to include everyone who undertakes this ugly conduct, and to operate as an indirect allusion to the person concerning whom it was revealed. That is more restraining and more wounding to him.

K104.03 — Accumulation and counting [IV:795]
“Who” is a substitute for “every,” or accusative in a construction of blame. Jammaʿa is also read with the consonant doubled, matching ʿaddadahu, “counts it over.” Another explanation takes ʿaddadahu to mean that he makes it a reserve against the events of time.

Wa-ʿadadahu is also read: he gathers the wealth, keeps track of its amount, and counts it; or he gathers his wealth and the people who support him. This derives from saying that someone possesses ʿadad and ʿudad when he has a plentiful number of helpers and the resources to sustain them. It is also said that wa-ʿadadahu means wa-ʿaddahu, “and counted it,” with the assimilation undone, like ḍaninū for ḍannū, “they were miserly.”

K104.04 — The illusion of immortality [IV:795–796]
Akhladahu and khalladahu have the same meaning. Wealth has lengthened his hopes and filled him with distant aspirations, until, through excessive heedlessness and long hopes, he thinks that wealth has left him immortal in this world, never to die. Or, in erecting buildings secured with stone and brick, planting trees, and cultivating the earth, he acts as someone who thinks his wealth has kept him alive.

Or it is an indirect allusion to righteous action: that is what makes its possessor abide eternally in bliss, whereas wealth has made no one abide there eternally.

It is related that al-Akhnas possessed four thousand dinars; another account says ten thousand. From al-Ḥasan: he visited a wealthy man who was ill. The man said, “What do you say of thousands which I have neither used to ransom myself from a base person nor bestowed upon an honourable one?” He asked, “But what did you keep them for?” The man said, “For the hostility of the times, the ruler’s harshness, the calamities of life, and fear of poverty.” He replied, “Then you will leave them to someone who will not praise you, and come before One who will not excuse you.”

K104.05 — The Crusher and the hearts [IV:796]
“No indeed!” restrains him from his supposition. La-yunbadhānni is also read: he and his wealth will surely be cast away. Another reading has la-yunbadhunna with ḍamma on the dhāl: he and his supporters will be cast away. La-yanbidhannahu, “he/it will surely cast him,” is also read.

“Into the Crusher”: into the fire whose nature is to crush everything thrown into it. Of a voracious man one says, “He is a crusher.” Al-ḥāṭima, “the crushing one,” is also read. The meaning is that it enters their interiors until it reaches their breasts and rises over their afʾida, the centres of their hearts. Nothing in the human body is more delicate than the fuʾād or more acutely pained by the slightest hurt touching it. How, then, when Jahannam’s fire rises over it and takes possession of it?

The hearts may also be singled out because they are the seats of unbelief, corrupt beliefs, and evil intentions. The fire’s rising over them means that it overtops, overcomes, and encompasses them. Or, figuratively, it examines the mines from which what necessitates it is drawn.

K104.06 — Closed doors and extended pillars [IV:796]
“Closed”: shut over them. The poet says:
“My she-camel longs for the mountains of Mecca,
while before her the gates of Ṣanʿāʾ are closed.”

Fī ʿumud is read with two ḍammas; ʿumd with the mīm quiescent; and ʿamad with two fatḥas. The meaning is that their despair of escape and certainty of everlasting imprisonment are reinforced: the doors are closed upon them and pillars stretched across the doors, fastening upon fastening.

Or the meaning is that it is closed over them while they are secured in extended pillars, like the stocks in which thieves are confined. God, protect us from the Fire, O best of those in whom refuge is sought!

K104.07 — Closing report [IV:796]
From God’s Messenger: “Whoever recites Sūrat al-Humazah, God gives him ten good deeds for every person who mocked Muḥammad and his companions.”

AL-ṬĪBĪ — FUTŪḤ AL-GHAYB

T104.01 — Breaking reputations and the correspondence of punishment [XVI:568–569]
Meccan; nine verses. In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

On “hamz means breaking”: according to some, hamz is like squeezing with the hand. One says, “I squeezed the thing in my palm”; from this comes hamz in letters. Hamz of a person is backbiting him. One says hāmiz, hammāz, and humaza of a man.

On “diminishing them,” al-Jawharī says: “Ghaḍḍa minhu yaghuḍḍu, with ḍamma in the imperfect, means that he lowered and diminished his standing.” Others relate to it lowering the gaze and voice, and restraining blame.

On “the pattern fuʿala indicates that this is a habit,” al-Intiṣāf says: “How fine is the correspondence of humaza and lumaza with ḥuṭama! Having marked him with this description and with a form indicating rootedness and firmness, He threatens him with this corresponding attribute, so that deed and recompense balance one another.”

I say: there is another subtle correspondence. Hamz includes breaking reputations; ḥaṭm includes breaking ribs. Nabdh, casting away, conveys contempt and belittlement, since the man imagined himself to belong among the honoured. Under His saying, ‘We seized him and his troops and cast them into the sea’ [28:40], he says: “To express contempt for them and belittle their number, He likens them to little pebbles which someone takes in his palm and throws into the sea.” Al-Wāḥidī transmits from Muqātil: “It crushes bones and consumes flesh until it assails the hearts.”

T104.02 — The poetic example and strange utterances [XVI:569–570]
On “And when I am absent, you are the backbiter, the slanderer”: it is said that the opening is:
“You proffer affection for me, falsely, when you meet me.”

Al-Zajjāj cites these words of Ziyād al-Aʿjam:
“When I meet you, you bare your teeth in a smile despite your resentment;
when I am absent, you are the backbiter, the slanderer.”

Ibn al-Sikkīt says: “Kashr means smiling. Kashara al-rajulu, iftarra, and ibtasama all mean that his teeth become visible.”

On “outlandish acts,” al-Asās says: “Among figurative uses is ‘So-and-so is devoted to awābid of speech,’ meaning its strange expressions, and to awābid of poetry, those poems whose excellence is unmatched.”

T104.03 — General language and indirect rebuke [XVI:570–571]
On “the occasion may be particular while the threat is general”: the Imām transmits from al-Farrāʾ: “An expression’s being general does not contradict a particular person’s being intended. For instance, someone says to you, ‘I have never visited you,’ and you say, ‘Everyone who has not visited me, I will not visit.’ In legal theory this is called restricting a general expression by a contextual indication of customary usage.”

On “to operate as an indirect allusion to the person concerning whom it was revealed”: if that person is al-Akhnas, Umayya, or al-Walīd, and the wording is made general as an indirect rebuke, this is more restraining and wounding. His name has not been explicitly stated so that he would put on the leopard’s hide against the person confronting him. Instead it prompts him to reflect on his own state: has he entered this general category before everyone else by backbiting the best of creation and diminishing his due? Al-Asās says: “Nakaytu fī l-ʿaduwwi nikāyatan means I inflicted many wounds upon the enemy. One says, ‘So-and-so inflicts little hurt but complains at length.’”

T104.04 — The relative clause and the unassimilated form [XVI:571–572]
On “or accusative in a construction of blame”: it is said that it may be an adjective of kull, “every,” because kull is definite. Thus under “Every soul—with it a driver and a witness” [50:21], he says that “with it a driver” is circumstantial and accusative in position, referring to kull, which becomes definite through being annexed to something treated as definite.

On ḍaninū: that is, in the poet’s words:
“Gently, reproacher! Have you learned from my character
that I am generous to people even when they are miserly?”

His saying “It is also said that wa-ʿadadahu means wa-ʿaddahu” is coordinated with “wa-ʿadadahu: he gathers the wealth and keeps track of its amount.” On this latter analysis, it is the object of an omitted verb, as in:
“I fed her straw and cold water.”

T104.05 — Two main readings, with three resulting explanations [XVI:572–573]
On “or he acts”: this is coordinated with “he thinks.” “Or it is an indirect allusion” is coordinated, in sense, with the whole explanation beginning “wealth has lengthened his hopes.” Hence the expression changes. There are thus two principal interpretations which yield three explanations.

The analysis is this: “he thinks” is circumstantial, referring to the pronoun in “amasses.” The supposition concerns either immortality in this world or everlasting bliss, as with the speaker who said, “If I am returned to my Lord, I shall certainly find a better place of return than this” [18:36], and al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil, who said, “I shall certainly be given wealth and children” [19:77]. On the first interpretation, the supposition is either literal—as intended by “he thinks that wealth has left him immortal in this world”—or figurative, as intended by “or, in erecting buildings, he acts,” just as God says, “Do you build on every elevation a monument, amusing yourselves, and take strong structures as though you would live for ever?” [26:128–129]. On the second interpretation, the verse contains an indirect allusion.

It is appropriate on the first interpretation to make “who” a substitute for “every.” The meaning is: woe to the man who amassed wealth and counted it, then entertained long hopes and fell into delusion, thinking his wealth had left him immortal in this world. On the second interpretation, it should be accusative for blame. The meaning is: woe to the impious slanderer—I mean the one whom accumulating wealth and relying on men emboldened to slander and impiety, while he also thinks his wealth will make him live eternally in bliss. “No indeed! He will surely be cast into the Crusher.” What makes its possessor abide in the lasting bliss of Paradise is righteous action, as in His saying, “The day when neither wealth nor sons avail, except one who comes to God with a sound heart” [26:88–89]. The two interpretations thus unfold what was folded together in “who is a substitute for every, or accusative in a construction of blame.” God knows best.

T104.06 — Ransoming one’s honour and the rebuke [XVI:573–574]
On “I have not used them to ransom myself from a base person”: I did not make my wealth a ransom for my honour, to escape his harm. He cites:
“I protect my honour with my wealth and do not stain it;
may God not bless wealth once honour is lost!”

On “the hostility of the times,” al-Asās says: “Nabā ʿannī fulān means he parted from me. Between us is nabwa, estrangement. He complains of the times’ estrangement and harshness.”

On “‘No indeed!’ restrains him from his supposition,” the Imām says: “It is not as he imagined, that wealth and numbers make one immortal; rather, knowledge and righteousness do. ʿAlī, God be pleased with him, said: ‘The hoarders of wealth have died while still alive, but the learned remain as long as time remains.’ Or kallā means ‘truly’: he will surely be cast away. The lām answers an oath, indicating the presence of an oath in kallā. Casting away conveys humiliation and contempt, since he imagined himself among the honoured.”

T104.07 — The fuʾād and the fire’s discernment [XVI:574–575]
On “nothing in the human body is more delicate than the fuʾād,” al-Rāghib says: “Fuʾād is like qalb, ‘heart,’ but it is called fuʾād when the sense of tafaʾʾud, burning, is considered in it. Faʾadtu al-laḥma means I roasted the meat; laḥm faʾīd is roasted meat. Singling out the hearts in ‘which rises over the hearts’ calls attention to the extreme effect upon them.”

On “or, figuratively, it examines the mines from which what necessitates it is drawn”: choosing the word “mines” hints at the reverse of the Prophet’s saying, “People are mines, like mines of gold and silver.” Since these people’s hearts are the settled seats of the pollution and filth of corrupt beliefs that necessitate the Fire, and the commencement of each person’s burning is fixed according to what he deserves, it is said that the Fire figuratively examines the mines of what necessitates it.

In al-Taysīr, Abū Saʿīd says: “It knows how much punishment each of them deserves because of the unbelief and corrupt beliefs in his heart.” This comes from saying iṭṭalaʿa fulānun ʿalā amrinā: he became acquainted with our affair and knew it. God has made the fire such that it burns each person according to his deserts, neither adding nor subtracting, as though it had ascertained the amount he deserves. He says: “Since it may be described as enraged and as calling whoever turned his back and withdrew, it may also be described in this way.”

T104.08 — Closure and the readings of “pillars” [XVI:575]
On “muʾṣada: closed,” al-Rāghib says: “A waṣīda is a chamber made in a mountain for storing property. One says awṣadtu al-bāba and āṣadtuhu: I closed the door and secured it. God Most High says, ‘Over them is a closed fire’ [90:20]; it is also read with hamza.”

On “fī ʿumud is read”: Abū Bakr, Ḥamza, and al-Kisāʾī read it with two ḍammas; the others with two fatḥas.

On “pillars are stretched across the doors”: it is said that, on this interpretation, fī ʿumud is circumstantial, referring to the pronoun in muʾṣada—namely the one referring to the doors. On the interpretation “secured in pillars,” it is circumstantial, referring to the pronoun in ʿalayhim, “over them.”

T104.09 — Stocks, the particular occasion, and the general scope [XVI:576]
On “like the stocks,” al-Jawharī says: “The miqṭara is the falaq: a piece of wood with holes into which prisoners’ legs are inserted.”

I say: the first interpretation suits the report that the verse was revealed concerning al-Akhnas ibn Sharīq, Umayya ibn Khalaf, or al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra and his backbiting God’s Messenger. Having explained that the Crusher is the fire which examines the mines of what necessitates it, God follows this with “It is closed over them.” That is, the fire has ascertained these people’s deserts for backbiting the best of humankind, and is therefore closed and shut over them. Their despair of escape and certainty of everlasting imprisonment are thus reinforced.

The second interpretation suits taking “every backbiter and slanderer” generally. This is indicated by his saying, “the laughing-stock who produces outlandish acts and comical displays,” because he attacks people’s reputations like a thief stealing their property. On this interpretation, their remaining for ever in the Fire would follow.

End of the sūrah.

TEXTUAL NOTES
K104-U1: The third variant of “will be cast” is printed as an active verb with an object pronoun; the agent is not explained in the Arabic passage. The English preserves that indeterminacy rather than importing the Turkish comparator’s supplied subject, “wealth.”
T104-U1: The discussion of wa-ʿadadahu moves between a verbal analysis and an accusative nominal analysis with an omitted verb. The cold-water example illustrates an understood “gave to drink”; the compact source wording and both analyses are retained.
T104-U2: XVI:574 prints وأقر بدء إحراق كل أحد على قدر استحقاقه. “The commencement of each person’s burning is fixed according to what he deserves” is a provisional construal of this difficult phrase. The scan confirms the wording; further witnesses are needed.
T104-U3: The main text at XVI:576 reads يلزم, “would follow,” in its final sentence; the printed apparatus records a witness with لا يلزم, “would not follow.” This reverses the implication. The translation follows the printed main text and retains this unresolved variant explicitly; it does not silently attribute a settled doctrine of everlasting punishment for every slanderer to Ṭībī.
