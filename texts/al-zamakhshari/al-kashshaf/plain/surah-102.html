Rivalry and the graves

Alhāhu (ألهاه) ʿan (عن) kadhā (كذا) and aqhāhu (أقهاه) mean that something occupied him and distracted him from something else. Takāthur (تكاثر) is competing in abundance and boasting of it: one group says, “We are more numerous,” and the other says, “We are more numerous.” It is reported that Banū ʿAbd Manāf and Banū Sahm boasted to one another about which was greater in number. Banū ʿAbd Manāf outnumbered them. Banū Sahm said: “Aggression destroyed us in the age of ignorance; count against us both the living and the dead.” Banū Sahm then outnumbered them. The meaning is: you competed over the number of the living, and once you exhausted their number, you turned to the graves and competed over the dead. Their proceeding to mention the dead is expressed as visiting the graves, in mockery of them. It is also said that they actually visited the graves, saying, “This is so-and-so's grave; this is so-and-so's grave,” while boasting to one another. The meaning is that this distracted you—something that does not concern you and yields you no benefit in this world or the next—from what does concern you: the affairs of religion, which are more important and more deserving of attention than anything else. Or He means: rivalry in wealth and children distracted you until you died and were buried. You spent your lives seeking this world, racing toward it and throwing yourselves upon it, until death came while you cared for nothing else. It distracted you from what had a greater claim upon you: striving for your final outcome and working for your hereafter. Visiting the graves is an expression for death. A poet said: “This year no intimate who has tasted a shared attachment will secure ten [nights of exclusive companionship], unless he visits the grave.” Another said: “Abū Mālik (مَالِك) visited the graves, and became the meanest of their visitors.” Ibn ʿAbbās read a-alhākum (أألهاكم), as a question whose purpose is to elicit acknowledgment.

Outnumbering; the irony of visiting graves (XVI, 558–559)

Arabic lemma: فكثرتهم بنوسهم

On “Banū Sahm outnumbered them”: they defeated them through greater numbers, from the expression kāthartuhu fa-kathartuhu (كاثرته فكثرته), “I competed with him in numbers and outnumbered him.” Takāthur (تكاثر) is striving to increase one's wealth and numbers. On “you turned to the graves and competed over the dead”: according to this, “the graves” is an indirect expression for moving from mentioning the living to mentioning the dead in mutual boasting. It is mockery because visiting graves was prescribed to recall death, reject love of this world, and abandon ostentation and boasting. These people reversed this: they made visiting graves a cause of greater hardness of heart, immersion in worldly love, and boasting of greater numbers. We have transmitted from Muslim, Abū Dāwūd, and al-Nasāʾī, from Burayda, that God's Messenger said: “I forbade you to visit graves; now visit them.” Abū Dāwūd's version reads: “Visit them, for they remind you of the hereafter.” On “or He means: rivalry in wealth and children distracted you until you died”: the three interpretations thus reduce to ‘visiting’ meaning either the transition from mentioning one group to mentioning another, actual visiting, or death. “Spending” is a circumstantial expression attached to “distracted you”; “from what had a greater claim upon you” is also governed by “distracted you.”

The verse about a shared attachment (559–560)

Arabic lemma: لن يخلص العام

On “This year no intimate…”: al-Fāʾiq says: “A woman's ḍamd (ضمد) is her joining together and taking two lovers.” Abū Dhuʾayb said: “You wish to join me and Khālid as your lovers— can two swords, woe to you, be joined in one sheath?” The author of the quoted verse is Miqdād ibn Ḥassān al-Zubayrī. It is preceded by: “I have found a shared attachment a grievous thing.” In the age of ignorance a woman would take a lover besides her husband; that is ḍamd (ضمد). On “ten” (ʿashran (عَشْرًا)): ten nights. A reading with kasra (كَسْرَة (ـِ)) on the ʿayn (عين (ع)), ʿishran (عِشْرًا), is also transmitted, meaning companionship. Muʿāshara (معاشرة) means association, as does taʿāshur (تعاشر); the corresponding noun is ʿishra (عِشْرة). Khalīl (خليل) means husband. The meaning is that a husband will not secure exclusive companionship with a woman for ten nights unless he dies. “Who has tasted a shared attachment” describes the intimate. Repeated warning

Kallā (كلّا), “no indeed,” rebukes and calls attention to the fact that someone concerned for his own welfare ought not to make this world his entire concern while neglecting his religion. “You will come to know” warns them so that they may fear and awaken from heedlessness. Repetition emphasizes the rebuke and warning directed at them. Thumma, “again/then,” indicates that the second warning is more emphatic and severe than the first, as when you tell someone you are advising, “I tell you, and then I tell you again: do not do it.” The meaning is: you will know the error of your present course when you see before you the terror of meeting God. This admonition is counsel and mercy for you. He then repeats the admonition and says, “If only you knew,” with the consequence left unstated: if you knew what lies before you with knowledge of something certain—as you know those matters of which you are assured and to whose knowledge you have devoted your attention—you would do something beyond description or comprehension. But you are astray and ignorant.

Kallā, stopping points, and the two senses (560–561)

Arabic lemma: {كلا

On “kallā (كلّا) rebukes and calls attention”: it rejects the preceding discourse and calls attention to what the following discourse indicates. He thus takes both its meanings into consideration. The Imām [Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī] says: “Kallā (كلّا) is connected with what precedes by rejection and denial: the matter is not as these people imagine, that true happiness lies in abundant numbers, wealth, and children. It is connected with what follows in the sense, ‘Truly, you will come to know’—but at a time when the transgressor becomes repentant, the unbeliever becomes a Muslim, and the covetous person becomes an ascetic.” The author's words intimate both meanings. Al-Kawāshī says: “The stop at ‘the graves’ is complete if kallā (كلّا) calls attention. If it rebukes, the stop is at kallā (كلّا).” If you ask whether the author's position entails using a shared expression simultaneously in both its different meanings, I answer: it does not. The intention is that, if one begins with kallā (كلّا), a new statement begins there. An implied question is supplied: what is the recompense of these heedless people, and what is said of them? The answer is: truly, they will know the outcome of their state when they see the blazing Fire. The discourse thus conveys rebuke by its meaning. If one stops at kallā (كلّا), the implied question occurs after it: what will be done to these rejected people who have been rebuked? The answer is: they will know what will be done to them when they see the blazing Fire. The discourse thus entails calling attention by its meaning. The author of al-Murshid says: “‘Until you visited the graves’ is a complete stop. Begin with kallā (كلّا) in the sense of threat and warning.” On “if you knew what lies before you”: it is said that ‘knowledge’ here means knowing the thing itself, not knowing it by its description.

Seeing the Fire; readings; certainty

He then says, “You will surely see the blazing Fire,” explaining what He had warned them against and threatened them with. The magnifying effect of clarifying something after leaving it unspecified has already been discussed. This is the response to an omitted oath. The oath emphasizes the threat and establishes that what they are threatened with admits no doubt. He repeats it, coordinating it with thumma, to intensify the threat and increase the terror. La-tarʾunna (لَتَرَؤُنَّ) is also read, with a hamza (همزة (ء)) [in place of the wāw (واو (و))]; it is considered objectionable. If you ask why, when changing a wāw (واو (و)) bearing ḍamma (ضَمَّة (ـُ)) into hamza (همزة (ء)) is a regular analogy, I answer: that applies to a wāw (واو (و)) whose ḍamma (ضَمَّة (ـُ)) is permanent. Here it is incidental, introduced to avoid the meeting of two vowelless letters. La-turawunna (لَتُرَوُنَّ) and la-turawunnahā (لَتُرَوُنَّها) are also read as passives: “you will surely be made to see,” and “you will surely be made to see it.” “With the very certainty of sight” (ʿayna al-yaqīn (عين اليقين)): a seeing that is certainty itself and its pure essence. Seeing may mean knowledge and visual perception.

The hamza reading and the canonical readers (562)

Arabic lemma: ذاك في الواو التي ضمتها لازمة

On “that applies to a wāw (واو (و)) whose ḍamma (ضَمَّة (ـُ)) is permanent”: al-Zajjāj says: “The reading is la-tarawunna (لَتَرَوُنَّ), with ḍamma (ضَمَّة (ـُ)) on the wāw (واو (و)) and without hamza (همزة (ء)). The wāw (واو (و)) receives ḍamma (ضَمَّة (ـُ)) because it and the nūn (نون (ن)) are both vowelless. Some have pronounced it with hamza (همزة (ء)), but the grammarians dislike this because its ḍamma (ضَمَّة (ـُ)) is not permanent: it was given a vowel to avoid two vowelless letters meeting. They change a wāw (واو (و)) with permanent ḍamma (ضَمَّة (ـُ)) into hamza (همزة (ء)), as in adʾur (أدؤر), the plural of dār (دار), ‘house’; adwur is also permissible.” On “la-turawunna (لَتُرَوُنَّ) is also read”: Ibn ʿĀmir and al-Kisāʾī read with ḍamma (ضَمَّة (ـُ)) on the tāʾ (تاء (ت)); the others with fatḥa (فَتْحَة (ـَ)). Among the seven readers there is no disagreement that la-tarawunnahā (لَتَرَوُنَّها) has fatḥa (فَتْحَة (ـَ)) on the tāʾ (تاء (ت)).

Ṭībī · فتوح الغيبT102.05

قوله: ({عين اليقين}: أي: الرؤية التي هي نفس اليقين)، قيل: أراد أن {عين اليقين} نصب على المصدر، والعين هاهنا بمعنى نفس الشيء، كقولك: جاء زيد نفسه وعينه. والصواب أن الرؤية هاهنا بمعنى الإبصار لا العلم.

وقلت: هذا هو الذي أراده بقوله: "ويجوز أن يراد بالرؤية العلم والإبصار"، على العطف التفسيري. وقال القاضي: "عين اليقين: الرؤية التي هي نفس اليقين؛ فإن علم المشاهدة أعلى مراتب اليقين".

وقال شيخنا شيخ الإسلام قدس سره في "العوارف": "علم اليقين ما كان من طريق النظر والاستدلال، وعين اليقين ما كان من طريق الكشوف والنوال، وحق اليقين ما كان بتحقيق الانفصال عن لوث الصلصال، بورود رائد الوصال. وقال الجنيد: حق اليقين ما يتحقق العبد بذلك، وهو أن يشاهد الغيوب كما يشاهد المرئيات مشاهدة عيان".

Knowledge, sight, and the degrees of certainty (562–563)

Arabic lemma: {عين اليقين

On “‘the very certainty of sight’: a seeing that is certainty itself”: it is said that ʿayna al-yaqīn (عين اليقين) is accusative as a verbal-noun expression. ʿAyn (عين (ع)) here means the thing itself, as when you say, “Zayd himself came”—nafsuhu or ʿaynuhu (عينه). The correct position is that seeing here means visual perception rather than knowledge. I say: this is what he intended by “seeing may mean knowledge and visual perception,” treating the coordination as explanatory. The Qāḍī says: “‘ʿAyn (عين (ع)) al-yaqīn’ means a seeing that is certainty itself, for knowledge through direct witnessing is the highest degree of certainty.” Our shaykh, Shaykh al-Islām—may his inner being be sanctified—says in al-ʿAwārif: “ʿIlm al-yaqīn (علم اليقين), knowledge of certainty, is reached through reflection and inference. ʿAyn (عين (ع)) al-yaqīn, the eye of certainty, is reached through unveilings and gifts. Ḥaqq al-yaqīn, the reality of certainty, comes through truly separating oneself from the stain of clay when the herald of union arrives.” Al-Junayd says: “The reality of certainty is that the servant becomes fully established in it: he witnesses unseen things just as he witnesses visible things, with direct vision.”

Zamakhsharī · الكشافK102.05

فإن قلت: ما النعيم الذي يسئل عنه الإنسان ويعاتب عليه؟ فما من أحد إلا وله نعيم؟ قلت:

هو نعيم من عكف همته على استيفاء اللذات، ولم يعش إلا ليأكل الطيب ويلبس اللين، ويقطع أوقاته باللهو والطرب، لا يعبأ بالعلم والعمل، ولا يحمل نفسه مشاقهما، فأما من تمتع بنعمة الله وأرزاقه التي لم يخلقها إلا لعباده، وتقوى بها على دراسة العلم والقيام بالعمل، وكان ناهضا بالشكر: فهو من ذاك بمعزل، وإليه أشار رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم فيما يروى: أنه أكل هو وأصحابه تمرا وشربوا عليه ماء فقال: «الحمد لله الذي أطعمنا وسقانا وجعلنا مسلمين» .

عن رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم: «من قرأ ألهاكم التكاثر لم يحاسبه الله بالنعيم الذي أنعم به عليه في دار الدنيا، وأعطى من الأجر كأنما قرأ ألف آية» .

The enjoyment about which people are questioned

“About the pleasures you enjoyed”: about the amusement and comfortable enjoyment whose pleasures distracted you from religion and its obligations. If you ask what enjoyment a person is questioned and reproached about, since everyone has some enjoyment, I answer: it is the enjoyment of someone who devotes all his concern to exhausting the pleasures of life, living only to eat fine food, wear soft clothes, and pass his time in amusement and entertainment. He pays no heed to knowledge and action and does not undertake their difficulties. But someone who enjoys God's blessings and provisions—which He created only for His servants—and draws strength from them to study knowledge and perform good works, while rising to the duty of gratitude, is far removed from this. God's Messenger indicated this in the report that he and his Companions ate dates and drank water afterward, and he said: “Praise belongs to God, who fed us, gave us drink, and made us Muslims.” God's Messenger is reported to have said: “Whoever recites ‘Rivalry in increase has distracted you,’ God will not call him to account for the enjoyment He granted him in the abode of this world, and he will be given a reward as though he had recited a thousand verses.”

تمت

Who is questioned about enjoyment? (563–564)

Arabic lemma: هو نعيم من عكف همته على استيفاء اللذات

On “the enjoyment of someone who devotes all his concern to exhausting pleasures”: the Qāḍī says: “The address, ‘Then that day you will surely be questioned about the pleasures you enjoyed,’ concerns specifically anyone whose worldly life distracted him from his religion, not the believers, because of the contextual indication and the many texts such as ‘Say: Who has forbidden God's adornment, which He has brought forth for His servants, and the good things of provision?’ (7:32), and ‘Eat of the good things’ (23:51). It is also said to concern unbelievers specifically; or to be general, since everyone is questioned about his gratitude.” I say: the latter is supported by what we have transmitted from Muslim, al-Tirmidhī, and Ibn Mājah, from Abū Hurayra. God's Messenger went out and encountered Abū Bakr and ʿUmar. He asked: “What brought you out of your homes?” They said: “Hunger.” He said: “By Him in whose hand is my soul, the same thing brought me out.” They came to the house of a man of the Anṣār, who brought them a cluster containing unripe, dried, and fresh dates, and slaughtered an animal for them. They ate from the sheep and the date-cluster and drank. When they had eaten and drunk their fill, God's Messenger said to them: “By Him in whose hand is my soul, you will surely be questioned about this enjoyment on the Day of Resurrection.” The report is abbreviated here. Al-Wāḥidī transmits from Muqātil: “He means the unbelievers of Mecca. In this world they lived amid goodness and blessings. On the Day of Resurrection they will be questioned about gratitude for what they enjoyed. They did not thank the Lord of blessings, since they worshipped others and associated partners with Him; then they will be punished. This is al-Ḥasan's position.” I say: this is supported by the fact that the address from the beginning of the sūrah is to those competing and boasting in abundance, who, as explained above, are unbelievers. Since preoccupation with worldly enjoyment is a characteristic of the heedless, and a believer must avoid base qualities, God's Messenger spoke sternly when he said, “You will be questioned about this enjoyment on the Day of Resurrection.” This does not mean that he was interpreting the verse by what he said. Complete.
