Names, place of revelation, and verse count [K-DKA I:1]

It is Meccan. It has also been said to be both Meccan and Medinan, since it was revealed once in Mecca and again in Medina. It is called “Mother of the Qurʾān” (Umm al-Qurʾān), because it includes the meanings found in the Qurʾān: praising God Most High as He deserves; imposing worship through commands and prohibitions; and promises and threats. For the same reason it is called “The Treasure” (al-Kanz) and “The Comprehensive” (al-Wāfiya). It is called “Praise” (al-Ḥamd (الحمد)), and “The Oft-Repeated” (al-Mathānī), because it is repeated in every rakʿa. It is called “Prayer” (al-Ṣalāh), because prayer is excellent, or valid, through its recitation within it; and “Healing” (al-Shifāʾ) and “The Healer” (al-Shāfiya). It has seven verses by agreement, although some count “upon whom You have bestowed favour” as a verse ending and do not count the basmala (بسملة), whereas others take the opposite position.

Meccan revelation and “Mother of the Qurʾān” [I:677]

Arabic lemma: لاشتمالها على المعاني التي في القرآن

On his words, “The sūrah of the Opening of the Book is Meccan; it is also said to be Meccan and Medinan”:

Al-Kawāshī says: “The correct view is that it is Meccan.” The Qāḍī [al-Bayḍāwī] says: “It has been established that it is Meccan, because of His saying, ‘We have indeed given you seven of the oft-repeated’ [15:87], which is Meccan.”

On his words, “because it includes the meanings found in the Qurʾān”:

That is, the Qurʾān sets out in detail the meaning that al-Fātiḥa contains in summary. In a related way Mecca is called “Mother of the Settlements,” because the earth was spread out from beneath it. Imām Muḥammad ibn Ismāʿīl al-Bukhārī said: “Al-Fātiḥa was called Mother of the Book because writing the codices begins with it, and recitation in prayer begins with it.”

The four kinds of religious knowledge [I:678]

Arabic lemma: لله رب العالمين* الرحمن الرحيم

The Qāḍī says: “It comprises theoretical wisdom and practical rulings, which consist in following the straight path, and knowledge of the ranks of the fortunate and the stations of the wretched.”

This may be expanded by saying that it comprises four kinds of knowledge upon which religion depends.

The first is knowledge of the foundations (ʿilm al-uṣūl (الأصول)). Its principal points are knowledge of God and His attributes, indicated by “to God, Lord of the worlds, the All-Merciful, the Merciful”; knowledge of prophethood, intended by “upon whom You have bestowed favour”; and knowledge of the return to God, intimated by “Master of the Day of Recompense.”

The second is knowledge of the branches (ʿilm al-furūʿ (الفروع)). Its foundation is acts of worship, intended by “You alone we worship.” Worship is bodily and financial. Both forms require the affairs of livelihood, comprising transactions and marriages; and these necessarily require judicial determinations. The branches are therefore established upon these foundations.

The third is knowledge of that through which perfection is obtained: the science of character (ʿilm al-akhlāq (الأخلاق)). Its highest aspect is reaching the presence of the All-Sufficient (al-ṣamadāniyya (الصمدانية)), taking refuge in the precinct of divine uniqueness (al-fardāniyya (الفردانية)), following His path, and remaining upright upon it. This is indicated by “You alone we ask for help; guide us on the straight path.”

The fourth is knowledge of narratives and accounts of past communities and bygone generations, both their fortunate and their wretched, together with the promises made to those among them who acted well and the threats directed at their wrongdoers. This is intended by “upon whom You have bestowed favour, not those who have incurred wrath, nor those astray.”

We shall disclose this meaning more fully when we begin interpreting the sūrah according to this pattern. Keep it in mind, so that it may serve as a decisive judge.

Commands, names, repetition, and prayer [I:679]

Arabic lemma: ومن التعبد بالأمر والنهي

On his words, “and imposing worship through commands and prohibitions”:

Al-Asās says: “Taʿabbadanī (تعبّدني) so-and-so and iʿtabadanī (اعتبدني) mean ‘he made me like a slave to him.’ Taʿabbada (تعبّد) so-and-so means ‘he practised devotion,’ and ‘he sat in his place of worship.’”

Here the verb takes bāʾ (باء (ب)) because it incorporates the meaning of imposing an obligation (taklīf (تكليف)): that is, He imposed commands and prohibitions upon him as worship, meaning the things commanded and prohibited. It is also possible for bāʾ (باء (ب)) to be as in “I wrote with the pen,” with command and prohibition retaining their literal meanings.

On “and al-Wāfiya for that reason”:

That is, it is called “The Treasure” and “The Comprehensive” for the meaning already mentioned: its inclusion of the meanings.

On “in every rakʿa”:

That is, in every prayer. God Most High says, “Bow with those who bow” [2:43]. It has also been said that this is because it is paired with another sūrah in every rakʿa.

On “because it is excellent, or valid”:

This explains why the name “prayer” is appropriate to al-Fātiḥa. The Ḥanafīs say that it is called the sūrah of Prayer because it is excellent: reciting it in prayer is preferable to reciting something else. The Shāfiʿīs explain the name by saying that prayer is valid only through it.

Verse endings and counting the basmala [I:680]

Arabic lemma: إلا أن منهم من عد (أنعمت عليهم

On his words, “except that some count ‘upon whom You have bestowed favour’ but not the naming formula, while others take the opposite position”:

The author of al-Murshid says: “If you stop at ‘upon whom You have bestowed favour,’ it is the end of a verse according to the people of Medina and Basra. The stop is permissible but not good, because ghayr (غير), when genitive, is connected to it as an adjective or substitute, and when accusative is a circumstantial expression or an exception. Its permissibility rests only upon the transmitted report that he—God bless him and grant him peace—used to stop at verse endings. This is a verse ending according to those I have mentioned, and that explains its permissibility.” His words end here.

I say: the second position is preferable, because the cadence of “upon whom You have bestowed favour” does not match that of the sūrah’s verse endings, and because of what Muḥyī al-Sunna related in Sharḥ (شرح) al-Sunna, from Ibn Jurayj:

“My father informed me, from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, that ‘We have indeed given you seven of the oft-repeated and the mighty Qurʾān’ [15:87] means Mother of the Qurʾān. My father said: Saʿīd ibn Jubayr recited it to me until he finished it, then said, ‘In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful: the seventh verse.’ Saʿīd said: Ibn ʿAbbās recited it to me just as I have recited it to you, then said, ‘In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful: the seventh verse.’”

قراء المدينة والبصرة والشأم وفقهاؤها على أن التسمية ليست بآية من الفاتحة ولا من غيرها من السور، وإنما كتبت للفصل والتبرك بالابتداء بها، كما بدأ بذكرها في كل أمر ذى بال، وهو مذهب أبى حنيفة رحمه الله ومن تابعه، ولذلك لا يجهر بها عندهم في الصلاة. وقراء مكة والكوفة وفقهاؤهما على أنها آية من الفاتحة ومن كل سورة، وعليه الشافعي وأصحابه رحمهم الله، ولذلك يجهرون بها. وقالوا:

قد أثبتها السلف في المصحف مع توصيتهم بتجريد القرآن، ولذلك لم يثبتوا (آمين) فلولا أنها من القرآن لما أثبتوها. وعن ابن عباس: «من تركها فقد ترك مائة وأربع عشرة آية من كتاب الله تعالى» .

The basmala as a verse [K-DKA I:1]

“In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.”

The Qurʾān readers and jurists of Medina, Basra, and Syria hold that the naming formula (al-tasmiya (التسمية)) is a verse neither of al-Fātiḥa nor of the other sūrahs. It was written only to separate them and to obtain blessing by beginning with it, just as every matter of consequence is begun by mentioning it. This is the position of Abū Ḥanīfa—God have mercy upon him—and his followers. Accordingly, they do not pronounce it aloud in prayer.

The readers and jurists of Mecca and Kufa hold that it is a verse of al-Fātiḥa and of every sūrah. This is the position of al-Shāfiʿī and his companions—God have mercy upon them—and accordingly they pronounce it aloud. They argue: the early generations entered it into the written Qurʾān, despite their injunction that the Qurʾān be kept free of additions; for that reason they did not enter Āmīn (آمين). Had it not belonged to the Qurʾān, they would not have entered it. It is related from Ibn ʿAbbās: “Whoever omits it has omitted one hundred and fourteen verses from the Book of God Most High.”

The readers’ positions on the basmala [I:681]

Arabic lemma: قراء المدينة والبصرة والشام

On “the readers of Medina, Basra, and Syria”:

Al-Shuʿla says: “From Mecca, Ibn Kathīr, and from Kufa, ʿĀṣim and al-Kisāʾī, hold that the basmala (بسملة) belongs to al-Fātiḥa and to every sūrah. This is the view of Ibn ʿAbbās and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, and the position of al-Shāfiʿī, ʿAṭāʾ, al-Zuhrī, and Ibn al-Mubārak—God be pleased with them.

A “matter of consequence” and the metaphor of being cut short [I:681–682]

Arabic lemma: في كل أمر ذي بال

On “every matter of consequence”:

Imām Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal related in his Musnad, from Abū Hurayra—God be pleased with him—that God’s Messenger—God bless him and grant him peace—said: “Every speech or matter of consequence that is not opened with the remembrance of God is cut short”—or he said, “severed.”

Al-Nihāya says: “Bāl (بال) means a condition or affair. A matter dhū bāl (بال) is an important one to which attention and concern are devoted. In another usage, bāl (بال) means the heart.”

It has been said that such a matter is called dhū bāl (بال), “possessing a heart,” because, by occupying the heart, it is as though it owns it and has become its possessor. An important affair may also be called dhū bāl (بال) through an implicit metaphor (istiʿāra (استعارة) makniyya), with “cut short” supplying a reinforcing feature (tarshīḥ (ترشيح)), as in “Your hater is the one cut off” [108:3]. Similarly, he—blessings upon him—gave the affair a head and the summit of a hump in his saying, “The head of the affair is Islam, and the summit of its hump is jihād.” Al-Tirmidhī transmitted this report.

On “to something omitted”:

Prepositions are never without something to which they are attached, because their function is to convey the meanings of verbs to nouns. They indicate an action in general, however, and a contextual indication is needed to specify it. It would have been more appropriate to say, “What follows the naming formula is the reading,” since beginning with the formula concerns the action that the person pronouncing it intends to perform. This is indicated by his saying, “Every agent who begins his action with ‘In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful’ implicitly supplies that for which the naming formula was made a beginning.” What is implicit is the action, not its object, just as what follows the slaughterer’s naming formula is the slaughtering, not the slaughtered animal. Why the divine name comes first [K-DKA I:3]

If you ask, “Why have you supplied the omitted verb after the phrase?” I answer: between the action and that to which it is attached, the latter is more important. People used to begin with their gods’ names, saying, “In al-Lāt (اللات)’s name,” or “In al-ʿUzzā (العزى)’s name.” The professed monotheist must therefore intend to reserve the beginning exclusively for the name of God, mighty and exalted. If you ask, “But He said, ‘Read in the name of your Lord’ [96:1], putting the verb first,” I answer: putting the verb first is more effective there, since that was the first sūrah revealed, and the command to read was therefore more important.

Instrument or accompaniment [K-DKA I:3–4]

If you ask, “What does attaching God’s name to reading mean?” I answer: there are two possibilities.

First, it is attached as the pen is attached to writing in “I wrote with the pen.” The believer holds that an action is not legally accorded weight, or performed according to the Sunna, unless it issues from mentioning God’s name, because of the Prophet’s saying—blessings and peace upon him—“Every matter of consequence that is not begun with God’s name is cut short.” Otherwise it is an action that counts as no action. He therefore treats his action as performed by means of God’s name, as writing is performed by means of the pen.

Second, it is attached as oil is attached to growing in “It grows with oil” [23:20]: the meaning is “Seeking blessing through God’s name, I read.” Likewise, someone praying for a bridegroom, “With harmony and sons,” means “May you wed accompanied by harmony and sons.” This interpretation is more idiomatic Arabic and better.

If you ask, “How, then, can God, blessed and exalted, say, ‘Seeking blessing through God’s name, I read’?” I answer: this is spoken upon the tongues of the servants, just as a person composes poetry in another’s voice. The same applies to “Praise belongs to God, Lord of the worlds” and what follows. Much of the Qurʾān follows this pattern: its meaning is to teach His servants how to seek blessing through His name and how to praise, glorify, and magnify Him.

وهو من الأسماء المحذوفة الأعجاز: كيد ودم، وأصله: سمو، بدليل تصريفه: كأسماء، وسمى، وسميت. واشتقاقه من السمو، لأن التسمية تنويه بالمسمى وإشادة بذكره، ومنه قيل للقب النبز: من النبز بمعنى النبر، وهو رفع الصوت. والنبز قشر النخلة الأعلى. فإن قلت: فلم حذفت الألف في الخط وأثبتت في قوله: باسم ربك؟ قلت: قد اتبعوا في حذفها حكم الدرج دون الابتداء الذي عليه وضع الخط لكثرة الاستعمال، وقالوا: طولت الباء تعويضا من طرح الألف. وعن عمر بن عبد العزيز أنه قال لكاتبه: طول الباء وأظهر السنات ودور الميم.

The bāʾ, and the form and spelling of ism [K-DKA I:4–5]

If you ask, “Single-letter particles of meaning ought to be built on fatḥa (فَتْحَة (ـَ)), the sister of sukūn (سُكُون (ـْ)), as are the kāf (كاف (ك)) of comparison, the lām (لام (ل)) of inception, the conjunctive wāw (واو (و)) and fāʾ (فاء (ف)), and others. Why, then, are the possessive lām (لام (ل)) and bāʾ (باء (ب)) built on kasra (كَسْرَة (ـِ))?” I answer: the lām (لام (ل)) is so marked to distinguish it from the lām (لام (ل)) of inception; the bāʾ (باء (ب)) because it invariably remains a particle and governs the genitive.

Ism (اسم), “name,” is one of the ten nouns whose beginnings the Arabs formed with sukūn (سُكُون (ـْ)). When they pronounce these at the beginning of speech, they add a hamza (همزة (ء)) to avoid beginning with an unvocalized consonant. Their practice is to begin with a vocalized consonant and pause on an unvocalized one, keeping their language free of every impediment and harshness and making it supremely well ordered and firm. When the word occurs in connected speech, no addition is needed. Some dispense with that addition and instead vocalize the unvocalized consonant, saying sim (سِم) or sum (سُم). The poet says:

In the name of Him whose name is in every sūrah.

It is among nouns whose final radical has been omitted, like yad, “hand,” and dam, “blood.” Its original form is simw [سِمْو], as its transformations demonstrate: asmāʾ (أسماء), “names”; sumayy (سُمَيّ), its diminutive; and sammaytu (سميت), “I named.” Its derivation is from sumuww (سمو), “elevation,” because naming gives prominence to the named thing and raises its mention. Similarly, a byname is called nabaz, from nabz (نبز) in the sense of nabr (نبر), raising the voice. Nibz is also the upper bark of a palm.

If you ask, “Why is the alif (ألف (ا)) omitted in writing here, but retained in ‘In the name of your Lord’?” I answer: because of its frequent use, its omission follows the rule for connected speech rather than initial pronunciation, although initial pronunciation is the basis of spelling. They also say that the bāʾ (باء (ب)) was lengthened to compensate for dropping the alif (ألف (ا)). It is related that ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz told his scribe: “Lengthen the bāʾ (باء (ب)), make the teeth clear, and round the mīm (ميم (م)).”

Allāh and ilāh [K-DKA I:5–6]

The original form of Allāh (الله) is al-ilāh (الإله). The poet says:

God forbid that you should be like a gazelle.

A parallel is al-nās (الناس), whose original form is al-unās (الأناس). The poet says:

Deaths come upon people living in security.

The hamza (همزة (ء)) was omitted, and the definite article substituted for it. Accordingly, in direct address one says yā Allāh (الله), with the initial hamza (همزة (ء)) pronounced, just as one says yā ilāh (إله).

Ilāh (إله) is a generic noun, like “man” and “horse”: it applies to every object of worship, rightly or wrongly worshipped. It then became predominant for the One rightly worshipped. Similarly, al-najm (النجم) is the name of any star, but became predominant for the Pleiades; al-sana (السنة), “the year,” for a year of drought; al-bayt (البيت), “the house,” for the Kaʿba; and al-kitāb (الكتاب), “the book,” for Sībawayh’s book. Allāh (الله), with the hamza (همزة (ء)) omitted, is exclusively applied to the One rightly worshipped and has not been used of another.

From this noun were derived taʾallaha (تألّه), allaha, and istaʾlaha (استأله) (تألّه، ألّه، استأله), just as istanwaqa (استنوق) and istaḥjara (استحجر) were derived from nāqa (ناقة), “she-camel,” and ḥajar (حجر), “stone.”

If you ask, “Is it a noun or an adjective?” I answer: a noun, not an adjective. You qualify it with an adjective but do not use it to qualify something else. You do not say “an ilāh (إله) thing,” just as you do not say “a man thing.” You say “one ilāh (إله), a ṣamad (صمد),” just as you say “a noble, good man.” Moreover, His attributes, exalted is He, must have a subject of which they are predicated. If all of them were made adjectives, they would remain without a noun that they qualify, which is impossible.

If you ask, “Does this noun have a derivation?” I answer: derivation means that two or more forms are united by a single meaning. The form of this noun and the form of their expression aliha (أَلِهَ), “he was bewildered,” together with its relatives daliha (دَلِهَ) and ʿaliha (عَلِهَ), are united by the meaning of bewilderment and amazement. Conceptions are bewildered in knowing the One worshipped, and discerning minds are amazed. For this reason error has multiplied, falsehood has spread, and sound reflection is scarce.

If you ask, “Is its lām (لام (ل)) pronounced emphatically?” I answer: yes. Al-Zajjāj has stated that its emphatic pronunciation (tafkhīm (تفخيم)) is a received practice. All the Arabs follow it, and their agreement proves that they inherited it from one generation to another.

This is a light conveyance, without the weight of Iraqi camel litters. On the road to al-Ṭāʾif I asked one of them, “What is this litter called?”—meaning the Iraqi litter. He said, “Is not that one called the shuqduf (شقدف)?” “Yes,” I answered. “This one,” he said, “is called the shuqandāf (شقنداف).” He increased the structure of the name for the increase in the thing named.

It has not been used of anyone other than God, mighty and exalted, just as Allāh (الله) is among predominant nouns. As for Banū Ḥanīfa calling Musaylima “Raḥmān (رحمن) of al-Yamāma,” and their poet saying of him,

You are the rain of humankind; may you remain a raḥmān (رحمن),

this is an instance of their obstinacy in unbelief.

If you ask, “When you say ‘Allāh (الله) is raḥmān (رحمن),’ do you make it fully declinable or not?” I answer: I compare it to its fellows in the same category—ʿaṭshān (عطشان), “thirsty,” gharthān (غرثان), “hungry,” sakrān (سكران), “drunk”—and do not make it fully declinable.

Its exclusive application to God prevents its having such a feminine; why deny it full declension?” I answer: just as that exclusiveness prevents a feminine on faʿlā (فَعْلى), like ʿaṭshā (عطشى), it also prevents a feminine on faʿlāna (فَعْلانة), like nadmāna (ندمانة). The absence of a feminine caused by this subsequent exclusiveness is therefore immaterial. One must return to its original status before that exclusiveness, comparing it to its counterparts.

If you ask, “What does it mean to describe God Most High by mercy (raḥma (رحمة)), when that means tenderness and compassionate inclination, and raḥim (رَحِم), ‘womb,’ is related to it because it curves around what is inside?” I answer: this is a metaphor for His bestowing favours upon His servants. When a king inclines compassionately towards his subjects and feels tenderness for them, he reaches them with kindness and favour; when harshness and cruelty seize him, he treats them roughly and withholds his goodness and kindness.

If you ask, “Why put the more intensive of the two descriptions before the less intensive, when the normal progression is from lower to higher—as in ‘so-and-so is learned, consummately learned; brave, valiant; generous, abundantly giving’?” I answer: when He says al-Raḥmān (الرحمن), encompassing great and mighty blessings and their foundations, He follows it with al-Raḥīm (الرحيم), as a completion and supplement, to encompass their small and subtle forms.

Praise and gratitude [K-DKA I:8–9]

“Praise belongs to God, Lord of the worlds, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.”

Ḥamd (حمد), “praise,” and madḥ (مدح), “commendation,” are closely related: they are the act of praising and proclaiming what is fine, whether a favour or something else. You say, “I praised the man for his beneficence,” and “I praised him for his lineage and courage.”

Shukr (شكر), “gratitude,” concerns a favour specifically, and is expressed through the heart, the tongue, and the bodily members. The poet says:

Your favour has gained you three things from me: my hand, my tongue, and my hidden heart.

Ḥamd (حمد) is expressed by the tongue alone and is therefore one branch of shukr (شكر). Hence the Prophet’s saying—peace upon him—“Praise is the head of gratitude. A servant who does not praise God has not thanked Him.” He made it gratitude’s head because mentioning a favour with the tongue and praising its giver spreads knowledge of it more widely and indicates its standing more clearly than inward belief and the conduct of the limbs. The heart’s action is hidden; bodily action admits of different interpretations. The tongue’s action is different: it is speech, which makes every hidden thing explicit and clarifies every ambiguity.

The opposite of ḥamd (حمد) is dhamm (ذم), “blame”; the opposite of shukr (شكر) is kufrān (كفران), “ingratitude.”

Case, definiteness, and readings of al-ḥamd [K-DKA I:9–10]

Al-ḥamdu (الْحَمْدُ) is nominative because it is the subject of a nominal sentence (mubtadaʾ (مبتدأ)); its predicate is the adverbial phrase li-llāh (لِلَّه), “to God.” Its underlying form is accusative, as some read it, with an implied governing verb. It belongs to the verbal nouns that the Arabs put in the accusative through implicit verbs to make statements, as in shukran (شكرًا), “thanks,” kufran (كفرًا), “ingratitude,” ʿajaban (عجبًا), “wonder,” and the like, including subḥānaka (سبحانك), “glory be to You,” and maʿādha (معاذ) llāh, “God forbid.” They put these expressions in the place of their verbs and let them serve their function. They consequently do not use the verbs alongside them, treating such usage like an abrogated law.

The change from accusative to nominative as the subject of a nominal sentence indicates the meaning’s permanence and stability. An example is His saying, “They said, ‘Peace!’ He said, ‘Peace!’” [11:69]. The second “peace” is nominative, indicating that Abraham—peace upon him—greeted them with a greeting better than theirs: the nominative signifies peace established for them, rather than peace newly occurring.

The meaning is “We praise God with praise.” Accordingly, “You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help” follows, explaining their praise of Him. It is as though it had been asked, “How do you praise?” and the answer were, “You alone we worship.”

If you ask, “What does the definite article signify here?” I answer: it resembles the definite article in “He sent them to the jostling [at the watering place]” (arsalahā al-ʿirāk (أرسلها العراك)). It specifies the genus: pointing to what everyone recognizes as what praise is, and what jostling is, among the kinds of action. The exhaustive inclusion that many people imagine is a mistaken assumption on their part.

Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī read al-ḥamdi (الْحَمْدِ) li-llāh (لِلَّه), putting kasra (كَسْرَة (ـِ)) on the dāl (دال (د)) to assimilate it to the lām (لام (ل)). Ibrāhīm ibn Abī ʿAbla read al-ḥamdu (الْحَمْدُ) lu-llāh (لُلَّه), putting ḍamma (ضَمَّة (ـُ)) on the lām (لام (ل)) to assimilate it to the dāl (دال (د)). What emboldened them to do this—although such vowel assimilation normally occurs within a single word, as in their expressions munḥadur (مُنْحَدُر) al-jabal and Mughīra—was that the two words, so frequently used together, came to occupy the position of a single word. Ibrāhīm’s reading is the better of the two, since he made a fixed vowel follow an inflectional vowel, which is stronger, unlike al-Ḥasan’s reading. Rabb and the worlds [K-DKA I:10–11]

Al-rabb (الرب) means “the owner.” An example is Ṣafwān’s saying to Abū Sufyān: “I would rather have a man of Quraysh rule me than a man of Hawāzin.” You say rabbahu (رَبَّه), yarubbuhu (يَرُبُّه), fa-huwa rabb (رب), “he ruled him, he rules him, so he is a lord,” just as you say namma (نَمَّ) ʿalayhi, yanimmu, fa-huwa namm (نَمّ), “he informed against him, he informs against him, so he is an informer.”

It may also be a verbal noun used adjectivally for intensification, as ʿadl (عَدْل), “justice,” is used as an adjective [meaning thoroughly just]. They use al-Rabb (الرب) without qualification for God alone. Of others it is restricted by a possessive construction, as in “master of the house” or “owner of the she-camel,” and His sayings “Return to your master” [12:50] and “He is my master, who has made my lodging good” [12:23].

Zayd ibn ʿAlī—God be pleased with them both—read rabba l-ʿālamīn in the accusative as an expression of praise. It is also said that the accusative is governed by what “Praise belongs to God” implies, as though the wording were “We praise God, Lord of the worlds.”

Al-ʿālam (العالَم) is a name for beings possessed of knowledge: angels and the two weighty kinds, humans and jinn. It is also said to mean everything through which the Creator is known, whether bodies or accidents (aʿrāḍ (أعراض)).

If you ask, “Why is it plural?” I answer: to include every kind to which the name applies.

If you ask, “It is a noun, not an adjective, whereas the plural with wāw (واو (و)) and nūn (نون (ن)) is made only from adjectives of rational beings or proper names treated similarly,” I answer: this is permissible because it contains an adjectival meaning, namely the indication of knowledge.

“Master of the Day of Recompense.”

It has been read malik (مَلِك) yawmi l-dīn, “King of the Day of Recompense”; mālik (مَالِك), “Master”; and malk (مَلْك), with the lām (لام (ل)) lightened [unvocalized]. Abū Ḥanīfa—God be pleased with him—read malaka (مَلَكَ) yawma l-dīn, with a verbal form and “day” in the accusative [“He possessed [authority on] the Day of Recompense”]. Malik (مَلِك) is the preferred reading, because it is the reading of the people of the two sanctuaries, and because of His sayings “To whom belongs kingship today?” [40:16] and “King of humankind” [114:2], and because ownership (milk, مِلْك) is general, whereas kingship (mulk, مُلْك) is particular.

The Day of Dīn (دين) is the Day of Recompense. Hence their saying, “As you deal, so shall you be dealt with,” and the line in the Ḥamāsa:

Nothing remained but aggression: we dealt with them as they had dealt with us.

If you ask, “What is this possessive construction?” I answer: it is the addition of an active participle to an adverbial expression, through an extension of usage that treats the latter like a direct object. Compare their saying, “O you who robbed the household on the night!” (yā sāriqa l-laylati ahla l-dār): the meaning remains temporal. The sense here is “Master of the whole affair on the Day of Recompense,” like His saying “To whom belongs kingship today?” [40:16].

How an active participle can qualify the definite divine name [K-JK I:55; T-DUB I:737–741; the end survives in K-DKA I:13]

If you ask, “An active participle’s possessive construction is not a true possessive construction and does not impart definiteness. How, then, may it qualify a definite noun?” I answer: it is not a true possessive construction only when the active participle is intended in the present or future, so that its elements are understood as separable: for example, “owning now,” or “tomorrow.”

But if a past meaning is intended, as in “He owned his slave yesterday,” or continuous time, as in “Zayd is an owner of slaves,” the construction is a true one, like “master of slaves.” That is the meaning in “Master of the Day of Recompense.” The meaning may also be “He possessed the affairs on the Day of Recompense,” as in “The inhabitants of the Garden called out” [7:44] and “The people of the Heights called out” [7:48]. Abū Ḥanīfa’s reading malaka (مَلَكَ) yawma l-dīn supports this.

These descriptions applied to God—glory be to Him—state that He is Lord and Owner of the worlds, with nothing in them outside His sovereignty and lordship; that He bestows every blessing, outward and inward, great and small; and that He owns the whole affair at the last, on the Day of Reward and Punishment. Coming after “Praise belongs to God” has indicated that praise belongs exclusively to Him and that He deserves it, these descriptions demonstrate that no one could be more entitled to praise and to being extolled as He deserves than One whose attributes these are.

(إيا) ضمير منفصل للمنصوب، واللواحق التي تلحقه من الكاف والهاء والياء في قولك: إياك، وإياه، وإياى، لبيان الخطاب والغيبة والتكلم، ولا محل لها من الإعراب، كما لا محل للكاف في أرأيتك، وليست بأسماء مضمرة، وهو مذهب الأخفش وعليه المحققون، وأما ما حكاه الخليل عن بعض العرب: «إذا بلغ الرجل الستين فإياه وإيا الشواب» فشيء شاذ لا يعول عليه، وتقديم المفعول لقصد الاختصاص، كقوله تعالى: (قل أفغير الله تأمروني أعبد) ، (قل أغير الله أبغي ربا) . والمعنى نخصك بالعبادة، ونخصك بطلب المعونة. وقرئ: إياك بتخفيف الياء، وأياك بفتح الهمزة والتشديد، وهياك بقلب الهمزة هاء. قال طفيل الغنوي:

والعبادة أقصى غاية الخضوع والتذلل. ومنه ثوب ذو عبدة إذا كان في غاية الصفاقة وقوة النسج، ولذلك لم تستعمل إلا في الخضوع لله تعالى، لأنه مولى أعظم النعم فكان حقيقا بأقصى غاية الخضوع.

Iyyāka and exclusive worship [K-DKA I:13]

“You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help.”

Iyyā (إِيَّا) is an independent accusative pronoun. The suffixes attached to it—the kāf (كاف (ك)), hāʾ (هاء (ه)), and yāʾ (ياء (ي)) in iyyāka (إِيَّاكَ), iyyāhu (إِيَّاهُ), and iyyāya (إِيَّايَ)—indicate second, third, and first person. They occupy no syntactic case position, just as the kāf (كاف (ك)) in araʾaytaka (أرأيتك) does not. They are not pronominal nouns. This is al-Akhfash’s position, adopted by the exacting scholars.

As for al-Khalīl’s report that some Arabs say, “When a man reaches sixty, let him beware of young women” (fa-iyyāhu wa-iyyā l-shawābb), this is an anomalous form on which one should not rely.

The object is placed first to convey exclusiveness, as in His sayings “Say: Is it other than God that you command me to worship?” [39:64] and “Say: Shall I seek a lord other than God?” [6:164]. The meaning is “We single You out for worship, and single You out in seeking assistance.”

It has also been read iyāka (إِيَاكَ), with the yāʾ (ياء (ي)) lightened; ayyāka (أَيَّاكَ), with fatḥa (فَتْحَة (ـَ)) on the hamza (همزة (ء)) and a doubled yāʾ (ياء (ي)); and hiyyāka (هِيَّاكَ), with the hamza (همزة (ء)) changed to hāʾ (هاء (ه)). Ṭufayl al-Ghanawī said:

Beware of the affair whose ways in, though spacious, leave its ways out too narrow for you.

Worship (ʿibāda (عبادة)) is the utmost degree of submission and abasement. Related to it is thawb dhū ʿabda (عَبْد), a cloth of extreme thickness and strength of weave. Hence the word is used only for submission to God Most High, since He bestows the greatest blessings and therefore deserves the utmost submission.

The turn from third person to direct address [K-DKA I:13–14]

If you ask, “Why turn from third-person expression to direct address?” I answer: in the science of eloquence this is called iltifāt (التفات). It can run from third person to direct address, from direct address to third person, and from third person to first person, as in His sayings “Until, when you are in the ships and they sail with them” [10:22] and “God is He who sent the winds, which raise a cloud, and We drove it” [35:9].

Imruʾ al-Qays made three turns in three lines:

Your night at al-Ithmid grew long; the carefree sleeper slept, while you did not.

He passed the night, and his night endured, like the night of one with an inflamed, afflicted eye.

That was because of news that reached me, which I was told concerning Abū al-Aswad.

This accords with their habit of varying speech and employing it in different ways. Moving speech from one mode to another better refreshes the listener’s attention and rouses attentive listening than continuing in a single mode. Particular instances may have particular benefits.

The benefit peculiar to this passage is that, after the One deserving praise has been mentioned and these mighty attributes applied to Him, knowledge has attached itself to an object of immense standing: One worthy of praise, utmost submission, and recourse for assistance in important affairs. That known One, distinguished by these attributes, is then addressed: “You—O One whose attributes these are—we single out for worship and assistance. We worship no other and seek no other’s help.” The direct address thus indicates more clearly that worship is His because of this distinguishing character, without which no one deserves worship.

Worship and asking for help [K-DKA I:14–15]

If you ask, “Why associate asking for help with worship?” I answer: to bring together that through which the servants draw near to their Lord and that which they seek and need from Him.

If you ask, “Why put worship before asking for help?” I answer: because presenting the means of approach before requesting the need makes them deserving of a favourable response.

If you ask, “Why leave the request for help unrestricted?” I answer: to include everything in which help is sought. Better still is to intend seeking His help and His granting of success (tawfīq (توفيق)) in performing worship. “Guide us” would then explain the assistance requested, as though He had asked, “How shall I help you?” and they answered, “Guide us on the straight path.” This is better because the discourse coheres, each part taking hold of the next.

Ibn Ḥubaysh [Zirr ibn Ḥubaysh in the Turkish identification, p.90] read nistaʿīn (نِسْتَعِين), with kasra (كَسْرَة (ـِ)) on the nūn (نون (ن)).

Guidance and the straight path [K-DKA I:15]

“Guide us on the straight path.”

Hadā (هدى) originally takes its complement with lām (لام (ل)) or ilā, as in His sayings “This Qurʾān guides to that which is most upright” [17:9] and “You surely guide to a straight path” [42:52]. Here it is treated like ikhtāra (اختار), “choose,” in “Moses chose his people” [7:155; that is, chose from among them].

To ask for guidance when they are already guided means asking for increased guidance through the bestowal of gracious aids (alṭāf (ألطاف)), as in His sayings “Those who are guided, He increases in guidance” [47:17] and “Those who strive for Our sake, We shall surely guide upon Our ways” [29:69]. It is related from ʿAlī and Ubayy—God be pleased with them both—that “Guide us” means “Make us steadfast.” Command and supplication have a single form, since each is a request; they differ only in rank. ʿAbd Allāh (الله) read arshidnā (أرشدنا), “Direct us rightly.”

Al-sirāṭ (السراط) means the main road, from saraṭa (سَرَطَ) a thing, “swallowed it,” since the road swallows travellers when they enter it. Likewise it is called laqam because it gulps them down. Ṣirāṭ (صراط) results from changing the sīn (سين (س)) to ṣād (صاد (ص)) because of the ṭāʾ (طاء (ط)), like muṣayṭir (مُصَيْطِر) for musayṭir (مُسَيْطِر). The ṣād (صاد (ص)) may also be tinged with the sound of zāy (زاي (ز)). All these forms have been read. The most eloquent is pure ṣād (صاد (ص)), the dialect of Quraysh and the form established in the authoritative codex (al-imām). Its plural is suruṭ (سُرُط), like kitāb (كتاب) and kutub. It may be masculine or feminine, like ṭarīq (طريق) and sabīl (سبيل). What is intended is the path of truth, the religion of Islam.

Why repeat “path”? [K-DKA I:15–16]

“The path of those upon whom You have bestowed favour, not those who have incurred wrath, nor those astray.”

“The path of those upon whom You have bestowed favour” is a substitute (badal) for “the straight path.” A substitution has the force of repeating the governing word, as though it said, “Guide us on the straight path; guide us on the path of those upon whom You have bestowed favour.” Compare “those who were deemed weak,” followed by “those of them who believed” [7:75].

If you ask, “What benefit does the substitution have? Why not simply say ‘Guide us on the path of those upon whom You have bestowed favour’?” I answer: its benefit is emphasis, through the doubling and repetition it contains, and the suggestion that the explanation and interpretation of “the straight path” is the path of the Muslims. This bears witness to the straightness of the Muslims’ path in the strongest and most emphatic way.

Compare saying, “Shall I point you to the most generous and excellent of people? So-and-so.” This describes his generosity and excellence more forcefully than “Shall I point you to so-and-so, the most generous and excellent?” You have mentioned him twice, first generally and then specifically. You have made “so-and-so” the explanation and clarification of “the most generous and excellent,” establishing him as a sign of generosity and excellence. It is as though you said, “Whoever seeks a man combining both qualities should turn to so-and-so: he is the particular, designated person in whom both meet, without dispute or challenge.”

“Those upon whom You have bestowed favour” are the believers. Favour is left unrestricted to include every favour, since whoever receives the blessing of Islam has no blessing left that does not reach and encompass him. It is related from Ibn ʿAbbās that they are Moses’ companions before they changed. It is also said that they are the prophets. Ibn Masʿūd read ṣirāṭa (صراطَ) man anʿamta ʿalayhim, using man, “those whom.”

Ghayr and those who incurred wrath [K-DKA I:16–17]

“Not those who have incurred wrath” (ghayri (غَيْرِ) l-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim) is a substitute for “those upon whom You have bestowed favour.” The sense is that those favoured are those who have been kept safe from God’s wrath and from error. Alternatively, it is an adjective: they combine unrestricted favour—the favour of faith—with safety from God’s wrath and error.

If you ask, “How can ghayr (غير) qualify a definite expression when it does not become definite even when annexed to definite nouns?” I answer: “those upon whom You have bestowed favour” does not designate a fixed set, as in the poet’s saying:

I may indeed pass by the base man as he reviles me.

Moreover, those who incurred wrath and those astray are the opposite of those favoured. Ghayr (غير) therefore does not have here the indeterminacy that prevents it from becoming definite.

It has also been read in the accusative as a circumstantial expression (ḥāl). This is the reading of God’s Messenger—God bless him and grant him peace—and of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, and it is also related from Ibn Kathīr. The bearer of the circumstance is the pronoun in ʿalayhim, “upon them”; its governing verb is anʿamta, “You bestowed favour.”

It is said that those who incurred wrath are the Jews, because of His saying, mighty and exalted, “those whom God cursed and against whom He was angry” [5:60]; and that those astray are the Christians, because of His saying, “who had gone astray before” [5:77].

If you ask, “What does God’s wrath mean?” I answer: the will to exact retribution from the disobedient and to send punishment down upon them, and to do to them what a king does when angry with those under his power. We seek refuge in God from His wrath, and ask Him for His good pleasure and mercy.

If you ask, “What is the difference between the first ʿalayhim and the second?” I answer: the first occupies an accusative position as an object; the second occupies a nominative position as the subject [of the passive expression al-maghḍūb (المغضوب)].

Negation and a variant pronunciation [K-DKA I:17]

If you ask, “Why does lā (لا) enter in wa-lā (ولا) l-ḍāllīn, ‘nor those astray’?” I answer: because ghayr (غير) contains the meaning of negation. It is as though the wording were “neither those who incurred wrath nor those astray.”

You can say anā (أنا) Zaydan ghayru ḍārib (ضارب), “I am not striking Zayd,” although you cannot say anā (أنا) Zaydan mithlu ḍārib (ضارب), “I am like one striking Zayd,” with that word order. The former is equivalent to anā (أنا) Zaydan lā (لا) ḍārib (ضارب), “I am not striking Zayd.”

It is related that ʿUmar and ʿAlī—God be pleased with them both—read wa-ghayri l-ḍāllīn, “and not those astray.” Ayyūb al-Sakhtiyānī read wa-lā (ولا) l-ḍaʾllīn, with a hamza (همزة (ء)), just as ʿAmr ibn ʿUbayd read wa-lā (ولا) jaʾnn (جَأْنّ) [55:39]. This is the speech of someone who goes to great lengths to avoid two unvocalized consonants meeting. Similar examples are Abū Zayd’s report of their saying shaʾbba (شَأْبّة) and daʾbba (دَأْبّة).

Āmīn [K-DKA I:17–18]

Āmīn (آمين) is a sound made the name of the verb istajib (استجب), “Respond,” just as ruwayd (رويد), ḥayyahal (حيهل), and halumma (هلمّ) are sounds made the names of the verbs “Give respite,” “Hasten,” and “Come.”

It is related from Ibn ʿAbbās: “I asked God’s Messenger—God bless him and grant him peace—what Āmīn (آمين) meant, and he said, ‘Do [it].’” It has two pronunciations, with its alif (ألف (ا)) long or short. A poet said:

May God have mercy upon a servant who says “Āmīn (آمين).”

Another said:

“Amīn (أمين)”—and may God increase the distance between us.

It is related from the Prophet—God bless him and grant him peace—“Gabriel, peace upon him, taught me to say Āmīn (آمين) when I finished reciting the Opening of the Book, and said, ‘It is like the seal upon a letter.’” It is not part of the Qurʾān, as shown by its not having been entered into the codices.

It is related from al-Ḥasan that the imam does not say it, since he is the one making the supplication. The same is related from Abū Ḥanīfa—God have mercy upon him—but the well-known report from him and his companions is that he says it quietly. ʿAbd Allāh (الله) ibn Mughaffal and Anas related quiet pronunciation from God’s Messenger—God bless him and grant him peace. Al-Shāfiʿī holds that it is pronounced aloud. Wāʾil ibn Ḥujr related that when the Prophet—God bless him and grant him peace—recited “nor those astray,” he would say Āmīn (آمين) and raise his voice with it.

Closing reports [K-DKA I:18–19]

It is related that God’s Messenger—God bless him and grant him peace—said to Ubayy ibn Kaʿb: “Shall I tell you of a sūrah whose like has not been revealed in the Torah, the Gospel, or the Qurʾān?” “Yes, Messenger of God,” I replied. He said, “The Opening of the Book: it is the seven oft-repeated verses and the mighty Qurʾān that I have been given.”

It is related from Ḥudhayfa ibn al-Yamān that the Prophet—God bless him and grant him peace—said: “God may send punishment upon a people, decisively decreed; then a child among them recites in the elementary school, ‘Praise belongs to God, Lord of the worlds.’ God Most High hears him and thereby lifts the punishment from them for forty years.”
