Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK094.01

Heading and verses 1–4

Meccan; eight verses. [K-DKA adds: revealed after al-Ḍuḥā.] In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful. “Have We not expanded your breast for you, and lifted from you your burden, which made your back creak, and raised your renown for you?”

Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK094.02

Expansion and the burden

The question repudiates the absence of expansion, and thus affirms and establishes that the expansion occurred. It is as though He had said, “We expanded your breast for you.” Accordingly, “and We lifted” is coordinated with it in consideration of its meaning. “We expanded your breast” means: We made it spacious enough to accommodate the concerns of prophethood and the calling of both classes of accountable beings, humans and jinn, together; or spacious enough to bear the hardships with which the unbelievers of your people and others confront you; or We expanded it by the knowledge and wisdom We deposited in it, removing the constriction and distress accompanying blindness and ignorance. Al-Ḥasan said: “It was filled with wisdom and knowledge.” Abū Jaʿfar al-Manṣūr is reported to have read a-lam nashraḥa laka, giving the ḥāʾ a fatḥa. They said: perhaps he articulated the ḥāʾ distinctly and fully at its point of articulation, so that the listener thought he had pronounced a fatḥa. The burden that made his back creak—that is, caused it to emit naqīḍ, the sound of creaking and coming apart because of its weight—is a figure for what weighed upon God's Messenger and distressed him: lapses before prophethood; or his lack of knowledge of rulings and revealed laws; or his desperate eagerness for the obstinate among his people to accept Islam and his grief over them. Lifting it from him means forgiving him; or teaching him the revealed laws; or establishing his excuse after he had conveyed the message and exerted himself fully in conveying it. Anas read “and We loosened” (wa-ḥalalnā), and “and We laid down” (wa-ḥaṭaṭnā). Ibn Masʿūd read: “and We loosened from you your heavy load” (wa-ḥalalnā ʿanka wiqraka).

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT094.01

The force of the question (XVI, 492)

On K094.02

On “it affirms and establishes the expansion”: He repudiates the absence of expansion. Once that absence is repudiated, expansion is established, because the interrogative hamza expresses repudiation; repudiation is negation, and negation applied to negation becomes affirmation. The hamza may not be treated here as a question seeking acknowledgment (taqrīr).

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT094.02

The breadth of the Prophet's breast (492–493)

On K094.02

On “We made it spacious enough to accommodate the concerns of prophethood and the calling of both classes of beings”: if you ask why he explains expansion of the breast here more comprehensively and expansively than at “He said: My Lord, expand my breast for me” (20:25), where he says, “When he was commanded to go to Pharaoh the tyrant, he knew that he had been charged with a mighty undertaking and a tremendous affair, requiring endurance possible only for someone with a steadfast heart and an expansive breast; he therefore asked his Lord to grant him expansion of his breast”—I answer: concerns are proportionate to aspirations. How well al-Ṣāḥib said: “A woman said: Why have anxieties assailed you, when your command is obeyed among the nations? I said: Leave me with the grief that chokes me; anxieties are proportionate to aspirations.” Every situation calls for its own discourse. When the one to whom God spoke was sent to Pharaoh the tyrant, he requested expansion, as God says: “Go to Pharaoh: he has exceeded all bounds. He said: My Lord, expand my breast for me” (20:24–25). But when the Beloved was summoned to the station of “two bow-lengths, or nearer” (53:9), he was told, “Have We not expanded your breast for you?” This will appear in the report of Mālik ibn Ṣaʿṣaʿa. Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq said: “Have We not expanded your breast for you to witness Me and behold Me?” Ibn ʿAṭāʾ said: “Have We not emptied your innermost consciousness of everything, so that you ceased to behold created existence and whatever is other than the Real? Your breast was expanded for vision, whereas Moses' breast was expanded for speech.” Sahl said: “Have We not broadened your breast with the light of messengership, making it a repository of realities?”

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT094.03

Opening the breast; the root meaning of sharḥ (493–494)

On K094.02

On al-Ḥasan's saying, “It was filled with wisdom and knowledge”: perhaps he alludes to what we have transmitted from al-Bukhārī, Muslim, al-Tirmidhī, and al-Nasāʾī, from Mālik ibn Ṣaʿṣaʿa, from the Prophet: “While I was beside the House, between sleeping and waking, a basin of gold containing Zamzam water was brought to me, and my breast was opened as far as such-and-such.” Qatāda said—addressing Anas—“What does he mean?” He replied: “To the bottom of my belly.” He continued: “My heart was taken out and washed with Zamzam water, then put back in its place, then filled with faith and wisdom. Then an animal smaller than a mule and larger than a donkey was brought to me”—the lengthy report continues. The Imām [Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī] said: “It is not unlikely that the black blood they washed from his heart was a sign of inclination and readiness to commit sins, and reluctance to perform acts of obedience. Once they removed it, that was a sign that its possessor would persevere in obedience and guard against evil deeds. God does whatever He wills and decrees whatever He wishes.” Al-Rāghib said: “The original sense of sharḥ is spreading out flesh and the like. One says sharaḥtu al-laḥma and sharraḥtuhu. From this comes expansion of the breast: its spreading out through divine light, tranquility from God, and a spirit from Him.”

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT094.04

The reading nashraḥa (494–495)

On K094.02

On “he read a-lam nashraḥa, with fatḥa on the ḥāʾ”: its original form is nashraḥan. The nūn was omitted and the fatḥa on the ḥāʾ retained as an indication of it. This is in al-Muntaqā. Ibn Jinnī said: “A-lam nashraḥa, with fatḥa on the ḥāʾ, is transmitted from Abū Jaʿfar al-Manṣūr. Ibn Mujāhid said: ‘This is wholly impermissible.’” Ibn Jinnī said: “The obvious position, and what accords with customary usage, is as Ibn Mujāhid states. Something like it nevertheless occurs in what I read to Abū ʿAlī in Abū Zayd's Nawādir: ‘From which of death's two days shall I flee— a day not decreed, or a day decreed?’ It is said that he intended lam yuqdar-an, with the light emphatic nūn. In our view omitting it is impermissible, because the emphatic nūn is most closely allied to expansion and elaboration, not concision and abbreviation. In Abū Zayd's Nawādir there is another verse, said to be fabricated: ‘Strike away the anxieties that visit you by night, as with a sword you strike a horse's forelock.’ He intended iḍrib-an, with the light nūn, and omitted it.”

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT094.05

The burden and the ordered correspondence of its meanings (495)

On K094.02

On “the sound of creaking and coming apart”: al-Ṣiḥāḥ says: “Anqaḍa al-ḥimlu ẓahrahu means that the load weighed upon his back. Its original sense concerns sound. Naqīḍ is the sound of camel-litters and riding gear.” Al-Rāghib says: “‘It made his back creak’ means that it broke it until it produced a creaking sound; the naqīḍ of joints is their sound. ‘Back’ is a metaphor, likening sins to a load that weighs its bearer down.” On “lifting it from him means forgiving him”: the expression consists of a subject and predicate. This sentence is coordinated with a corresponding sentence, “the burden is a figure”—that is, a metaphor preceded by a comparison. “And We lifted” is then a reinforcement of the metaphor (tarshīḥ), because it is a description appropriate to that from which the metaphor is drawn. This is what he means by “lifting it from him means forgiving him,” and the rest. When “burden” is borrowed for sin, its reinforcement is appropriately understood as forgiveness. When it is borrowed for ignorance of rulings, the fitting explanation is instruction in the revealed laws. When it is understood as his desperate eagerness for their acceptance of Islam, the corresponding interpretation is the establishment of his excuse: do not be consumed by eagerness for their guidance or let yourself perish in grief over them, for you have exerted yourself in conveying the message and have established the proof against them. This is the rhetorical pattern of gathering and ordered distribution (laff wa-nashr).

Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK094.03

Raising his renown; clarification after an initial general expression

Raising his renown consists in joining his mention to God's in the testimony of faith, the call to prayer, the announcement that prayer is beginning, the tashahhud, and sermons; and in more than one place in the Qurʾān: “God and His Messenger have the greater right that they should please Him” (9:62); “whoever obeys God and His Messenger” (4:13); and “obey God and obey the Messenger” (5:92). It also consists in calling him “God's Messenger” and “God's Prophet.” His mention in the scriptures of earlier peoples, and the pledge taken from the prophets and their communities that they would believe in him, belong to this as well. If you ask what benefit there is in adding “for you” (laka), when the meaning stands without it, I answer: the addition achieves what is achieved by initially leaving something unspecified and then clarifying it. It is as though He said, “Have We not expanded for you…?” One understands that something has been expanded. He then says, “your breast,” clarifying what had been understood only generally. The same applies to “for you your renown” and “from you your burden.”

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT094.06

Readings, renown, and the prophetic covenant (496)

On K094.03

On “Anas read wa-ḥalalnā and wa-ḥaṭaṭnā”: Ibn Jinnī reports that Abān said: “I said to Anas, ‘Abū Ḥamza, what of wa-waḍaʿnā?’ He said: ‘Waḍaʿnā, ḥalalnā, and ḥaṭaṭnā are equivalent. Gabriel came to the Prophet and said: Recite according to seven aḥruf, provided you do not mix forgiveness with punishment or punishment with forgiveness.’” I say: a lengthy report from Anas occurs in Muslim, al-Tirmidhī, Abū Dāwūd, and al-Nasāʾī, ending: “Then he said: Each of them is healing and sufficient—whether you say ‘All-Hearing, All-Knowing’ or ‘Mighty, Wise’—provided you do not conclude a verse of punishment with mercy or a verse of mercy with punishment.” [These are Ṭībī's transmitted attributions.] On “calling him God's Messenger and God's Prophet”: Jaʿfar said: “No one mentions you as messenger without mentioning Me as Lord.” Ibn ʿAṭāʾ said: “I made faith in Me complete through mentioning you together with Me.” On “the pledge taken from the prophets and their communities that they would believe in him”: perhaps he means what is indicated by “When God took the covenant of the prophets: Whatever scripture and wisdom I give you, then a messenger comes to you confirming what is with you, you shall believe in him and you shall support him” (3:81).

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT094.07

The added laka and the connection with consolation (497)

On K094.03

On “the addition of ‘for you’”: the author said: “Laka may be added to express particularization, as in ‘You alone we worship’ (1:5), although the meaning is complete with ‘we worship You’; or it may be a matter of giving priority to what is more important.” Al-Sayyid Ibn al-Shajarī says in al-Amālī: “The lām in laka is causal, as when you say, ‘I did that to honor you.’ If you omit the lām, you say, ‘I did it, honoring you.’ If you omit the verbal noun, you restore the lām and say, ‘I did that for you.’ Thus the meaning is: Have We not expanded your breast for your guidance? Compare: ‘Whomever God wishes to guide, He expands his breast for Islam’ (6:125). Once the verbal noun is omitted, the lām must be expressed. Likewise, ‘We raised your renown for you’ means: We raised your renown to honor you.” On “the idolaters used to taunt”: his explanation amounts to this. The occasion of revelation of “Have We not expanded your breast for you?” was that the idolaters taunted God's Messenger and the believers for poverty, causing him concern. This was dispelled by “Have We not expanded your breast for you?” The question repudiates the denial of expansion, thereby emphatically establishing it: have you not seen what God did for you at the outset—expanding your breast and raising your renown—when you knew nothing of what you know now and your name was then obscure? We did for you what We did. Judge what is to come by that, and do not worry about their taunting you and the believers for poverty, for with hardship comes ease.

Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK094.04

Verses 5–6: hardship and ease

“For with hardship comes ease; indeed, with hardship comes ease.” If you ask how “for with hardship comes ease” connects with what precedes, I answer: the idolaters used to taunt God's Messenger and the believers for their poverty and straitened circumstances, until it occurred to him that their aversion to Islam arose from its adherents' poverty and their contempt for them. God reminded him of the great blessings He had bestowed upon him, then said, “For with hardship comes ease.” It is as though He said: We have granted you all that We have granted you, so do not despair of God's bounty; with the hardship in which you now find yourselves comes ease. If you ask: maʿa means accompaniment; how can ease and hardship accompany one another? I answer: He means that God will bring them ease a short time after the hardship in which they were living. He brings the anticipated ease so near that He makes it seem simultaneous with the hardship, to give further consolation and strengthen their hearts.

Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK094.05

“One hardship will not overcome two eases”

If you ask about the saying of Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn Masʿūd, “One hardship will not overcome two eases”—also transmitted as a report traced to the Prophet, who came out one day laughing and saying, “One hardship will not overcome two eases”—I answer: this proceeds on the apparent sense and rests on strong hope, and on the principle that God's promise should be understood only in the fullest and most ample sense that its wording can bear. The explanation is that the second sentence may repeat the first, as “woe that day to those who deny” is repeated to establish its meaning in people's minds and fix it in their hearts, or as an individual word is repeated in “Zayd came to me—Zayd.” Alternatively, the first sentence may promise that hardship will necessarily be followed by ease, and the second may be a fresh promise that hardship will be followed by ease. On the interpretation that it is a new statement, there are two eases. The hardship is one because its definiteness must either refer to something already known—the hardship in which they were living—in which case it is the same hardship, just as Zayd is the same in “With Zayd there is wealth; with Zayd there is wealth”; or it refers to the class familiar to everyone, in which case it is likewise the same. Ease, however, is indefinite and denotes some member of its class. If the second statement is new rather than a repetition, it unquestionably denotes a member other than the first. If you ask what the two eases mean, I answer: they may mean the conquests made easy for them in the days of God's Messenger and those made easy in the days of the caliphs; or ease in this world and ease in the next. Compare: “Say: Do you await for us anything but one of the two good outcomes?” (9:52), namely the good of victory and the good of reward. If you ask what the indefiniteness signifies, I answer: magnification—as though He said, “With hardship comes a mighty ease—and what an ease!” In Ibn Masʿūd's codex it occurs only once. If you ask: if it is not repeated in his reading, why did he say, “By Him in whose hand is my soul, if hardship were in a hole, ease would pursue it until it entered upon it; one hardship will not overcome two eases”? I answer: by the two eases he seems to have intended the magnifying force of the indefinite “ease,” interpreting it as the ease of both abodes. Those are indeed two eases.

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT094.08

The report and the apparent sense (498)

On K094.05

On “it has been transmitted as a report traced to the Prophet”: Mālik transmits in al-Muwaṭṭaʾ from Zayd ibn Aslam: “Abū ʿUbayda wrote to ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, mentioning the assembled Roman forces and his apprehension concerning them. ʿUmar wrote back: To proceed: whatever hardship befalls a believing servant, God brings relief after it. One hardship will not overcome two eases.” On “this proceeds on the apparent sense”: ‘apparent’ (ẓāhir) means wording admitting more than one interpretation, with one of them rendered more probable by a cogent indication. What they said follows the apparent sense: the revelation admits both repetition and a new statement. The indication favoring one possibility—the new statement, because it is fuller and more ample—is that God's promise is to be understood in the fullest of the possible senses. “And on the principle that God's promise…” is an explanatory coordination with “and rests on strong hope,” which is similarly related to “proceeds on the apparent sense.” “The explanation is…” sets out the two possibilities.

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT094.09

How the unrepeated reading can also signify two eases (499)

On K094.05

On this basis, even without repetition—as in Ibn Masʿūd's reading—the intended meaning is conveyed. The indefinite yusran can signify either some instance of ease or magnification. Since the matter rests on strong hope, the latter is preferred. The difference is that the first interpretation signifies the intended meaning by linguistic convention, as will follow, while the second signifies it by implication and indirect expression (kināya). Magnification in yusran requires ease to reach its utmost extent; otherwise it would not mean ease in both abodes. From this follows the multiplicity of ease, and the saying, “One hardship will not overcome two eases.” This is what he indicates by “those are indeed two eases.” If this meaning is intended through repetition, it is more eloquent than treating the sentence as a fresh statement. Without the transmitted saying and prophetic report drawing attention to this subtle point, it would not be understood. One may also say: since the verse concerns the noble Companions and promises them relief after distress, it must be understood as ease in both abodes. In this world that consists of wealth after poverty, strength after weakness, and honor after humiliation. As for the next world, there is no dispute about it.

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT094.10

Definite and indefinite repetition (499–500)

On K094.05

On “the hardship is one”: know that, according to the exact investigators, the definite article lām is assigned to indication and reference to something known. The author of al-Takhmīr says: “The lām is for indication itself. Sometimes the indication points to an individual already familiar to the addressee, and sometimes to a class. Its meaning is one in every case. Understand this: people's errors concerning it are considerable, and it is a point of doctrinal grammatical importance.” I say: it consequently requires something previously available to be indicated. Whenever the discourse contains something that can serve as such a referent in any way, that is selected. Al-Bazdawī says: “The defining lām refers back to what is known: a thing is mentioned and then returned to, the second being the first. An example is our scholars' ruling about someone who acknowledges a thousand with a particular qualification, then acknowledges it again with the same qualification: the second is the first. If each expression is indefinite, there is disagreement over whether unity of the sitting is a condition for the second to be identical with the first. According to Abū Ḥanīfa it is; according to Abū Yūsuf it is not.” The author of al-Muṭliʿ transmits from al-Farrāʾ that when the Arabs mention an indefinite noun and then repeat an indefinite noun like it, they become two: “When you earn a dirham, spend a dirham.” The second differs from the first. If they repeat it in definite form, it is the same. Al-Zajjāj states something similar. Al-Sayyid says in al-Amālī: “Hardship is definite and ease indefinite because, when a noun is repeated indefinitely, the second differs from the first: ‘A man came to me, and I said to a man such-and-such.’ The same applies when the first is definite and the second indefinite: ‘The man arrived, and I said to a man such-and-such.’ If the first is indefinite and the second definite, the second is the first; likewise when a definite noun follows a definite noun: ‘The man arrived, and I honored the man.’ Hence Ibn ʿAbbās said, ‘One hardship will not overcome two eases.’”

Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK094.06

Verses 7–8: continuing in worship

“So when you have finished, exert yourself; and direct your longing toward your Lord.” If you ask how “so when you have finished, exert yourself” connects with what precedes, I answer: after enumerating His earlier blessings and promising those to come, He urged him to gratitude, diligence in worship, and exertion in it: to join one act of worship to another, pursue them successively, and take care that none of his times be empty of them. Whenever he finishes one act of worship, he follows it with another. Ibn ʿAbbās said: when you finish your prayer, exert yourself in supplication. Al-Ḥasan said: when you finish warfare, exert yourself in worship. Mujāhid said: when you finish your worldly business, exert yourself in prayer. Al-Shaʿbī saw a man lifting a stone and said: “This is not what someone with time to spare has been commanded to do.” Sitting idle without occupation, or occupying oneself with what benefits neither one's religion nor one's worldly affairs, is evidence of foolish judgment, feeble understanding, and heedlessness taking control. ʿUmar said: “I dislike seeing any of you idle and aimless, engaged neither in work for this world nor in work for the next.” Abū al-Sammāl read fariġta, with kasra on the rāʾ; this is not an eloquent usage. Among novel interpretations is the report that one of the Rāfiḍa read fa-nṣib, with kasra on the ṣād, meaning: appoint ʿAlī to the imamate. Were this valid for the Rāfiḍī, it would equally be valid for a Nāṣibī to read it in this fashion and make it a command to naṣb, which is hatred and hostility toward ʿAlī. “And direct your longing toward your Lord”: make Him exclusively the object of your longing; ask only for His bounty, relying upon Him. Fa-raġġib is also read: encourage people to seek what is with Him. The Prophet is reported to have said: “Whoever recites ‘Have We not expanded,’ it is as though he came to me while I was grieving and relieved my grief.”

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT094.11

The question about indefiniteness; successive acts of devotion (501–502)

On K094.05, K094.06

On “then what does this indefiniteness mean?”: the fāʾ indicates an objection. If the two eases mean what you have explained in either of the two ways, they ought to be definite; why, then, are they indefinite? On “when you finish your prayer, exert yourself in supplication”: this is coordinated with “when he finishes one act of worship, he follows it with another.” Both “you have finished” and “exert yourself” are unrestricted. They may remain unrestricted: when you finish one act of worship, follow it with another. Or they may be specified as prayer and supplication, because prayer is the best of the acts of worship and supplication is its marrow; or as warfare and worship, as in the saying, “We have returned from the lesser struggle to the greater struggle”; or as worldly business and prayer, because ‘having free time’ is most often used of worldly affairs. An example is the report, “[Take advantage of] your free time before you become occupied.” This transmission from Mujāhid is mentioned in Sharḥ al-Sunna. On “idle and aimless” (fāriġan sabahlalan): al-Nihāya says: “In ʿUmar's report, ‘I dislike seeing any of you aimless, engaged neither in work for this world nor in work for the next,’ the indefiniteness in ‘world’ and ‘next world’ goes back to the noun annexed to them, namely ‘work’: neither in any work belonging to this world's affairs nor in any work belonging to the next world's affairs. One says jāʾa yamshī sabahlalan when someone comes and goes idly, engaged in nothing.”

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT094.12

Exclusive longing and the unusual conjunction (503)

On K094.06

On “make Him exclusively the object of your longing”: particularization is conveyed by placing the prepositional phrase before the verb. Al-Sayyid says in al-Amālī: “The fāʾ occurs together with the wāw, and ilā is governed by what comes after the fāʾ. Similarly, in ‘and your garments—purify them’ (74:4), what precedes the fāʾ is made accusative by what follows it. This is among the remarkable features of their speech: ordinarily fāʾ coordinates, introduces a response, or introduces something resembling a response, such as the predicate of an incomplete noun—that is, a relative pronoun whose relative clause is verbal. Here it falls outside its assigned function.” The sūrah is complete, by God's praise and aid. God suffices us; how excellent a trustee He is.

Textual and review notes

SOURCE AND REVIEW NOTES K094-U1: K-DKA's transcription has ʿumūm al-nubuwwa, “the full scope of prophethood,” where the Kashshāf quoted and discussed by Ṭībī has humūm al-nubuwwa, “the concerns of prophethood.” The latter is used here and the difference is retained for collation. K094-U2: K-DKA's transcription has a damaged or variant form in ʿUmar's saying; Ṭībī preserves and explicitly explains sabahlalan. Likewise, the threefold explanation of lifting the burden is rendered according to the clearer quoted form in Ṭībī. T094-U1: The text of the magnification argument on p. 499 is syntactically damaged around “required it to reach its utmost extent.” Visual inspection confirms the incomplete phrase أن يتناهى في in the printed edition itself. The translation renders the evident argumentative connection and does not claim to establish every missing word. This locus remains for comparison with another textual witness. T094-U2: Technical discussions of readings, grammatical permissibility, legal acknowledgment, and the authors' sectarian polemic are translated as their historical claims. The closing merit report and other transmitted reports have not been independently authenticated.
