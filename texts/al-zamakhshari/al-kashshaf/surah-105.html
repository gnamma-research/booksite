AL-FĪL (105) — THE ELEPHANT Complete first-pass translation of both authorial layers • C05 • 27 October 2026

SOURCE AND STATUS Kashshāf: Dār al-Kitāb al-ʿArabī, 1407 AH, IV:797–800, OpenITI Shamela0023627-ara1. Ṭībī: Futūḥ al-Ghayb, Dubai, 1434/2013, XVI:577–584, OpenITI Sham19Y0020891-ara1. Both layers are translated through the final report and gloss. These historical narratives and reports are the authors’ transmitted material, not independently authenticated conclusions. Modern editorial footnotes are excluded; unresolved readings remain flagged.

Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK105.01

Heading and verses [IV:797]

Meccan; five verses. [The DKA heading additionally places its revelation after al-Kāfirūn.] In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

Have you not seen how your Lord dealt with the People of the Elephant? (1) Did He not bring their scheme to nothing, (2) and send against them birds in flocks, (3) pelting them with stones of sijjīl, (4) and make them like eaten crop-stalks? (5)

Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK105.02

Abraha’s expedition [IV:797]

It is related that Abraha ibn al-Ṣabbāḥ al-Ashram, ruler of Yemen on behalf of Aṣḥama the Negus, built a church in Ṣanʿāʾ, named it al-Qullays, and intended to divert the pilgrims to it. A man of Kināna went and “sat” in it at night, angering him. Another account says an Arab travelling party lit a fire, which the wind carried to the church and burned it. Abraha swore to demolish the Kaʿba and set out with the Abyssinians, bringing his enormous, powerful elephant Maḥmūd and twelve other elephants. Eight are also reported; another account says a thousand, while [another has] the elephant alone. [The transmitted conjunction is awkward; the alternatives are not harmonised.]

When he reached al-Mughammas, ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib went out and offered him a third of Tihāma’s wealth to turn back. He refused, arranged his army, and put the elephant in front. Whenever they directed it toward the sanctuary it knelt and would not move; toward Yemen or any other direction it trotted. God sent black birds—also said to be green, or white—each carrying a stone in its beak and two in its feet, larger than a lentil and smaller than a chickpea. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have seen about a qafīz-measure of them at Umm Hāniʾ’s, striped red like Ẓafār agate.

The stone struck a man’s head and emerged from his rear, and each bore the name of the person it would strike. They fled and perished along every road and watering place. Abraha sickened; his fingertips and limbs fell away, and he did not die until his chest split open over his heart. His minister Abū Yaksūm escaped, with his bird circling above him, until he reached the Negus and told him the story. When he finished, the stone fell on him and he collapsed dead before him.

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT105.01

Names and vocabulary of the expedition [XVI:577–578]

On K105.02

Meccan; five verses. In the name of God, the All-Merciful, the Merciful.

On al-Ashram: sharm means cutting the nose-tip or a she-camel’s vulva. It is said he was called al-Ashram because his father struck him with a spear and split his nose and forehead.

On “he sat in it at night”: an indirect expression meaning he relieved himself. Al-Mughammas is said to be a place between Mecca and Minā. On “he arranged his army,” al-Jawharī says: “ʿAbbaytu l-jaysha taʿbiyatan, taʿbiʾatan and taʿbīʾan means I stationed it in its positions; Abū Zayd gives ʿabaʾtuhu with hamza.”

On “Abraha sickened”: dawā, with short final alif, means illness; dawiya, with kasra, means became ill. It is also explained as “sickened from disease.” On “his limbs”: irb means a limb, as in prostrating on seven ārāb. On “a bird circling”: taḥlīq means rising high in flight.

Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK105.03

ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib and the prayer [IV:797–798]

It is said that Abraha was the grandfather of the Negus who lived in the Messenger’s time, [Abraha’s rule being] forty years [before the prophetic mission]; twenty-three years is also reported. [The bracketed temporal clarification follows Ṭībī’s explanation below.] ʿĀʾisha says: “I saw the elephant’s driver and keeper, blind and crippled, begging for food.”

The narrative also relates that Abraha took two hundred camels belonging to ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib. He went to recover them, and his imposing appearance impressed Abraha, for he was large and handsome. Abraha was told, “This is Quraysh’s chief and the lord of Mecca’s caravan, who feeds people on the plain and wild animals on the mountain-tops.” When he stated his request, Abraha said, “You have fallen in my estimation. I have come to destroy the House that is your religion and your fathers’ religion, your protection and your ancient honour, yet a few confiscated camels distract you from it!” He replied, “I am the lord of the camels; the House has a Lord who will protect it.” He returned to the House’s door, grasped its ring, and said: “O God, a man protects his household: protect Your people! Let not their cross and their power soon overcome Your power. If You leave them with our Kaʿba, then [do] whatever seems good to You. Lord, I hope for none against them but You; Lord, protect Your sanctuary from them.”

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT105.02

Chronology and appearance [XVI:579]

On K105.02, K105.03

“Who lived in the Prophet’s time” distinguishes which Negus is meant. The author of al-Jāmiʿ says: “Negus is the title of Abyssinia’s king. The one who became Muslim and believed in the Prophet was Aṣḥama. He became Muslim before the conquest, died before it, and the Prophet prayed over him.” [His wording is retained.]

“Forty years” means before the prophetic mission. It is a second predicate of the first kāna: he existed and ruled forty years before the mission. This account is closer than twenty-three years, since, by the agreement of transmitters, the Prophet was born in the Elephant’s year and commissioned forty years later. The Negus accepted Islam in the fifth year after the mission. Ibn al-Jawzī reports that the Messenger was born on Monday, ten days into Rabīʿ al-Awwal in the Elephant’s year; Ibn Isḥāq gives twelve days. Ibn Qutayba says: “They agree that God’s Messenger was born in the Elephant’s year.”

“Concerning them” means concerning the camels and recovering them. On “his appearance impressed him,” al-Asās says: “Jahartuhu, ijtahartuhu and istajhartuhu: I saw him as imposing in appearance. Jaharanī fulān: his beauty and bearing struck me with awe.”

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT105.03

The prayer’s wording [XVI:580–581]

On K105.03

Dhawd denotes between three and ten camels. Though the camels were very numerous, Abraha seemingly diminishes their number to belittle the request and deter him from making it in that situation.

On the verses beginning “Lāhumma”: its original form is Allāhumma. Riḥālaka is transmitted, and also ḥilālaka, plural of ḥilla, a place where people settle. Ḥilāl, with kasra, also means people settled together as neighbours: the sanctuary’s inhabitants. Al-Asās gives “I settled among the people” and “I settled in the dwelling”; it is their maḥalla and ḥilla; a tribe described as ḥilla or ḥilāl is settled in one place.

On “their cross”: one says the Byzantines came with crosses. Maḥāla and miḥāl mean resourceful contrivance, as in “Man fails, but resourcefulness does not.” Miḥāl is also said to mean punishment or strength, as in “He is mighty in power” [13:13].

In “fa-amrun mā,” mā is either additional for reinforcement or relative: whatever benefit seems good to You. Al-Nihāya explains ghaduwwan, with dotted ghayn: “Ghaduww is the original form of ghad, the day following today; its final root letter was dropped. The full form occurs only in verse, as in: ‘People are only like dwellings and their inhabitants: inhabited the day they settle, and tomorrow desolate.’ ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib did not mean the particular next day, but a near time.”

Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK105.04

The birds and “Have you not seen?” [IV:798–799]

As he prayed he turned and saw birds approaching from Yemen. “By God,” he said, “these are strange birds, neither seabirds nor birds of Tihāma.” The narrative also says that Mecca’s people took possession of the invaders’ property, and ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib amassed a great fortune from their jewels and gold, which made him wealthy. Asked about the birds, Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī said that Mecca’s pigeons descend from them. It is said that they arrived in the evening and attacked next morning. ʿIkrima says anyone struck developed smallpox, the first smallpox to appear.

Alam tar is also read with a quiescent rāʾ, emphatically displaying the effect of the jussive governor. The meaning is: you have seen the effects of God’s act upon the Abyssinians and heard reports of it through widespread transmission (tawātur), so these take the place of direct witnessing. Kayfa, “how,” is accusative through “your Lord dealt,” not through “have you not seen,” because of its interrogative meaning.

“To nothing” (fī taḍlīl): into waste and nullity. Ḍallala kaydahu means making a scheme stray and fail; compare “The unbelievers’ scheming is only in vain” [40:25]. Imruʾ al-Qays was called “the errant king” because he squandered his father’s kingdom. First they plotted against the House by building al-Qullays, intending to supersede it by directing pilgrims there; He frustrated their scheme by burning it. They then plotted to destroy the House; He frustrated that by sending the birds.

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT105.04

A great fortune [XVI:581]

On K105.04

Al-jawr, with fatḥa on jīm, quiescent wāw and rāʾ, occurs in a copy collated against the author’s handwriting: abundant wealth, so called because its accumulation exceeds the limit. Al-ḥawz, with ḥāʾ and zāy, is also transmitted. Al-Jawharī says: “Ḥawz is gathering; whoever brings something into his possession has ḥāzahu ḥawzan wa-ḥiyāzatan, and iḥtāzahu.” Al-juʾr also occurs. Al-Jawharī says: “Ghayth juʾr is copious, abundant rain; juʾar, like nughar, is also said.” They recite: “Let no heavy, thunder-roaring downpour water it.” ʿAzf here is the rumble of thunder.

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT105.05

Seeing how, and the prelude to prophecy [XVI:582]

On K105.04

On “you have seen the effects of God’s act,” the Qāḍī says: “‘Have you not seen?’ addresses God’s Messenger. Though he did not witness the event, he saw its effects and heard widely transmitted reports, as though he had seen it. ‘How He dealt’ is said rather than ‘what He did’ because the point is to recall its indications of God’s perfect knowledge and power and His Prophet’s dignity and honour: it belongs to the anticipatory signs of prophecy.”

The Imām says: “Things have entities and modes. Their modes are what theologians call ‘the aspect establishing the proof’ (wajh al-dalīl). Desert of praise is discerned by seeing modes, not merely entities. Hence: ‘Have they not looked at the sky above them, how We built it?’ [50:6]. This event undoubtedly laid the foundation for his prophethood and anticipated his mission.” Irhāṣ derives from rahṣ, the bottom course of a wall: something resembling a miracle precedes the prophetic claim, such as clouds shading the Messenger and stones and clods speaking to him.

Ḥazāʾiq means groups. Al-Asās gives ḥizqa, ḥazīqa and ḥazīq for a group, and “they followed one another like bands of locusts.”

Al-Zamakhsharī · al-KashshāfK105.05

Flocks, sijjīl, and eaten stalks [IV:799–800]

Abābīl means bands, singular ibbāla. Their proverb says, “A handful upon a great bundle.” Ibbāla is the great bundle: the birds’ tightly gathered band is likened to it. It is also said that abābīl, like ʿabādīd and shamāṭīṭ, has no singular.

Abū Ḥanīfa read yarmīhim, “he pelts them”: God, or the birds, for ṭayr is a masculine collective noun and is made feminine only according to its meaning.

Sijjīl appears to be a proper name for the register containing the unbelievers’ punishment, just as sijjīn names the register of their deeds: stones forming part of the recorded punishment. It derives from isjāl, sending, because punishment is described as sent: “He sent against them birds”; “We sent against them the flood” [7:133]. Ibn ʿAbbās explains it as clay baked like bricks. It is also said to be Arabicised from Persian sang-gil. Another explanation is “from His severe punishment,” citing Ibn Muqbil’s verse: “A severe striking (sijjīlan), enjoined by champions upon one another.” But the actual word is sijjīnan: the poem is a well-known nūn-rhyming poem in his collection.

They are likened to crop leaves when eaten—that is, infested by worms—or to straw eaten and excreted by animals. The expression observes Qurʾānic propriety, like “They both ate food” [5:75]. Or it means that the grain was eaten, leaving the stalks empty.

From God’s Messenger: “Whoever recites Sūrat al-Fīl, God spares him throughout his life from being swallowed by the earth and from bodily transformation.”

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT105.06

Bundles, scattered bands, and the disputed verse [XVI:583–584]

On K105.05

On “a handful upon a great bundle,” al-Maydānī says: “Ibbāla is a bundle of firewood; ḍighth a handful of mixed fresh and dry grass. Ībāla is also transmitted, and some lighten it to ibāla. Its meaning is one affliction upon another.”

On ʿabādīd and shamāṭīṭ, al-Jawharī says: “ʿAbādīd are groups of people going in every direction. Shamāṭīṭ are scattered pieces; horses arriving shamāṭīṭ arrive dispersed in separate groups.”

On isjāl, sending, al-Asās says: “This is musjal: released, unrestricted; one may take it or leave it. Asjaltu l-bahīmata maʿa ummihā means I let the young animal go with its mother.”

On “His severe punishment,” al-Zajjāj says: “When Arabs describe something disagreeable as sijjīl they mean severity; the term is not applied otherwise. Ibn Muqbil says: ‘And foot-soldiers striking the bright blades openly, a severe striking, enjoined by champions upon one another.’”

In his book’s marginal note: “Abū ʿUbayda cited it thus in his Majāz; Ibn Muqbil’s poetry has sijjīnan, and that is correct. Rijla is the collective of rājil, foot-soldier. A thing’s ḍāḥiya is its exposed side. Sijjīnan qualifies ḍarban.” Another transmission instead has “the bright blades from various sides” (al-bīḍ ʿan ʿurḍ). Al-bīḍ means swords. ʿUrḍ, with ḍamma, means the middle or side of something. Thus: many foot-soldiers strike with swords in battle from different sides, striking fiercely as champions enjoin upon one another.

Al-Ṭībī · Futūḥ al-GhaybT105.07

Qurʾānic propriety and the empty stalks [XVI:584]

On K105.05

On “like ‘they both ate food’” [5:75]: in the two verses animal dung and human waste are expressed indirectly, observing propriety. Their dismemberment is likened to the dispersal of particles of dung, at once preserving propriety and displaying the ugliness of their state and outcome.

On “its grain was eaten, leaving it empty”: devoid of good. The meaning is stalks whose grain has been eaten, like saying “So-and-so is beautiful” meaning beautiful of face: what is understood is omitted. This is al-Ḥasan’s interpretation.

End of the sūrah.

Textual and review notes

TEXTUAL AND REVIEW NOTES K105-U1: The conflicting elephant counts and the clause “and it was alone” occur in the printed base at XVI:578 as well as the transcription. The English marks the alternative rather than inventing a single consistent count. T105-U1: The electronic wording says “with dotted ghayn” when explaining عرض, which begins with ʿayn. The intelligible word is rendered ʿurḍ; the letter-name needs scan/manuscript collation before finalisation. K105-U2: The DKA poem reads عدوا where Ṭībī’s gloss explicitly discusses غدوا, “soon/tomorrow”; the latter is followed and the difference recorded.
