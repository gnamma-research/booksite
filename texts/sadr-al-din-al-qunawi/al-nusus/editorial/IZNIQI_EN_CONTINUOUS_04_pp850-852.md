# Al-Iznīqī — Continuous English translation, segment 4: pp. 850–852

**Source:** M. S. Balaban and A. Çalışkan (eds.), *İhya* 9/2 (2023), authorial text pp. 831–852, here the concluding three printed pages. This English is a fresh reading from rendered printed-page images, with present-day bibliographical footnotes omitted. **Draft:** close rendering of the argument, not yet manuscript-collated or a word-for-word critical edition of all embedded citations.

### [850] Final defence of agreed first principles; Conclusion, part one

Every party must ultimately return for doctrinal judgement to the established, unequivocal texts, not to a private understanding that overturns those texts. People of the established schools—the Jabriyya, Qadariyya, Mujassima, Muʿtazila, Khawārij, Rāfiḍa and others—may dispute the meanings of particular scriptural texts while still making the Qurʾān and the Sunnah the reference for their arguments. When opposing interpretations collide, the clear and decisive passages must govern disputed or ambiguous ones, not the reverse. The author invokes the distinction between firm (*muḥkam*) and ambiguous (*mutashābih*) discourse and warnings against seeking the ambiguous merely to sow dissension. He also invokes reported Prophetic statements against sectarian conflict and misappropriation of religion.

Many apparently incompatible theological descriptions of God arise because a debater takes a statement made under one qualified meaning and applies it under a different one. A statement of divine nearness is not necessarily a declaration of incarnation, and a statement of divine transcendence need not imply the absence of divine knowledge or providence. The criterion is the intended sense within the established foundations of revelation. The author's attachment to the people of Sunnah is expressed strongly, but the same discussion repeatedly warns that interpretation must precede condemnation.

The author concludes the main defence by invoking the Qurʾānic announcement that God sent His Messenger with guidance and the religion of truth, and by praising those who sustain the community's revealed acts and institutions. Such an expression of communal allegiance is not an argument that one ought to anathematise every individual who errs in disputed terminology. Praise belongs to God who guided us to this, and without His guidance we could never have been guided.

**Conclusion.** It contains two chapters.

**First chapter: Explaining what is meant by the mistaken interpretation and clarifying the right meaning.**

Know that, on the principle discussed earlier, faith does not increase or diminish in its very essence. Such is the statement associated with the Lamp of the Community and the greatest of the imams, Abū Ḥanīfa—may God be pleased with him. Faith in itself is the affirmation or assent (*taṣdīq*); the core act of assent does not admit of more and less in the same way as things with measurable magnitude, although associated aspects can differ. This is because the content to which faith assents is the divine truth, not an amount of substance that grows and shrinks with each later event. Where increase is spoken of, the proper meaning must be sought among what belongs to faith through its strength, its consequences, its evidence, and its conduct, rather than assuming a quantitative division of its essence.

Al-Iznīqī begins to distinguish the claim that knowledge of God is *certain and adequate for the duty of faith* from a claim to have embraced the whole inner reality of the Real. Both may be called knowledge; they answer different questions. The first is the recognition required of a believer under the authoritative teaching of revelation. The second is the ultimate knowledge of the very Essence and all its inexhaustible relations. To take the first as proof of the second, or the denial of the second as a denial of the first, is the logical error at the centre of the original dispute.

### [851] Assent, greater certainty, and the schools' disagreement about increase in faith

The author develops the distinction further. The core of faith as assent is not multiplied merely because the acts and qualities accompanying it become more numerous. Works of righteousness may increase the light and stability of faith in the heart, whereas sins and disregard may weaken the dispositions that support it. The reports about increasing faith, and the Qurʾānic statement in which Abraham asks that his heart be reassured [Q 2:260], show that the believer's preparedness, composure, evidence and firmness can improve. They do not require that a proposition which was previously assented to was only partially true, or that the object of faith had become a different object.

The argument therefore distinguishes the essence of *taṣdīq* from its strength and weakness, and the general assent to necessary religious truths from a detailed knowledge of all their implications. Increased proofs may lead to greater tranquillity and resistance to doubts. Yet the author notes that scholars who define faith differently draw different conclusions from the same scriptural evidence. This is one reason a dispute about increase and decrease must not be treated as though the opponents were always denying the same proposition.

He adduces earlier theologians, including legal authorities from the Ḥanafī and Shāfiʿī traditions and noted representatives of kalām, to show the variety of technical formulations. Some define faith by internal assent; others add verbal declaration as a requirement for worldly legal treatment. A third usage treats the outward works of religion as belonging to the complete reality of faith. Where these definitions differ, disagreement about increase can be partly verbal; where they disagree over obligations, its doctrinal implications must be considered separately.

The text insists that we must not lightly dismiss the belief of the common people. Ordinary believers may possess sincere certainty without being able to demonstrate it in the sophisticated vocabulary of the theologians. A scholar's ability to refute doubts, and the exceptionally unveiled person's witnessed certainty, are different capacities from the minimum truth of faith. If the person possesses what is required, the lack of speculative proof or of mystical attainment does not remove him from Islam. Error and confusion may indeed occur in certain arguments, but the duty of interpreters is to distinguish the deficiency of a proof from the rejection of a religious truth.

The position the author endorses is that the essential assent necessary for faith is not increased or diminished by measurable increments, while its light, fullness of assurance, and accompanying qualities can be described as stronger or weaker. This is the relevant interpretation of the statement ascribed to the great Imam. Once one has determined the meaning of *faith* being used, the apparent contradiction with Qurʾānic reports of increase need not be real. This supports the larger point of the treatise: rigorous clarification of terms is preferable to the hasty accusation of other Muslims.

### [852] Conclusion, part two: restraining the tongue concerning the people of the qibla

**Second chapter: Refraining from accusations against the people of the qibla.**

Know that holding back the tongue from the people of the qibla is obligatory concerning anyone who faces it and remains within the basic profession of Islam, except where a decisive legal cause for exclusion has been established. The Messenger of God is reported to have said that three things belong to the fundamental practice of faith, among them refraining from accusations against someone who declares “There is no god but God” and not casting him outside Islam merely because of a sin. The report is cited for its intended legal and moral rule, not to deny that the religious law has specific provisions concerning apostasy.

Understand that declaring a person an unbeliever is a legal judgement, not merely an exercise in intellectual preference. The point is not that error is impossible, or that one may believe whatever one likes while retaining the same doctrinal standing. Rather, where a person professes the faith, departure from it must be established by a decisive expression or act and under the conditions and exceptions recognised by jurists. The religious law does not make a person an unbeliever merely because one of his expressions is liable to a hostile interpretation.

Some texts or actions are indeed regarded as demonstrably contrary to Islam: a direct and unqualified denial of a necessary truth, an explicit rejection of the authority of revelation, or deliberately treating forbidden things as permitted when their prohibition is decisive. But doubt about a disputed proposition, a doubtful implication drawn by an opponent, or a technical expression used in a different discipline is not automatically equivalent to such explicit repudiation. Where an interpretation is possible, its grounds must be assessed before the charge is pronounced.

**Question:** What if the outward wording or an act appears to entail unbelief under one interpretation? **Answer:** If there is a single sound and legally permissible construction which does not entail unbelief, the jurist should lean toward the construction that preserves the person's standing as a believer. The author cites the Prophetic warning not to suspect evil of a word spoken by a brother while one can find a good construction for it. The requirement here is not to conceal proven wrongdoing but to reject reckless certainty in doubtful cases. 

The treatise closes, in the preserved text, with this protection of communal faith and this warning against applying technical judgements before reconstructing what a speaker intended.
