### [831] Invocation, reason for writing, beginning of the Introduction

In the name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful, whose help we seek.

Praise belongs to God who immersed in the seas of His knowledge the intellects of those of understanding, and who bewildered in the wilderness of His Majesty the spiritual aspirations of the hearts of the God-fearing. He caused the spirits of the afflicted to perish through the disclosure of the love of His divinity, and He rendered the hands of the intelligent incapable of grasping the majesty of the perfection of His attributes and His most beautiful Names. No flight of thought can sever the veil of His Majesty, and no reasoning of the contemplative mind penetrates the unseen of His beauty or the most secret chambers of His dominion. He veiled Himself from the inmost sights of beholders by the lights of His splendour, and He absented His majesty from the passing thoughts of those who gaze. He transcends designation by symbols and allusions and any enclosure through suppositions, understandings, or fancies. And blessings be upon His beloved, the Chosen One and the noble victor, the lamp which shines upon both humankind and jinn, and upon his family, the lamps of darkness, his companions, the stars of guidance, and all who follow them until the final return.

Now the cause of dictating this treatise and setting it down, and the reason for writing and editing these pages, is that the following saying occurs among the litanies of trustworthy eminent masters and the people of spiritual acceptance and regard: **“Glory be to You; we have not known You as You deserve to be known.”** Some people accused the one who uttered it of error and folly, others of unbelief and deviation—God protect us from such an ugly utterance and reprehensible contention; may God pardon them their lapses and the injury they have inflicted upon the people of this clear religious community. I, the weak and desirous servant Muḥammad b. Quṭb al-Dīn al-Iznīqī—may God fill his breast with the true light—have undertaken to dispel the error of those people, the possessors of insight, and to reveal what may help them. I ask the brothers who bring good news: do not deprive me of your favour concerning this difficult problem; open to me, through Your kindness, the doors of Your proof, and bring me to the point of sound argument through Your care, amen. I have organised the treatise as an introduction, chapters, and conclusion.

**Introduction.** It contains two inquiries.

**First inquiry: the distinction, in Sufi terminology and in reality, between the meanings of recognition and knowledge.** Kamāl al-Dīn al-Qāshānī says that the *ʿārif* is one whom God makes witness to His own Essence, attributes, Names, and acts. Recognition (*maʿrifa*) is a spiritual state in which witnessing of the Real through the Real predominates, while the traces of separate bodily individuality and the ray of individuating unicity fade.

### [832] How Sufi authors understand maʿrifa; a terminological caution

Kamāl al-Dīn also says that in this usage *maʿrifa* is witnessing, indeed certainty: an encompassing vision of the Reality as it is. A true recognition of God is possible through His making Himself witnessed by the one who knows. Abū Yazīd al-Bisṭāmī, as reported by al-Rāzī, says that the knower knows God without a stable claim to a self or an independent spiritual state: the divine traces burn away his distinct self. He is also reported to have said that when kings enter a village they despoil it [Q 27:34]: when recognition descends upon the heart, human dwelling-places are ruined. Al-Shiblī says: those who truly recognise God are merciful to people even when no one is gracious to them. Al-Ḥusayn b. Manṣūr al-Ḥallāj speaks of bringing the servant to the station of knowledge through unveiling and depriving him of all independent capacities before the Real. Another saying warns that if Solomon were granted every conceivable gift, this would not by itself amount to knowledge of the Real. How then could the people of this world claim its full possession?

Al-Rāzī says that recognition is more exalted than poverty, love, and unification, because it annihilates the person's self-regard in God, effaces the rest of the universe from that consciousness, and is marked by the disappearance of self-sensation. In contrast, the one who is merely poor still perceives his poverty, the lover his love, and the person who proclaims unity may still perceive his own proclaiming of unity.

**Supplement on the names of the cognitive acts.** In ordinary language *maʿrifa* and *ʿilm* can mean the same thing. Thus the opening of the discussion in *al-Najāt*—every instance of knowing is either conception or assent—treats them as corresponding usages. Sometimes *ʿilm* denotes assent (*taṣdīq*), and *maʿrifa* conception (*taṣawwur*). This matches one grammatical difference: the verb *ʿalima* can take two objects, while *ʿarafa* takes one. Sometimes *ʿilm* concerns complex things, whether as concepts or judgements, while *maʿrifa* concerns simple things, even when conceived or affirmed. Hence one may say “I recognised God” rather than simply “I knew Him” with the other verbal construction. In another usage *ʿilm* concerns universal concepts and rules, while *maʿrifa* concerns particulars. Yet again, *maʿrifa* denotes an apprehension following ignorance, or the second apprehension of something after an intervening lack of apprehension; neither qualification is required for *ʿilm*. For this reason God is called *ʿālim*, knowing, but not *ʿārif*, someone who comes to know.

### [833] Interpreting specialist language; the possibility of knowing the divine reality

I say: just as every group of learned people has conventional terms whose meanings must be sought from those who use them, the Sufis too have specialist terms, and we must refer back to them for their meanings: abiding and annihilation, station and passing state, gathering and separation, poverty and sufficiency, affirmation and effacement, knowledge and recognition. To pass judgement upon them—accusing them of heresy or atheism—before investigating their intention is to cast an accusation in blindness.

**Second inquiry: whether knowledge of the very Reality of God can actually occur, and whether it is possible.** Some Sufis, some followers of al-Shāfiʿī, such as al-Ghazālī and the Imam of the Two Sanctuaries, the majority of critically discerning investigators, and the philosophers hold that God's essential reality is not known to human beings. Many of the theologians disagree. The arguments of the two parties are set out at length in works of theology, and we shall pass them by to avoid weariness.

As to the *possibility* of knowing that reality, the philosophers deny it. They argue that an intelligible object must be apprehended either through something self-evident or through deliberation. This Reality is not self-evident; and consideration either gives a description, which does not yield its essence, or a definition, which cannot be constructed in this case because the divine Reality is not composite. In response we deny that essential comprehension can be confined to self-evidence and definition. God may create knowledge of what is not naturally self-evident in a person without a preceding process of thought: through inspiration, revelation, the purification of the inward being, or the transformation of inferential cognition into immediate knowledge. Moreover, even if a description does not necessarily disclose the essence, it does not follow that it *cannot* do so. Among the investigators who withhold a decisive judgement are Qāḍī Abū Bakr and Ḍirār b. ʿAmr. It has been related of Ḍirār that he believed God has a reality unknown to us at present, but that God has the power to create a sixth sense through which its particular nature would be apprehended.

**Chapter One: transmitted evidence from God, His Messenger, and trustworthy masters that human intellect is inadequate to the full knowledge of God.** God says, “They do not encompass Him in knowledge” [Q 20:110]. The Prophet is reported to have said in prayer: “Were You to punish the knowing one who has not ignorance with him, none of those who know You would know You,” and to have been asked “Have You not worshipped Him?” and to have acknowledged his own limitation. The wording of these reports is transmitted with variants. Dāwūd, peace be upon him, is reported to have said that God made the servant's awareness of the impossibility of fully praising Him a way of giving thanks, and awareness of inability to know Him fully itself a kind of recognition. Sahl b. ʿAbd Allāh al-Tustarī is reported to have said that the divine Essence is described through knowledge but cannot be enclosed by it: the Divine has veiled creation from the final reality of His Essence while directing them to His signs. Other masters have called the endpoint of such cognition perplexity and bewilderment. The one who seeks unification by abandoning every distinction nevertheless has not grasped what is beyond every distinction.

### [834] The inherited sayings about recognition and its limits

A master is reported to have said, “My Lord, increase me in bewilderment concerning You.” Yūsuf b. al-Ḥusayn speaks of the ocean of divine unity as one in which love and recognition continually intensify; the journey does not culminate in comprehending its source. Al-Ḥusayn b. Manṣūr al-Ḥallāj reports that the Real cannot be contained in an utterance or adequately grasped by a mind. Al-Shiblī, asked for a phrase concerning divine unity, replied that the speaker is heedless, the silent one ignorant, the one who points towards Him a polytheist, the one who claims nearness distant, the one who feigns comprehension unaware, and the one who witnesses through his own imagined representation has taken that representation for the Real. All that the intellects, fancies, or understandings enumerate of Him is a created and delimited object.

Al-Shiblī also says, “No one who has merely smelled the fragrance of divine unification can suppose that he has attained it.” Ibn ʿAṭāʾ says that reason is an instrument for service, not for arrogating complete knowledge of the divine Lordship. Al-Rāzī compares intellect to a capacity traversing the world of origination: if it were to reach the Reality that originates the world, its finite pretensions would dissolve. He says that those who have attained the deepest insight say, “We have not known Him except through confession that we cannot encompass Him.” Others say, “None truly knows God but God.”

A saying attributed to Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq praises God who has not made a route for created beings to knowing Him through an encompassing grasp, but rather through the recognition of their incapacity to know Him completely. Al-Wāsiṭī warns that recognition does not disclose a way for the servant to claim ownership of God: it is marked by the servant's need and surrender, while the true knower is effaced. ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Jīlānī says that the gates of understanding are screened from the ultimate majesty of the Real. Intellect cannot measure Him, imagination cannot represent Him, and finite instruments cannot reach the source of divine beauty. The highest accomplishment of these faculties is to discern the limits of their own judgments.

ʿAlī, may God be pleased with him, says that recognition of the Real means knowing that everything a person represents in his mind as God falls short of Him. Such speech must be understood according to the registers and qualifications that the speakers themselves intend. The discussion continues in the following chapter by appealing also to rational and persuasive evidence.
