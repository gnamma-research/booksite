# Al-Iznīqī — Continuous English translation, segment 3: pp. 844–849

**Source:** Balaban–Çalışkan, *İhya* 9/2 (2023), medieval Arabic text printed pp. 844–849. Independently written English prose rendered from the edition's page images; medieval verses and quotations have their own attribution cautions. **Status: scholarly draft, not exhaustive or manuscript collated.** Modern footnotes excluded.

### [844] True unity is not an achievement of the one who claims to unify

One of the Sufi masters asks whether the person who proclaims unity by his own act has really established the One. The one who makes an act of unification and thereby draws a distinction between himself and the Real has indeed established the distinction he wished to escape. A unification that contains the mark of an independent claimant is still clothed in otherness. The true unification under discussion is that by which every separate form and effect is effaced, leaving no independent thing which can be presented as the object of a second creator.

The language of the unity of the Real must therefore be distinguished according to the rank of the discourse. In an inferior rank there is still an affirmation of the One over against another, and a speaker who performs that affirmation. In the essential rank, the limitation of all these terms is exposed. The subject of such discourse is not a second, invisible body that ought to replace created bodies, but the Real, of whose unity even the highest finite formulation falls short.

The text cites one poet: “I witnessed my own self through the attributes of the Reality, and what I had attributed to myself veiled me from my Beloved.” Here the spiritual observer does not mean that the finite creature has become a self-existing Lord. Rather, the observer's successive degrees of self-regard must be passed through until the distinction no longer functions as an object independently owned. Among the sayings of Ibn ʿArabī, Ibn al-Fāriḍ, and later poets are verses that make the same point with increasingly paradoxical expressions: “I witnessed”, “I was witnessed”, “the observer”, and “the observed” are not stable descriptions of a fixed and unchanging degree of realization. Their meanings depend on the station from which the speaker uses them.

The passage also quotes the saying “No one declares the One to be one except the One,” and interprets the person who performs an independent act of unification as *jāḥid* (one who negates or denies), since his independent act itself establishes what he claimed to remove. If the servant passes away in the act of unification, a different sense of the expression becomes intelligible. The long poetic quotation which follows attempts to convey this distinction through the first-person rhetoric of spiritual vision.

### [845] Mystical poetry: one reality, many names and the passing away of self-description

Here the treatise gives an extended Arabic poem attributed to Ibn al-Fāriḍ. Its poet addresses an inward witness and a beloved whose reality is concealed by all forms of ordinary naming. The poem speaks of a gathering in which the separations between the poet's knowledge, the reality witnessed, and the recipient's self-conscious representations cease to dominate his awareness. In the language of those states, the poet sees the Beloved wherever he turns; what had first appeared an object outside him is now the principle by which every perception receives its meaning.

The poetic voice says in effect: “I witnessed through myself what I had been seeking; what had stood between us fell away. Then I perceived the poverty of every name, and the claims I formerly made about union themselves became a veil.” Other lines describe return, bewilderment, the passing away of self-regard and its restoration, and how the terms “absence” and “presence” change at successive degrees.

Al-Iznīqī follows with Persian verses attributed to Khwāja ʿAṭṭār: whether the knower is considered under the rank of unity or the appearance of multiplicity, the many names and forms of the things we see do not establish many independent absolutes. The same point is illustrated through the words of Rūmī and another Turkish-Persian mystical poet, who compare exterior difference with interior belonging to a single source. **These poems remain among the lines that need a fresh bilingual transcription and verse-by-verse metrical translation.** They are described here in relation to the prose argument rather than presented as a literal poem translation.

### [846] Why a description of essential unity must not be read as incarnation

Following the poems, the author returns to prose. You should know, he says, that the affirmation of the true Necessary Reality and the acceptance of the One as the one absolute Reality are not innovations against the law of Islam. The words of the spiritual authorities, including the accomplished masters whose sayings have just been cited, do not amount to making God a thing that indwells in bodies, or to granting created souls independent divinity. To say that the Real manifests without being contained by the manifold loci of manifestation is not the same as saying that a created form is itself divine under every respect.

The text here invokes Zayn al-Dīn al-Khāfī, whose formulation excludes *ḥulūl* (incarnation or indwelling) within spirits and bodies. Under the viewpoint being defended, the multiple manifestations do not divide the self-existing reality of God, nor does the procession of ranks entail that divine life was previously deprived of some perfection. The spiritual claim is one of priority and dependence: the created forms are what they are through God, and the Real is not sustained by the forms.

Al-Iznīqī adduces the Qurʾān, inherited reports, and the language of the established scholars of Sunnī jurisprudence to defend religious continuity. He insists that the Quranic and Prophetic sources are to be taken seriously, and that expressions whose surface appearance might violate the necessary religious distinction between Creator and creature cannot be treated as unqualified assertions of that violation. If an expression has an admissible sense consonant with revelation and the speaker's established principles, the interpreter must not automatically insist on the unacceptable meaning.

Neither should the existence of spiritually exalted people make the general disciplines of law and theological truth irrelevant. Al-Iznīqī opposes those who treat the claims of vision as a license to dispense with basic doctrines or commands. Several of the quotations gathered here are from other authorities; the author deploys them as evidence for the interpretive position he is constructing.

### [847] Saints and jurists are not entitled to set the revealed law aside

The discussion continues by appeal to a common principle recognised among legal scholars: no saintly disclosure or visionary interpretation releases a person from the basic law of the Prophet. A saying attributed to the Prophet states that whoever introduces into religious practice what has no place there is rejected. Another inherited maxim describes every innovation in religion as an error inasmuch as it sets aside a necessary source of the law. If the understanding of a person, however exalted, conflicts with an established and decisive obligation, the new understanding must be measured against that obligation rather than making the law subordinate to private experience.

This is the point at which the author cites earlier Sufi masters who were careful to affirm the authority of Qurʾān and Sunnah. Al-Junayd says that a wayfarer who has not memorised the Qurʾān and studied the sayings of the Messenger should not be followed as a spiritual guide. Abū al-Qāsim al-Qushayrī is cited for the principle that true spiritual mastery must be accompanied by knowledge of religious law, and not merely by spiritual exercises.

The author's target is not the disciplined Sufi who explains a difficult phrase through the intended technical meaning. He attacks the self-declared possessor of secret knowledge who abandons prayer and worship, treats ordinary prohibitions as irrelevant, or claims an independent authority that dispenses with the distinction between God and His servant. The latter person cannot appeal to spiritual realization as a defence of blatant transgression.

A saying transmitted from the earlier masters states that one who has not learned the outward rules of law and the inward demands of the path has failed to realise the station he claims. Several witnesses to this tradition insist that the further a wayfarer progresses in inner refinement, the stronger his commitment to the revealed commands must become. An outward life of obedience and an inner life of devotion are complementary disciplines.

### [848] Fanāʾ and baqāʾ do not annul servanthood

The author quotes al-Jīlānī again: some imagine that after annihilation one no longer requires the religious law, or that the arrival at a spiritual goal releases a person from servanthood. This is false. The highest form of annihilation does not permit the worshipper to act without accountability. The most complete *fanāʾ* is the effacement of the self's independent claims and competing objects; it is not the abolition of prescribed worship or the opportunity to violate divine commands.

The true perfected person does not regard the self as a separate necessary existent. Yet this contemplative awareness of the divine source leaves the ordinary obligations of the creature intact. Annihilation and subsistence may be described as the removal of the veils that obscure dependence; in relation to the acts of a servant, they cannot logically cancel the servant's created condition. If there are expressions such as “I am the Real” in mystical discourse, they require their own interpretive context, and cannot serve as an unqualified scriptural rule for the community.

The author distinguishes inner reports of presence from outward jurisdiction. Religious obligation attaches to the outwardly responsible individual. The claim of exceptional spiritual awareness does not change the established identity of the worshipper as one addressed by divine revelation. Al-Junayd and other masters therefore regard the actions of those who knowingly and deliberately depart from obligatory worship as a departure from the authentic path, however frequently they speak of illumination.

The spiritually perfected may indeed utter startling expressions while overwhelmed by the force of a momentary state. Such expressions should be understood as reports of their state, and should not be used to fashion a universal creed denying creaturely dependence. A distinction is required between an actual spiritual event, a distorted intellectual inference from it, and a public claim that legal responsibilities no longer bind the claimant.

### [849] Figurative expressions, exceptional states and the limits of mutual accusation

The author further cautions that those who experience a powerful disclosure may speak in an abbreviated or figurative idiom. The right response is not to dismiss their entire discipline, nor to treat every paradox as a literal truth to be affirmed without conditions. A metaphor may indicate a relation of dependence, the disappearance of independent agency, or the loss of self-attention, without signifying that the creature has absorbed the Essence into a finite form.

He illustrates this with the terms attributed to the early caliphs and the Sufi elders, including “I saw nothing without seeing God before it,” “I saw nothing except that I saw God with it,” and “I saw nothing except that I saw God after it.” The differences in these formulations belong to different perspectives and modes of witnessing. The expressions are not necessarily rival accounts of the existence of multiple gods. Their authority and the precise wording of their attributions need further separate checks.

Persian and Arabic verses on the experience of intoxication and soberness reinforce the point. The image of wine in a cup may be used to indicate a quality or appearance; it does not require a person to infer that the physical cup and its contents are numerically one object. A person who utters an exceptional expression under an overwhelming state cannot automatically be assimilated to a philosopher asserting a permanent and general formula, or to a lawbreaker rejecting a necessary article of faith.

The conclusion of this discussion is juridical and spiritual at once. Human beings differ in their cognitive capacities, in the conditions under which their judgements are made, and in the clarity of the evidence made available to them. A person ought to seek the best interpretation that preserves what is firmly established in the law. One may reject what is demonstrably contrary to revelation without giving a licence for hasty accusations against all those who employ unfamiliar language. Al-Iznīqī returns at the end of the treatise to the issue of the limits of doctrinal assent and the obligation of restraint toward the people of the qibla.
