### [831] Invocation, reason for writing, beginning of the Introduction

In the name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful, whose help we seek.

Praise belongs to God who immersed in the seas of His knowledge the intellects of those of understanding, and who bewildered in the wilderness of His Majesty the spiritual aspirations of the hearts of the God-fearing. He caused the spirits of the afflicted to perish through the disclosure of the love of His divinity, and He rendered the hands of the intelligent incapable of grasping the majesty of the perfection of His attributes and His most beautiful Names. No flight of thought can sever the veil of His Majesty, and no reasoning of the contemplative mind penetrates the unseen of His beauty or the most secret chambers of His dominion. He veiled Himself from the inmost sights of beholders by the lights of His splendour, and He absented His majesty from the passing thoughts of those who gaze. He transcends designation by symbols and allusions and any enclosure through suppositions, understandings, or fancies. And blessings be upon His beloved, the Chosen One and the noble victor, the lamp which shines upon both humankind and jinn, and upon his family, the lamps of darkness, his companions, the stars of guidance, and all who follow them until the final return.

Now the cause of dictating this treatise and setting it down, and the reason for writing and editing these pages, is that the following saying occurs among the litanies of trustworthy eminent masters and the people of spiritual acceptance and regard: **“Glory be to You; we have not known You as You deserve to be known.”** Some people accused the one who uttered it of error and folly, others of unbelief and deviation—God protect us from such an ugly utterance and reprehensible contention; may God pardon them their lapses and the injury they have inflicted upon the people of this clear religious community. I, the weak and desirous servant Muḥammad b. Quṭb al-Dīn al-Iznīqī—may God fill his breast with the true light—have undertaken to dispel the error of those people, the possessors of insight, and to reveal what may help them. I ask the brothers who bring good news: do not deprive me of your favour concerning this difficult problem; open to me, through Your kindness, the doors of Your proof, and bring me to the point of sound argument through Your care, amen. I have organised the treatise as an introduction, chapters, and conclusion.

**Introduction.** It contains two inquiries.

**First inquiry: the distinction, in Sufi terminology and in reality, between the meanings of recognition and knowledge.** Kamāl al-Dīn al-Qāshānī says that the *ʿārif* is one whom God makes witness to His own Essence, attributes, Names, and acts. Recognition (*maʿrifa*) is a spiritual state in which witnessing of the Real through the Real predominates, while the traces of separate bodily individuality and the ray of individuating unicity fade.

### [832] How Sufi authors understand maʿrifa; a terminological caution

Kamāl al-Dīn also says that in this usage *maʿrifa* is witnessing, indeed certainty: an encompassing vision of the Reality as it is. A true recognition of God is possible through His making Himself witnessed by the one who knows. Abū Yazīd al-Bisṭāmī, as reported by al-Rāzī, says that the knower knows God without a stable claim to a self or an independent spiritual state: the divine traces burn away his distinct self. He is also reported to have said that when kings enter a village they despoil it [Q 27:34]: when recognition descends upon the heart, human dwelling-places are ruined. Al-Shiblī says: those who truly recognise God are merciful to people even when no one is gracious to them. Al-Ḥusayn b. Manṣūr al-Ḥallāj speaks of bringing the servant to the station of knowledge through unveiling and depriving him of all independent capacities before the Real. Another saying warns that if Solomon were granted every conceivable gift, this would not by itself amount to knowledge of the Real. How then could the people of this world claim its full possession?

Al-Rāzī says that recognition is more exalted than poverty, love, and unification, because it annihilates the person's self-regard in God, effaces the rest of the universe from that consciousness, and is marked by the disappearance of self-sensation. In contrast, the one who is merely poor still perceives his poverty, the lover his love, and the person who proclaims unity may still perceive his own proclaiming of unity.

**Supplement on the names of the cognitive acts.** In ordinary language *maʿrifa* and *ʿilm* can mean the same thing. Thus the opening of the discussion in *al-Najāt*—every instance of knowing is either conception or assent—treats them as corresponding usages. Sometimes *ʿilm* denotes assent (*taṣdīq*), and *maʿrifa* conception (*taṣawwur*). This matches one grammatical difference: the verb *ʿalima* can take two objects, while *ʿarafa* takes one. Sometimes *ʿilm* concerns complex things, whether as concepts or judgements, while *maʿrifa* concerns simple things, even when conceived or affirmed. Hence one may say “I recognised God” rather than simply “I knew Him” with the other verbal construction. In another usage *ʿilm* concerns universal concepts and rules, while *maʿrifa* concerns particulars. Yet again, *maʿrifa* denotes an apprehension following ignorance, or the second apprehension of something after an intervening lack of apprehension; neither qualification is required for *ʿilm*. For this reason God is called *ʿālim*, knowing, but not *ʿārif*, someone who comes to know.

### [833] Interpreting specialist language; the possibility of knowing the divine reality

I say: just as every group of learned people has conventional terms whose meanings must be sought from those who use them, the Sufis too have specialist terms, and we must refer back to them for their meanings: abiding and annihilation, station and passing state, gathering and separation, poverty and sufficiency, affirmation and effacement, knowledge and recognition. To pass judgement upon them—accusing them of heresy or atheism—before investigating their intention is to cast an accusation in blindness.

**Second inquiry: whether knowledge of the very Reality of God can actually occur, and whether it is possible.** Some Sufis, some followers of al-Shāfiʿī, such as al-Ghazālī and the Imam of the Two Sanctuaries, the majority of critically discerning investigators, and the philosophers hold that God's essential reality is not known to human beings. Many of the theologians disagree. The arguments of the two parties are set out at length in works of theology, and we shall pass them by to avoid weariness.

As to the *possibility* of knowing that reality, the philosophers deny it. They argue that an intelligible object must be apprehended either through something self-evident or through deliberation. This Reality is not self-evident; and consideration either gives a description, which does not yield its essence, or a definition, which cannot be constructed in this case because the divine Reality is not composite. In response we deny that essential comprehension can be confined to self-evidence and definition. God may create knowledge of what is not naturally self-evident in a person without a preceding process of thought: through inspiration, revelation, the purification of the inward being, or the transformation of inferential cognition into immediate knowledge. Moreover, even if a description does not necessarily disclose the essence, it does not follow that it *cannot* do so. Among the investigators who withhold a decisive judgement are Qāḍī Abū Bakr and Ḍirār b. ʿAmr. It has been related of Ḍirār that he believed God has a reality unknown to us at present, but that God has the power to create a sixth sense through which its particular nature would be apprehended.

**Chapter One: transmitted evidence from God, His Messenger, and trustworthy masters that human intellect is inadequate to the full knowledge of God.** God says, “They do not encompass Him in knowledge” [Q 20:110]. The Prophet is reported to have said in prayer: “Were You to punish the knowing one who has not ignorance with him, none of those who know You would know You,” and to have been asked “Have You not worshipped Him?” and to have acknowledged his own limitation. The wording of these reports is transmitted with variants. Dāwūd, peace be upon him, is reported to have said that God made the servant's awareness of the impossibility of fully praising Him a way of giving thanks, and awareness of inability to know Him fully itself a kind of recognition. Sahl b. ʿAbd Allāh al-Tustarī is reported to have said that the divine Essence is described through knowledge but cannot be enclosed by it: the Divine has veiled creation from the final reality of His Essence while directing them to His signs. Other masters have called the endpoint of such cognition perplexity and bewilderment. The one who seeks unification by abandoning every distinction nevertheless has not grasped what is beyond every distinction.

### [834] The inherited sayings about recognition and its limits

A master is reported to have said, “My Lord, increase me in bewilderment concerning You.” Yūsuf b. al-Ḥusayn speaks of the ocean of divine unity as one in which love and recognition continually intensify; the journey does not culminate in comprehending its source. Al-Ḥusayn b. Manṣūr al-Ḥallāj reports that the Real cannot be contained in an utterance or adequately grasped by a mind. Al-Shiblī, asked for a phrase concerning divine unity, replied that the speaker is heedless, the silent one ignorant, the one who points towards Him a polytheist, the one who claims nearness distant, the one who feigns comprehension unaware, and the one who witnesses through his own imagined representation has taken that representation for the Real. All that the intellects, fancies, or understandings enumerate of Him is a created and delimited object.

Al-Shiblī also says, “No one who has merely smelled the fragrance of divine unification can suppose that he has attained it.” Ibn ʿAṭāʾ says that reason is an instrument for service, not for arrogating complete knowledge of the divine Lordship. Al-Rāzī compares intellect to a capacity traversing the world of origination: if it were to reach the Reality that originates the world, its finite pretensions would dissolve. He says that those who have attained the deepest insight say, “We have not known Him except through confession that we cannot encompass Him.” Others say, “None truly knows God but God.”

A saying attributed to Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq praises God who has not made a route for created beings to knowing Him through an encompassing grasp, but rather through the recognition of their incapacity to know Him completely. Al-Wāsiṭī warns that recognition does not disclose a way for the servant to claim ownership of God: it is marked by the servant's need and surrender, while the true knower is effaced. ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Jīlānī says that the gates of understanding are screened from the ultimate majesty of the Real. Intellect cannot measure Him, imagination cannot represent Him, and finite instruments cannot reach the source of divine beauty. The highest accomplishment of these faculties is to discern the limits of their own judgments.

ʿAlī, may God be pleased with him, says that recognition of the Real means knowing that everything a person represents in his mind as God falls short of Him. Such speech must be understood according to the registers and qualifications that the speakers themselves intend. The discussion continues in the following chapter by appealing also to rational and persuasive evidence.

## [835–838] Chapter Two — Rational and persuasive arguments in agreement with transmitted evidence

### Rational argument I: the infinite is not exhausted by finite concepts

First: God, exalted is He, is without limit in Essence and attributes, whereas the intellect is limited in its own essence and properties. What is finite cannot bring the infinite fully before itself in detailed apprehension. For example, intellect is incapable of comprehending God's beginninglessness as such: whatever expanse of earlier time intellect or imagination reaches, the Real is not beginningless because He existed within that expanse, but because His existence is beyond it. The same applies to an exhaustive knowledge of His endless continuance. Our perfected master Zayn al-Dīn al-Khāfī says that His pre-eternity surpasses what intellects grasp through the notion of pre-eternity, and His endlessness exceeds what understandings grasp through the notion of endless duration.

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), Arabic edition printed p. 835, PDF index 21, argument 1. *Editorial restriction:* Complete prose argument according to printed p. 835, with quotation attributed by the author to Zayn al-Dīn al-Khāfī; attribution requires separate historical checking.

### Rational argument II: the knower cannot encompass even the self

Second: A human being cannot fully know his own self, the self designated by the word 'I'. God says, 'They ask you about the spirit. Say: the spirit belongs to my Lord's command, and you have been given little knowledge' [Q 17:85]. If this is true of what is most immediately evident to a person, how much more of the Reality furthest beyond the connections of intellect and imagination? Intellect resembles a candle: what is nearest to the flame is ordinarily the most illuminated, and nothing is nearer to something than itself. If the light of intellect is too weak to illuminate its own reality fully, how could it illuminate the divine Presence of Majesty, immeasurably beyond it? Zayn al-Dīn al-Khāfī therefore says that whatever a person witnesses through insight or grasps through knowledge, the Real is above it.

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 835, PDF index 21, argument 2, prose through al-Khāfī quotation; poems excluded. *Editorial restriction:* The printed argument continues with Arabic and Persian verse and further observations about the senses and the nature of time and space. Those have not been translated in this record; the passage is therefore an excerpt, not the complete argument.

### Rational argument III: the lamp and the sun

Third: It is established in the sciences of reality that God is absolute Light, the Light of lights. Just as a lamp has no visible effect when placed before the disc of the sun, the light of intellect diminishes, perishes and disappears before the light of divine Majesty. Hence it is said that intellect ranges among created things, but when it reaches the Presence of their Creator it melts away. Intellect is also compared to a drop disappearing into the sea. Yet these illustrations must not be mistaken for exact comparisons: both the lamp and the sun, the drop and the sea, are finite, whereas the actual contrast is between the limited light of intellect and the immeasurable majesty of God. How, then, could intellect's distinct light remain before the radiance of His Majesty?

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 836, PDF index 22, argument 3. *Editorial restriction:* The edition says that the lamp/sun and drop/sea comparisons are finite-to-finite analogies; the ultimate epistemic relation is not reducible to quantitative size. Earlier English draft was less precise here; this replaces its paraphrase.

### Rational argument IV: the Most Manifest and the Hidden

Fourth: The light of intellect stands in relation to the source of spiritual lights as the light of bodily sight stands to the brightest of corporeal lights. The sun is, in one respect, the most evident thing to vision and, in another respect, the most hidden. So the Light of lights, in relation to the eye of insight, is in one respect the most evident of all and in another the most hidden. The corroborating names are: He is the Manifest; He is the Hidden.

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 836, PDF index 22, argument 4. *Editorial restriction:* Manifestness and hiddenness are predicated under different aspects, not as formal contradictories in the same respect.

### Rational argument V: time, place and the limit of negation

Fifth: Intellect operates only upon what lies within time and place. Whatever it apprehends, it apprehends as past, future, or present, all of which fall under time; and whatever it conceives, it conceives as here or there, all of which fall under place. If you say that the Real is unlike this, what you have known in this knowledge is the negation from Him of what is other than the Real. But negating what is other than the Real is not itself the very finding of the Real.

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 836, PDF page index 22. *Editorial restriction:* Independently checked on the rendered 2023 Arabic source page. This describes the discursive intellect considered here; it does not establish that every possible intellect is spatial.

### Rational argument VI: judging perfection presupposes adequate comprehension

Sixth: People commonly say that none can discriminate between one accomplished scholar and another outstanding expert except someone more learned than both. To judge whether the two are equal or differ in excellence, the judge would have to comprehend the extent of their knowledge and their respective merits. How, then, can deficient intellects encompass the perfection of God's Majesty and the attributes of His Beauty?

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 836, PDF index 22, argument 6. *Editorial restriction:* This argument challenges the claim of an exhaustive appraisal of divine perfection. It does not, by itself, rule out every true predication about divine attributes.

### Rational argument VII: genuine knowledge of non-encompassment

Seventh: In its ordinary operation apprehension belongs to intellect, yet intellect apprehends only what is determinate like itself. It cannot reach the unification intended here because it establishes distinguishing forms, whereas this unification requires their effacement: the absorption of plurality into the source of unicity and the disappearance of origination before the beginningless. God says, 'Everything is perishing except His Face' [Q 28:88]. Intellect cannot accomplish this by its own resources. If, however, God makes the servant aware of intellect's inability, and the servant ceases to expect that discursive thought can wholly attain the goal, he acknowledges his incapacity and confesses his ignorance. This is the furthest point of such knowledge: intellect cannot encompass subtleties and realities whose hiddenness or inexhaustible multiplicity defeat it. Yet its recognition that what cannot be encompassed remains beyond its grasp is itself a genuine kind of knowledge. As Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq is reported to have said: 'Inability to comprehend comprehension is itself a comprehension.'

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 836, PDF index 22, argument 7. *Editorial restriction:* The saying attributed to Abū Bakr requires authentication. This negative cognition of non-encompassment is not presented as identical with the positive plenitude of mystical realization. The end of the Arabic has been normalized from rendered PDF and extracted text; manuscript collation remains outstanding.

### Rational argument VIII: the desert mirage and the turn to divine guidance

Eighth: They say that when a person is in a vast desert, overcome by heat and thirst with no water to be found, he takes whatever direction he looks in to offer water and drink. Upon reaching it he finds nothing: it was a mirage. Similarly, human intellects and souls stand in the desert of divine Majesty, dazzled by the brilliance of its light. Longing for the divine Presence grows intense, and each person supposes that the direction in which he looks contains the water sought. Those who negate attributes turn toward transcendence, those who liken God to creation toward affirmation, and the parties of compulsion and human freedom likewise take different directions. The author presents these sectarian positions as paths that, when absolutized, may fail to reach what they seek, citing the Qurʾānic image of deeds made as scattered dust [Q 25:23]. The spiritually strong, in contrast, recognise humanity's incapacity and intellect's bewilderment, cease relying on themselves, and seek God's guidance. Thus Abraham asks for judgement [Q 26:83], Joseph asks to die in submission [Q 12:101], Moses asks that his breast be expanded [Q 20:25], and Jesus distinguishes his own knowledge from God's [Q 5:116]. The text closes with a saying attributed to the Prophet: 'Show us things as they are.'

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 837, PDF index 23, argument 8; all Arabic prose and quoted scriptural phrases. *Editorial restriction:* Translation of the authorial argument, not an endorsement of the author’s sectarian generalisation. ### Rational argument IX: the child reaching for an image in a mirror

Ninth: A child looks into a mirror and sees a form there. Desiring it, the child approaches, and the image appears to approach in return. Wanting to grasp it, the child reaches out, only to meet the mirror's hard barrier and lose the image as an object of grasping. So it is with the intellectual faculty contemplating the mirror of 'whoever knows himself': it receives a glimmer of the lights of 'knows his Lord'. The more intently it looks, the greater and fuller the disclosure appears. Intellect longs to attain the ultimate reality of divine self-sufficiency and extends the hand of seeking, but discovers between itself and the object sought the veil of beginninglessness, eternity and necessity. It remains unable to pass this barrier. Even the act of reaching can cause the disclosure to become concealed behind the overwhelming radiances of divine Majesty.

### Rational argument X: withdrawal from other things and the limits of praising God

Tenth: The more completely a person withdraws contemplative attention from what is other than the Real, and the more fully he is absorbed in knowing God, the more complete that knowledge becomes. ## [838–839] Chapter Three — Why the divine Majesty defeats exhaustive cognition

The Imam Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī says: Know that the reason human understandings fail to attain the comprehension of God, despite the fact that He is the most manifest of all realities and His Majesty the most glorious, is precisely the very extremity of that manifestness. If all created things gave no evidence of Him but an isolated object did, the observer might more easily single Him out under a particular relation. But because all existents point toward Him, in their origin and in their continued being, His signs fill the entire world. And because of their universality, they may fail to be singled out by someone whose faculty of perception is accustomed to objects separated and contrasted with one another.

A poetic line says, in effect: “Every thing contains a sign pointing to the One.” If bodily sight were so feeble that the sun's rays, rather than illuminating things, overpowered the seeing faculty, the very intensity of brightness would veil what it made visible. In the same way the perfection of divine manifestness may become the occasion of human concealment, not because the Real is absent but because the receiving capacity is restricted. If the sun shone equally upon every corporeal object without alternative conditions, the observing eye might not recognise the light as such, because our usual perception of light depends on its contrast with shade. We discover light through the differences among things it illuminates. If those differences did not occur, the common luminous cause could be ignored.

Thus the complete evidence of divine Reality is in one respect also a veil for human faculties. A finite intellect attempting to embrace all of those testimonies cannot make the Real into one more delimited object beside them. This is why al-Ghazālī, as cited here, understands the excess of evidence and illumination—not a lack of divine actuality—as a cause of perplexity.

### [839] Chapter Four: the saying attributed to Abū Ḥanīfa

**Chapter Four: Establishing the meaning of the saying related from the Lamp of the Community, the Greatest Imam, Abū Ḥanīfa—may God be pleased with him.** It is reported that he said: “We have not worshipped You as You deserve to be worshipped, but we have known You as You deserve to be known.”

Know that he meant by the right of knowledge the *knowledge required by the religious law*, that which God commanded His servants to possess. In the consideration of the law, this stands in the place of the complete knowledge appropriate to the obligations of its subjects, as his words in *al-Fiqh al-Akbar* show. There is no contradiction in this report to the position we are establishing, for what we mean by true and complete recognition is to become acquainted with the ultimate reality (*kunh*) of the divine Essence exactly as it is in itself. A further possibility is that the Imam intended his recognition of the very subtle realities and finely distinguished matters of the divine affair, so far beyond enumeration and measure that attempts to circumscribe them must end in confession of incapacity. Since that reality is far above determination, the right of recognition here consists in acknowledging that one cannot comprehend it, and a true knowledge of what is not encompassed may include knowing that one cannot encompass it. It is reported from al-Ṣiddīq: “Inability to grasp the grasping is itself a grasping.”

The station in question requires some unfolding, even if its most complete explanation must remain an allusion. It is the Presence of the Most Holy Essence, the unseen of Identity, absoluteness and beginninglessness, in which the consideration of endlessness is already contained. It is neither witnessed nor conceived nor known nor apprehended under any determinate form whatsoever, and it does not fall under any determinate judgement. It is restricted neither by a limitation nor by the opposite qualification, “unrestricted”; it transcends both restriction and nonrestriction, for it is the Reality of existence insofar as it is existence. Beyond it there is only absolute nonexistence, established neither mentally nor outwardly.

Now suppose the highest forms of comprehension, knowledge and witnessing reach their limits, so that incapacity and deficiency become evident to the knower. When he recognises that beyond what he knows and the outer boundary of his witnessing lie oceans in which all divers and contemplatives become bewildered, he has reached an important recognition of his limitation. Even the most complete apprehension cannot make the ultimate Reality into an ordinary object possessed by a human faculty. The sayings of the spiritually perfected which confess their inability must be understood accordingly.

### [840] The unseen of Identity and the differentiated ranks of the journey

The special unseen Presence meant by the passage just explained is the inward meaning of the noble revealed expression under discussion. It is hidden beyond what can be marked out in the Preserved Tablet and beyond the second rank of intelligible determination, and indeed beyond the totality of things that can be conceived, represented or grasped. It is not the result of placing a further hidden object after the sequence of ordinary things. Nor does denial of such a limit imply a denial that real recognitions are granted to the servants of God.

Here al-Iznīqī turns to a long account attributed to ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Jīlānī, describing the servant's successive transformations. A wayfarer turns aside from the power of Satan, relinquishes the world and the later world's attachments, opposes his passions and bad habits, and subjects the self to discipline and self-accounting. He withdraws from making his own deeds and spiritual states into possessions. When he abandons corrupting attachments and worships in sincerity, he proceeds from one doorway of the way to another. At the opening of the door of satisfaction with the divine decree, his own projects cease to compete with the divine command. He rises through further openings, receiving purification, and moves toward the Presence of holiness and intimacy.

The Sufi masters also distinguish several kinds of *fanāʾ* (the passing away of particular self-conscious dispositions) and *baqāʾ* (endurance by divine gift). The wayfarer does not gain an independent eternal existence or become a second divinity; rather, the capacities and claims that previously obstructed his reception of the Real are gradually stripped away. Under the most complete circumstances, created powers and intentions are not regarded as sovereign opposites to God. The higher the rank reached, the less appropriate it becomes to claim that divine Reality has been confined to the individual's own experience.

### [841] Servanthood, proximity, and the cessation of separate self-claim

The quoted description continues: such servants are guarded by God even while they still act within their embodied lives. When called to attend to outward duties, they return to them, observe them, and guard their hearts. Their regard is upon divine care rather than their own supposedly independent accomplishments. The servant is addressed with the words, “Enter Egypt, God willing, in security” [Q 12:99 as used in the surrounding discourse], and recognises that the divine command extends across the different stations without annulling their distinct obligations.

A report from the Prophet concerning the approach of the servant through obligatory and additional worship is cited: the servant draws near to God by that which has been made obligatory, then through supererogatory works until divine love is granted. The famous language of God becoming the servant's hearing and sight is invoked to express dependence and the transformation of the servant's operation, not the physical replacement of created organs by the divine Essence. The author then cites a saying attributed to Moses asking where he might find God, receiving the answer that He is present in the heart of the one who relinquishes possessiveness and worldly grasping. These reports, especially where wording has been conflated or expanded, require independent textual authentication.

Once the servant's soul has been brought under discipline, he passes through further degrees metaphorically figured as the kingdoms of power, instruction, majesty, beauty, awe, mercy, gentleness and intimacy. The sequence is not a catalogue of separate independently existing gods or ontological substances. It describes the increasing evacuation of self-preference and the opening of faculties to the divine gift. Nearness gives way to love, love to a further attraction, attraction to an opening of intimate discourse, and that to a divinely bestowed effusion. When the servant reaches this point, separate self-assertion fails, speech becomes inadequate, and the customary vocabulary of the attributes falls silent. The passage insists that the end of an accomplished saint's rank is still to be distinguished from the distinctive rank of prophecy and the sealing of prophecy.

Al-Iznīqī then adduces a further passage from Ibn ʿArabī: the essential self-disclosure is received in accordance with the capacity and preparedness of the recipient. If someone sees only a reflection, even a genuine reflection, he has not thereby brought the Real within the mirror, and cannot claim to have possessed the unlimited Essence.

### [842] Ibn ʿArabī's mirror; apparently contradictory predications

The cited Ibn ʿArabī passage develops the analogy. In a mirror one sees an image and cannot simultaneously inspect the mirror as a distinct object in the same attention. The image is truly connected with the person reflected, but it does not contain the person's independent reality. A divine disclosure is not literally a reflected human image, yet the analogy points to a genuine relation between what appears and a recipient without making the recipient into the ground of the Real's being. The Real appears to the servant under the form appropriate to the servant's preparedness, and the servant must not suppose that this has confined the Essence.

Qūnawī's and Ibn ʿArabī's technical expressions distinguish the mirror from what is manifest in it. When the Real and creation are spoken of, under differing respects, as manifest and appearing, unseen and witnessed, or inward and outward, an observer may become confused. An inaccurate observer treats every such predicate as if it occurred with one and the same reference. In some modes of witnessing this difficulty produces perplexity and a truthful confession that comprehension has failed. Such confession is sound knowledge of a limit, provided that it is not confused with ignorance of what a person is actually obliged and able to know. One can know that a thing is not fully known; this is not the same as knowing nothing about it.

The highest station of recognition cannot be exhausted in any one of the limited stations of its recipients. The most complete human witnessing has a comprehensive aspect: rather than excluding the various appearances as rivals, it gathers them according to their corresponding determinations. For that reason a statement may be right within the station and perspective in which it is uttered while being incapable of serving as the absolute measure for all the others. The text cites the spiritually complete who are not restricted to one kind of manifestation, and it presents genuine unity as compatible with many distinct legitimate relations, without rendering the Essence numerically divisible.

### [843] The most comprehensive human reality and the language of unity

The discussion continues from the long quotation of the Akbarī authorities. The perfected human being is described as the comprehensive form which gathers the realities of the divine Names and the ranks in which created things receive their shares of manifestation. Its distinctive human constitution is not one more divine essence, and its comprehensiveness does not annihilate the distinction between the servant and the Lord. The author stresses that the perfected one's knowledge and witnessed disclosure are not equivalent to a fragmented grasp of one spiritual attribute at the expense of all others. In the more comprehensive station, inward and outward, gathering and differentiation, appear within a unified account while still being distinguishable under their proper considerations.

When God unveils these realities to His servants, their speech must be conditioned by the point from which it is uttered. The Sufi doctrine of the highest *tawḥīd* is not the mere affirmation of a numerical “one” set beside several other things. The presence of a speaker who boasts “I have united the Real” reintroduces the very distinction that the highest expression seeks to pass beyond. The inherited sayings therefore speak of the one who unifies and the one who is unified as inadequate terms when placed alongside the essential truth of unity.

Such negation is not a call to deny the real obligation of the servant, or to identify the self-existing Creator with the dependent human being in every respect. The descriptions of utmost *fanāʾ* affirm that no independent created claimant remains in the spiritual apprehension, not that the religious law has become void or that the human person may declare himself God. The text's argument is that differing uses of unity, existence, knowledge, manifestation and incapacity must be traced to their respective objects and stations.

## DRAFT SEGMENT 4: 03_pp844-849


**Source:** Balaban–Çalışkan, *İhya* 9/2 (2023), medieval Arabic text printed pp. 844–849. Independently written English prose rendered from the edition's page images; medieval verses and quotations have their own attribution cautions. **Status: scholarly draft, not exhaustive or manuscript collated.** Modern footnotes excluded.

### [844] True unity is not an achievement of the one who claims to unify

One of the Sufi masters asks whether the person who proclaims unity by his own act has really established the One. The one who makes an act of unification and thereby draws a distinction between himself and the Real has indeed established the distinction he wished to escape. A unification that contains the mark of an independent claimant is still clothed in otherness. The true unification under discussion is that by which every separate form and effect is effaced, leaving no independent thing which can be presented as the object of a second creator.

The language of the unity of the Real must therefore be distinguished according to the rank of the discourse. In an inferior rank there is still an affirmation of the One over against another, and a speaker who performs that affirmation. In the essential rank, the limitation of all these terms is exposed. The subject of such discourse is not a second, invisible body that ought to replace created bodies, but the Real, of whose unity even the highest finite formulation falls short.

The text cites one poet: “I witnessed my own self through the attributes of the Reality, and what I had attributed to myself veiled me from my Beloved.” Here the spiritual observer does not mean that the finite creature has become a self-existing Lord. Rather, the observer's successive degrees of self-regard must be passed through until the distinction no longer functions as an object independently owned. Among the sayings of Ibn ʿArabī, Ibn al-Fāriḍ, and later poets are verses that make the same point with increasingly paradoxical expressions: “I witnessed”, “I was witnessed”, “the observer”, and “the observed” are not stable descriptions of a fixed and unchanging degree of realization. Their meanings depend on the station from which the speaker uses them.

The passage also quotes the saying “No one declares the One to be one except the One,” and interprets the person who performs an independent act of unification as *jāḥid* (one who negates or denies), since his independent act itself establishes what he claimed to remove. If the servant passes away in the act of unification, a different sense of the expression becomes intelligible. The long poetic quotation which follows attempts to convey this distinction through the first-person rhetoric of spiritual vision.

### [845] Mystical poetry: one reality, many names and the passing away of self-description

Here the treatise gives an extended Arabic poem attributed to Ibn al-Fāriḍ. Its poet addresses an inward witness and a beloved whose reality is concealed by all forms of ordinary naming. The poem speaks of a gathering in which the separations between the poet's knowledge, the reality witnessed, and the recipient's self-conscious representations cease to dominate his awareness. In the language of those states, the poet sees the Beloved wherever he turns; what had first appeared an object outside him is now the principle by which every perception receives its meaning.

The poetic voice says in effect: “I witnessed through myself what I had been seeking; what had stood between us fell away. Then I perceived the poverty of every name, and the claims I formerly made about union themselves became a veil.” Other lines describe return, bewilderment, the passing away of self-regard and its restoration, and how the terms “absence” and “presence” change at successive degrees.

Al-Iznīqī follows with Persian verses attributed to Khwāja ʿAṭṭār: whether the knower is considered under the rank of unity or the appearance of multiplicity, the many names and forms of the things we see do not establish many independent absolutes. The same point is illustrated through the words of Rūmī and another Turkish-Persian mystical poet, who compare exterior difference with interior belonging to a single source. **These poems remain among the lines that need a fresh bilingual transcription and verse-by-verse metrical translation.** They are described here in relation to the prose argument rather than presented as a literal poem translation.

### [846] Why a description of essential unity must not be read as incarnation

Following the poems, the author returns to prose. You should know, he says, that the affirmation of the true Necessary Reality and the acceptance of the One as the one absolute Reality are not innovations against the law of Islam. The words of the spiritual authorities, including the accomplished masters whose sayings have just been cited, do not amount to making God a thing that indwells in bodies, or to granting created souls independent divinity. To say that the Real manifests without being contained by the manifold loci of manifestation is not the same as saying that a created form is itself divine under every respect.

The text here invokes Zayn al-Dīn al-Khāfī, whose formulation excludes *ḥulūl* (incarnation or indwelling) within spirits and bodies. Under the viewpoint being defended, the multiple manifestations do not divide the self-existing reality of God, nor does the procession of ranks entail that divine life was previously deprived of some perfection. The spiritual claim is one of priority and dependence: the created forms are what they are through God, and the Real is not sustained by the forms.

Al-Iznīqī adduces the Qurʾān, inherited reports, and the language of the established scholars of Sunnī jurisprudence to defend religious continuity. He insists that the Quranic and Prophetic sources are to be taken seriously, and that expressions whose surface appearance might violate the necessary religious distinction between Creator and creature cannot be treated as unqualified assertions of that violation. If an expression has an admissible sense consonant with revelation and the speaker's established principles, the interpreter must not automatically insist on the unacceptable meaning.

Neither should the existence of spiritually exalted people make the general disciplines of law and theological truth irrelevant. Al-Iznīqī opposes those who treat the claims of vision as a license to dispense with basic doctrines or commands. Several of the quotations gathered here are from other authorities; the author deploys them as evidence for the interpretive position he is constructing.

### [847] Saints and jurists are not entitled to set the revealed law aside

The discussion continues by appeal to a common principle recognised among legal scholars: no saintly disclosure or visionary interpretation releases a person from the basic law of the Prophet. A saying attributed to the Prophet states that whoever introduces into religious practice what has no place there is rejected. Another inherited maxim describes every innovation in religion as an error inasmuch as it sets aside a necessary source of the law. If the understanding of a person, however exalted, conflicts with an established and decisive obligation, the new understanding must be measured against that obligation rather than making the law subordinate to private experience.

This is the point at which the author cites earlier Sufi masters who were careful to affirm the authority of Qurʾān and Sunnah. Al-Junayd says that a wayfarer who has not memorised the Qurʾān and studied the sayings of the Messenger should not be followed as a spiritual guide. Abū al-Qāsim al-Qushayrī is cited for the principle that true spiritual mastery must be accompanied by knowledge of religious law, and not merely by spiritual exercises.

The author's target is not the disciplined Sufi who explains a difficult phrase through the intended technical meaning. He attacks the self-declared possessor of secret knowledge who abandons prayer and worship, treats ordinary prohibitions as irrelevant, or claims an independent authority that dispenses with the distinction between God and His servant. The latter person cannot appeal to spiritual realization as a defence of blatant transgression.

A saying transmitted from the earlier masters states that one who has not learned the outward rules of law and the inward demands of the path has failed to realise the station he claims. Several witnesses to this tradition insist that the further a wayfarer progresses in inner refinement, the stronger his commitment to the revealed commands must become. An outward life of obedience and an inner life of devotion are complementary disciplines.

### [848] Fanāʾ and baqāʾ do not annul servanthood

The author quotes al-Jīlānī again: some imagine that after annihilation one no longer requires the religious law, or that the arrival at a spiritual goal releases a person from servanthood. This is false. The highest form of annihilation does not permit the worshipper to act without accountability. The most complete *fanāʾ* is the effacement of the self's independent claims and competing objects; it is not the abolition of prescribed worship or the opportunity to violate divine commands.

The true perfected person does not regard the self as a separate necessary existent. Yet this contemplative awareness of the divine source leaves the ordinary obligations of the creature intact. Annihilation and subsistence may be described as the removal of the veils that obscure dependence; in relation to the acts of a servant, they cannot logically cancel the servant's created condition. If there are expressions such as “I am the Real” in mystical discourse, they require their own interpretive context, and cannot serve as an unqualified scriptural rule for the community.

The author distinguishes inner reports of presence from outward jurisdiction. Religious obligation attaches to the outwardly responsible individual. The claim of exceptional spiritual awareness does not change the established identity of the worshipper as one addressed by divine revelation. Al-Junayd and other masters therefore regard the actions of those who knowingly and deliberately depart from obligatory worship as a departure from the authentic path, however frequently they speak of illumination.

The spiritually perfected may indeed utter startling expressions while overwhelmed by the force of a momentary state. Such expressions should be understood as reports of their state, and should not be used to fashion a universal creed denying creaturely dependence. A distinction is required between an actual spiritual event, a distorted intellectual inference from it, and a public claim that legal responsibilities no longer bind the claimant.

### [849] Figurative expressions, exceptional states and the limits of mutual accusation

The author further cautions that those who experience a powerful disclosure may speak in an abbreviated or figurative idiom. The right response is not to dismiss their entire discipline, nor to treat every paradox as a literal truth to be affirmed without conditions. A metaphor may indicate a relation of dependence, the disappearance of independent agency, or the loss of self-attention, without signifying that the creature has absorbed the Essence into a finite form.

He illustrates this with the terms attributed to the early caliphs and the Sufi elders, including “I saw nothing without seeing God before it,” “I saw nothing except that I saw God with it,” and “I saw nothing except that I saw God after it.” The differences in these formulations belong to different perspectives and modes of witnessing. The expressions are not necessarily rival accounts of the existence of multiple gods. Their authority and the precise wording of their attributions need further separate checks.

Persian and Arabic verses on the experience of intoxication and soberness reinforce the point. The image of wine in a cup may be used to indicate a quality or appearance; it does not require a person to infer that the physical cup and its contents are numerically one object. A person who utters an exceptional expression under an overwhelming state cannot automatically be assimilated to a philosopher asserting a permanent and general formula, or to a lawbreaker rejecting a necessary article of faith.

The conclusion of this discussion is juridical and spiritual at once. Human beings differ in their cognitive capacities, in the conditions under which their judgements are made, and in the clarity of the evidence made available to them. A person ought to seek the best interpretation that preserves what is firmly established in the law. One may reject what is demonstrably contrary to revelation without giving a licence for hasty accusations against all those who employ unfamiliar language. Al-Iznīqī returns at the end of the treatise to the issue of the limits of doctrinal assent and the obligation of restraint toward the people of the qibla.

## DRAFT SEGMENT 5: 04_pp850-852


**Source:** M. S. Balaban and A. Çalışkan (eds.), *İhya* 9/2 (2023), authorial text pp. 831–852, here the concluding three printed pages. This English is a fresh reading from rendered printed-page images, with present-day bibliographical footnotes omitted. **Draft:** close rendering of the argument, not yet manuscript-collated or a word-for-word critical edition of all embedded citations.

### [850] Final defence of agreed first principles; Conclusion, part one

Every party must ultimately return for doctrinal judgement to the established, unequivocal texts, not to a private understanding that overturns those texts. People of the established schools—the Jabriyya, Qadariyya, Mujassima, Muʿtazila, Khawārij, Rāfiḍa and others—may dispute the meanings of particular scriptural texts while still making the Qurʾān and the Sunnah the reference for their arguments. When opposing interpretations collide, the clear and decisive passages must govern disputed or ambiguous ones, not the reverse. The author invokes the distinction between firm (*muḥkam*) and ambiguous (*mutashābih*) discourse and warnings against seeking the ambiguous merely to sow dissension. He also invokes reported Prophetic statements against sectarian conflict and misappropriation of religion.

Many apparently incompatible theological descriptions of God arise because a debater takes a statement made under one qualified meaning and applies it under a different one. A statement of divine nearness is not necessarily a declaration of incarnation, and a statement of divine transcendence need not imply the absence of divine knowledge or providence. The criterion is the intended sense within the established foundations of revelation. The author's attachment to the people of Sunnah is expressed strongly, but the same discussion repeatedly warns that interpretation must precede condemnation.

The author concludes the main defence by invoking the Qurʾānic announcement that God sent His Messenger with guidance and the religion of truth, and by praising those who sustain the community's revealed acts and institutions. Such an expression of communal allegiance is not an argument that one ought to anathematise every individual who errs in disputed terminology. Praise belongs to God who guided us to this, and without His guidance we could never have been guided.

**Conclusion.** It contains two chapters.

**First chapter: Explaining what is meant by the mistaken interpretation and clarifying the right meaning.**

Know that, on the principle discussed earlier, faith does not increase or diminish in its very essence. Such is the statement associated with the Lamp of the Community and the greatest of the imams, Abū Ḥanīfa—may God be pleased with him. Faith in itself is the affirmation or assent (*taṣdīq*); the core act of assent does not admit of more and less in the same way as things with measurable magnitude, although associated aspects can differ. This is because the content to which faith assents is the divine truth, not an amount of substance that grows and shrinks with each later event. Where increase is spoken of, the proper meaning must be sought among what belongs to faith through its strength, its consequences, its evidence, and its conduct, rather than assuming a quantitative division of its essence.

Al-Iznīqī begins to distinguish the claim that knowledge of God is *certain and adequate for the duty of faith* from a claim to have embraced the whole inner reality of the Real. Both may be called knowledge; they answer different questions. The first is the recognition required of a believer under the authoritative teaching of revelation. The second is the ultimate knowledge of the very Essence and all its inexhaustible relations. To take the first as proof of the second, or the denial of the second as a denial of the first, is the logical error at the centre of the original dispute.

### [851] Assent, greater certainty, and the schools' disagreement about increase in faith

The author develops the distinction further. The core of faith as assent is not multiplied merely because the acts and qualities accompanying it become more numerous. Works of righteousness may increase the light and stability of faith in the heart, whereas sins and disregard may weaken the dispositions that support it. The reports about increasing faith, and the Qurʾānic statement in which Abraham asks that his heart be reassured [Q 2:260], show that the believer's preparedness, composure, evidence and firmness can improve. They do not require that a proposition which was previously assented to was only partially true, or that the object of faith had become a different object.

The argument therefore distinguishes the essence of *taṣdīq* from its strength and weakness, and the general assent to necessary religious truths from a detailed knowledge of all their implications. Increased proofs may lead to greater tranquillity and resistance to doubts. Yet the author notes that scholars who define faith differently draw different conclusions from the same scriptural evidence. This is one reason a dispute about increase and decrease must not be treated as though the opponents were always denying the same proposition.

He adduces earlier theologians, including legal authorities from the Ḥanafī and Shāfiʿī traditions and noted representatives of kalām, to show the variety of technical formulations. Some define faith by internal assent; others add verbal declaration as a requirement for worldly legal treatment. A third usage treats the outward works of religion as belonging to the complete reality of faith. Where these definitions differ, disagreement about increase can be partly verbal; where they disagree over obligations, its doctrinal implications must be considered separately.

The text insists that we must not lightly dismiss the belief of the common people. Ordinary believers may possess sincere certainty without being able to demonstrate it in the sophisticated vocabulary of the theologians. A scholar's ability to refute doubts, and the exceptionally unveiled person's witnessed certainty, are different capacities from the minimum truth of faith. If the person possesses what is required, the lack of speculative proof or of mystical attainment does not remove him from Islam. Error and confusion may indeed occur in certain arguments, but the duty of interpreters is to distinguish the deficiency of a proof from the rejection of a religious truth.

The position the author endorses is that the essential assent necessary for faith is not increased or diminished by measurable increments, while its light, fullness of assurance, and accompanying qualities can be described as stronger or weaker. This is the relevant interpretation of the statement ascribed to the great Imam. Once one has determined the meaning of *faith* being used, the apparent contradiction with Qurʾānic reports of increase need not be real. This supports the larger point of the treatise: rigorous clarification of terms is preferable to the hasty accusation of other Muslims.

### [852] Conclusion, part two: restraining the tongue concerning the people of the qibla

**Second chapter: Refraining from accusations against the people of the qibla.**

Know that holding back the tongue from the people of the qibla is obligatory concerning anyone who faces it and remains within the basic profession of Islam, except where a decisive legal cause for exclusion has been established. The Messenger of God is reported to have said that three things belong to the fundamental practice of faith, among them refraining from accusations against someone who declares “There is no god but God” and not casting him outside Islam merely because of a sin. The report is cited for its intended legal and moral rule, not to deny that the religious law has specific provisions concerning apostasy.

Understand that declaring a person an unbeliever is a legal judgement, not merely an exercise in intellectual preference. The point is not that error is impossible, or that one may believe whatever one likes while retaining the same doctrinal standing. Rather, where a person professes the faith, departure from it must be established by a decisive expression or act and under the conditions and exceptions recognised by jurists. The religious law does not make a person an unbeliever merely because one of his expressions is liable to a hostile interpretation.

Some texts or actions are indeed regarded as demonstrably contrary to Islam: a direct and unqualified denial of a necessary truth, an explicit rejection of the authority of revelation, or deliberately treating forbidden things as permitted when their prohibition is decisive. But doubt about a disputed proposition, a doubtful implication drawn by an opponent, or a technical expression used in a different discipline is not automatically equivalent to such explicit repudiation. Where an interpretation is possible, its grounds must be assessed before the charge is pronounced.

**Question:** What if the outward wording or an act appears to entail unbelief under one interpretation? **Answer:** If there is a single sound and legally permissible construction which does not entail unbelief, the jurist should lean toward the construction that preserves the person's standing as a believer. The author cites the Prophetic warning not to suspect evil of a word spoken by a brother while one can find a good construction for it. The requirement here is not to conceal proven wrongdoing but to reject reckless certainty in doubtful cases. 

The treatise closes, in the preserved text, with this protection of communal faith and this warning against applying technical judgements before reconstructing what a speaker intended.
