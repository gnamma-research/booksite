## [835–838] Chapter Two — Rational and persuasive arguments in agreement with transmitted evidence

### Rational argument I: the infinite is not exhausted by finite concepts

First: God, exalted is He, is without limit in Essence and attributes, whereas the intellect is limited in its own essence and properties. What is finite cannot bring the infinite fully before itself in detailed apprehension. For example, intellect is incapable of comprehending God's beginninglessness as such: whatever expanse of earlier time intellect or imagination reaches, the Real is not beginningless because He existed within that expanse, but because His existence is beyond it. The same applies to an exhaustive knowledge of His endless continuance. Our perfected master Zayn al-Dīn al-Khāfī says that His pre-eternity surpasses what intellects grasp through the notion of pre-eternity, and His endlessness exceeds what understandings grasp through the notion of endless duration.

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), Arabic edition printed p. 835, PDF index 21, argument 1. *Editorial restriction:* Complete prose argument according to printed p. 835, with quotation attributed by the author to Zayn al-Dīn al-Khāfī; attribution requires separate historical checking.

### Rational argument II: the knower cannot encompass even the self

Second: A human being cannot fully know his own self, the self designated by the word 'I'. God says, 'They ask you about the spirit. Say: the spirit belongs to my Lord's command, and you have been given little knowledge' [Q 17:85]. If this is true of what is most immediately evident to a person, how much more of the Reality furthest beyond the connections of intellect and imagination? Intellect resembles a candle: what is nearest to the flame is ordinarily the most illuminated, and nothing is nearer to something than itself. If the light of intellect is too weak to illuminate its own reality fully, how could it illuminate the divine Presence of Majesty, immeasurably beyond it? Zayn al-Dīn al-Khāfī therefore says that whatever a person witnesses through insight or grasps through knowledge, the Real is above it.

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 835, PDF index 21, argument 2, prose through al-Khāfī quotation; poems excluded. *Editorial restriction:* The printed argument continues with Arabic and Persian verse and further observations about the senses and the nature of time and space. Those have not been translated in this record; the passage is therefore an excerpt, not the complete argument.

### Rational argument III: the lamp and the sun

Third: It is established in the sciences of reality that God is absolute Light, the Light of lights. Just as a lamp has no visible effect when placed before the disc of the sun, the light of intellect diminishes, perishes and disappears before the light of divine Majesty. Hence it is said that intellect ranges among created things, but when it reaches the Presence of their Creator it melts away. Intellect is also compared to a drop disappearing into the sea. Yet these illustrations must not be mistaken for exact comparisons: both the lamp and the sun, the drop and the sea, are finite, whereas the actual contrast is between the limited light of intellect and the immeasurable majesty of God. How, then, could intellect's distinct light remain before the radiance of His Majesty?

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 836, PDF index 22, argument 3. *Editorial restriction:* The edition says that the lamp/sun and drop/sea comparisons are finite-to-finite analogies; the ultimate epistemic relation is not reducible to quantitative size. Earlier English draft was less precise here; this replaces its paraphrase.

### Rational argument IV: the Most Manifest and the Hidden

Fourth: The light of intellect stands in relation to the source of spiritual lights as the light of bodily sight stands to the brightest of corporeal lights. The sun is, in one respect, the most evident thing to vision and, in another respect, the most hidden. So the Light of lights, in relation to the eye of insight, is in one respect the most evident of all and in another the most hidden. The corroborating names are: He is the Manifest; He is the Hidden.

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 836, PDF index 22, argument 4. *Editorial restriction:* Manifestness and hiddenness are predicated under different aspects, not as formal contradictories in the same respect.

### Rational argument V: time, place and the limit of negation

Fifth: Intellect operates only upon what lies within time and place. Whatever it apprehends, it apprehends as past, future, or present, all of which fall under time; and whatever it conceives, it conceives as here or there, all of which fall under place. If you say that the Real is unlike this, what you have known in this knowledge is the negation from Him of what is other than the Real. But negating what is other than the Real is not itself the very finding of the Real.

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 836, PDF page index 22. *Editorial restriction:* Independently checked on the rendered 2023 Arabic source page. This describes the discursive intellect considered here; it does not establish that every possible intellect is spatial.

### Rational argument VI: judging perfection presupposes adequate comprehension

Sixth: People commonly say that none can discriminate between one accomplished scholar and another outstanding expert except someone more learned than both. To judge whether the two are equal or differ in excellence, the judge would have to comprehend the extent of their knowledge and their respective merits. How, then, can deficient intellects encompass the perfection of God's Majesty and the attributes of His Beauty?

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 836, PDF index 22, argument 6. *Editorial restriction:* This argument challenges the claim of an exhaustive appraisal of divine perfection. It does not, by itself, rule out every true predication about divine attributes.

### Rational argument VII: genuine knowledge of non-encompassment

Seventh: In its ordinary operation apprehension belongs to intellect, yet intellect apprehends only what is determinate like itself. It cannot reach the unification intended here because it establishes distinguishing forms, whereas this unification requires their effacement: the absorption of plurality into the source of unicity and the disappearance of origination before the beginningless. God says, 'Everything is perishing except His Face' [Q 28:88]. Intellect cannot accomplish this by its own resources. If, however, God makes the servant aware of intellect's inability, and the servant ceases to expect that discursive thought can wholly attain the goal, he acknowledges his incapacity and confesses his ignorance. This is the furthest point of such knowledge: intellect cannot encompass subtleties and realities whose hiddenness or inexhaustible multiplicity defeat it. Yet its recognition that what cannot be encompassed remains beyond its grasp is itself a genuine kind of knowledge. As Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq is reported to have said: 'Inability to comprehend comprehension is itself a comprehension.'

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 836, PDF index 22, argument 7. *Editorial restriction:* The saying attributed to Abū Bakr requires authentication. This negative cognition of non-encompassment is not presented as identical with the positive plenitude of mystical realization. The end of the Arabic has been normalized from rendered PDF and extracted text; manuscript collation remains outstanding.

### Rational argument VIII: the desert mirage and the turn to divine guidance

Eighth: They say that when a person is in a vast desert, overcome by heat and thirst with no water to be found, he takes whatever direction he looks in to offer water and drink. Upon reaching it he finds nothing: it was a mirage. Similarly, human intellects and souls stand in the desert of divine Majesty, dazzled by the brilliance of its light. Longing for the divine Presence grows intense, and each person supposes that the direction in which he looks contains the water sought. Those who negate attributes turn toward transcendence, those who liken God to creation toward affirmation, and the parties of compulsion and human freedom likewise take different directions. The author presents these sectarian positions as paths that, when absolutized, may fail to reach what they seek, citing the Qurʾānic image of deeds made as scattered dust [Q 25:23]. The spiritually strong, in contrast, recognise humanity's incapacity and intellect's bewilderment, cease relying on themselves, and seek God's guidance. Thus Abraham asks for judgement [Q 26:83], Joseph asks to die in submission [Q 12:101], Moses asks that his breast be expanded [Q 20:25], and Jesus distinguishes his own knowledge from God's [Q 5:116]. The text closes with a saying attributed to the Prophet: 'Show us things as they are.'

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 837, PDF index 23, argument 8; all Arabic prose and quoted scriptural phrases. *Editorial restriction:* Translation of the authorial argument, not an endorsement of the author’s sectarian generalisation. Verse references are printed alongside the passages; the final saying's prophetic attribution needs authentication. 'Those who negate attributes' is an interpretative rendering of *muʿaṭṭila*.

### Rational argument IX: the child reaching for an image in a mirror

Ninth: A child looks into a mirror and sees a form there. Desiring it, the child approaches, and the image appears to approach in return. Wanting to grasp it, the child reaches out, only to meet the mirror's hard barrier and lose the image as an object of grasping. So it is with the intellectual faculty contemplating the mirror of 'whoever knows himself': it receives a glimmer of the lights of 'knows his Lord'. The more intently it looks, the greater and fuller the disclosure appears. Intellect longs to attain the ultimate reality of divine self-sufficiency and extends the hand of seeking, but discovers between itself and the object sought the veil of beginninglessness, eternity and necessity. It remains unable to pass this barrier. Even the act of reaching can cause the disclosure to become concealed behind the overwhelming radiances of divine Majesty.

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 837, PDF index 23, argument 9; full anecdote and conclusion, later Persian poems omitted. *Editorial restriction:* The 'whoever knows himself knows his Lord' formula is alluded to and its attribution must be assessed separately. The following editorial observation and Persian verses on p. 837 are still not included. This is an excerpt, not the entire ninth argument with poems.

### Rational argument X: withdrawal from other things and the limits of praising God

Tenth: The more completely a person withdraws contemplative attention from what is other than the Real, and the more fully he is absorbed in knowing God, the more complete that knowledge becomes. Hence it is said that estrangement from self-attachment is proportionate to one's knowledge of the Lord. Yet even Muḥammad—peace be upon him—who on the night in question reached the highest possible station and whose recognition was the most complete, acknowledged incapacity in praising God, saying: 'I cannot enumerate praise of You.' If he, despite his supreme nearness, did not encompass God in the manner befitting Him, how could someone below his rank do so? The author adduces another reported saying discouraging speculation about the divine Essence and the Qurʾānic words, 'Above every possessor of knowledge is one who knows' [Q 12:76].

*Source:* Balaban–Çalışkan (2023), printed p. 838, PDF index 24, argument 10; concluding authorial prose. *Editorial restriction:* Quotation-based argument only. The prophetic report 'do not contemplate the Essence' is source-critical and requires hadith verification. 'The night in question' is a reference to the Prophet's privileged spiritual experience; do not add historical details absent from the text.

## [838–839] Chapter Three — Why the divine Majesty defeats exhaustive cognition

The Imam Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī says: Know that the reason human understandings fail to attain the comprehension of God, despite the fact that He is the most manifest of all realities and His Majesty the most glorious, is precisely the very extremity of that manifestness. If all created things gave no evidence of Him but an isolated object did, the observer might more easily single Him out under a particular relation. But because all existents point toward Him, in their origin and in their continued being, His signs fill the entire world. And because of their universality, they may fail to be singled out by someone whose faculty of perception is accustomed to objects separated and contrasted with one another.

A poetic line says, in effect: “Every thing contains a sign pointing to the One.” If bodily sight were so feeble that the sun's rays, rather than illuminating things, overpowered the seeing faculty, the very intensity of brightness would veil what it made visible. In the same way the perfection of divine manifestness may become the occasion of human concealment, not because the Real is absent but because the receiving capacity is restricted. If the sun shone equally upon every corporeal object without alternative conditions, the observing eye might not recognise the light as such, because our usual perception of light depends on its contrast with shade. We discover light through the differences among things it illuminates. If those differences did not occur, the common luminous cause could be ignored.

Thus the complete evidence of divine Reality is in one respect also a veil for human faculties. A finite intellect attempting to embrace all of those testimonies cannot make the Real into one more delimited object beside them. This is why al-Ghazālī, as cited here, understands the excess of evidence and illumination—not a lack of divine actuality—as a cause of perplexity.
