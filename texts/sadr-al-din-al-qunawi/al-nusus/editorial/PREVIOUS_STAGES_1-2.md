# I. Consecutive translation: chapter II, the arguments from reason

**Heading, printed p. 835:**

> الفصل الثاني في الحجج والدلائل العقلية والإقناعية الموافقة للنقل

**Translation:** Chapter Two: On rational and persuasive proofs consonant with transmitted evidence.

### Argument one — finitude and infinitude (p. 835)

**Translation:** First: God, exalted is He, is without limit in Essence and attributes, whereas intellect is limited in its essence and properties. What is limited has no means of encompassing what is unlimited by bringing it before itself in exhaustive detail. For example, intellect is incapable of comprehending God's beginningless antiquity, properly speaking. Each stretch of time which intellect and imagination can reach fails to constitute the measure of His pre-eternity: the Real is not called beginningless because He existed *within* that stretch of time, but because His existence lies beyond it. The same applies, by analogy, to comprehending His perpetual endlessness, in its complete and detailed meaning. Our master, the most perfected Zayn al-Dīn al-Khawāfī, said: His pre-eternity lies beyond what intellects apprehend through the meaning of the pre-eternal, and His endlessness beyond the utmost that understandings grasp under the meaning of the everlasting.

**Analytical note:** An object of intellect is formed under a bounded temporal concept, and God is not being located within an indefinitely prolonged timeline. The contrast is between the conditions of rational representation and the divine Reality, not between two measurable durations.

### Argument two — ignorance of one's own self (p. 835)

**Translation:** Second: The human being is incapable of knowing his very self—the self to which he refers by saying “I.” God says: “They ask you about the spirit. Say: the spirit belongs to my Lord's command, and you have been given but little knowledge” [Q 17:85]. If this is so concerning what is most evident to him, how much more so concerning what is most removed from the connections of intellects and the bonds of imaginations? By way of example, intellect is like a candle. The nearer something lies to a candle, the more it is illuminated, and the thing nearest to anything is that thing itself. If the intellect's light is so weak that it cannot see its own reality illuminated through itself, how could the Presence of Majesty, infinitely beyond it, become illuminated through that light? Therefore our perfected master Zayn al-Dīn al-Khawāfī said, in effect: whatever a person sees through the eye of insight, or to which his knowledge becomes connected, the Real, exalted is He, surpasses it.

[The edition then cites a brief Arabic verse and two Persian couplets illustrating human incapacity to know the Creator when one cannot fully know oneself. They have not been critically translated here; they are excluded from the claim of complete translation of the poetic material.]

**Analytical note:** The “I” is itself a determinate expression of subjectivity. The demonstration is an argument from the failure of the knower to exhaust his own self, rather than an assertion that all self-knowledge is impossible.

### Argument three — the lamp before the sun (p. 836)

**Translation:** Third: It is established in the true sciences that He, glorified and exalted, is absolute Light and the Light of lights. Just as a lamp placed before the disc of the sun would have no visible effect, the light of intellect, in the presence of the light of God's Majesty, fades away, perishes, and vanishes. Hence they have said that intellect moves through created things. Should it reach the Presence of the Originator of things, its condition is like a drop which falls into the sea and is effaced from distinctness there. There is no relation between the finite and the infinite; the drop and the sea are finite, whereas here the relation is that of the finite to the Infinite. Intellect's light is therefore meagre and limited, while the light of divine Majesty is beyond every measure. How then can the intellect's light remain as a separate light in the presence of the light of His Majesty and the dawning of His splendour?

**Analytical note:** The drop metaphor is insufficient if read quantitatively, as the author himself signals: the divine limitlessness is not merely a sea larger than the human drop.

### Argument four — the sun is both manifest and concealed (p. 836)

**Translation:** Fourth: The relation of the intellect's light to the source of spiritual lights is like the relation of the light of sight to the brightest of corporeal lights. Just as the sun is, in one respect, the most evident of things to sight, and, in another respect, hidden from it, so the Light of lights is, in one respect, the most evident of things to the eye of insight, and, in another, hidden from it. This is confirmed by [the divine names] “He is the Manifest, He is the Hidden.”

**Analytical note:** “Manifest” and “Hidden” are not mutually exclusive predicates *under the same respect*. The example concerns differing operations and limits of vision.

### Argument five — cognition under time and place (p. 836)

**Arabic anchor:**

> العقل لا يتصرف إلا فيما يكون في زمان ومكان

**Translation:** Fifth: Intellect operates only upon what is situated in time and place, because whatever it apprehends, it apprehends as something either past, future, or present, and all of this falls under time. Whatever it conceives, it conceives either here or there, and all of this falls under place. But the Real is different from that. Your object of knowledge in this sort of knowing amounts to negating whatever is other than the Real from Him, and negating what is other than the Real is not itself identical with finding [*wijdān*] the Real.

**Analytical note:** This is the exact distinction of **apophatic judgement** from **direct realization**. Merely negating spatial and temporal predicates cannot be equated with unconditioned disclosure. The author's argument is about the specific operations of discursive intellect he is criticizing, not a demonstrated thesis that every kind of intellect is bound to physical space and time.

### Argument six — comparison presupposes comprehension (p. 836)

**Translation:** Sixth: People commonly say that only someone more learned than both an accomplished scholar and another distinguished expert can discriminate between them. This is because he must comprehend the extent of their knowledge and their respective merits in order to judge whether they are equal or unequal and which has greater excellence. How, then, could deficient human intellects encompass the perfection of God's Majesty and the attributes of His Beauty?

**Analytical note:** This challenges the *criterion of exhaustive judgement*: to measure fully the perfection of a subject presupposes a cognitive comprehensiveness greater than the subject measured. It does not deny every sound assertion about divine attributes.

### Argument seven — the intellect's ultimate recognition of its limits (p. 836)

**Translation:** Seventh: Apprehension ordinarily occurs through intellect, but intellect does not apprehend except something made determinate like itself. It cannot, by its own resources, reach unification [*tawḥīd*], for intellect establishes the distinguishing forms, whereas unification in the sense under discussion occurs only through their effacement, the absorption of multiplicity into the source of unity, and the disappearance of origination in the beginningless. God says: “Everything is perishing except His Face” [Q 28:88]. This is something that intellect cannot accomplish by itself.

Yet if God enables the servant to recognise the intellect's inability to comprehend, and he gives up the hope of reaching the goal by thought alone, and recognises the majesty of the Real beyond the intellect's capacity or its reaching the most holy Presence, then he acknowledges his incapacity and confesses his ignorance. This is the end-point of knowing Him under that consideration. Intellect cannot recognise the details, subtleties, and realities that it does not encompass as though it had grasped them; but it can know that it does not encompass them. Knowledge that one does not know a certain matter is itself a kind of knowledge. Likewise, knowing that one's knowledge cannot encompass what lies beyond it is genuine knowledge. As Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq is reported to have said: “Inability to grasp the grasping is itself a grasping.”

**Analytical note:** This is not a licence to equate the confession of ignorance with the plenitude of unconditioned realization. The first is still a cognitive acknowledgement; the latter, if real, would require a different source and mode of knowledge. This is exactly the distinction the author develops across arguments five to seven.

**Extent of translation above:** The seven numbered arguments of the second chapter are translated consecutively from printed **pp. 835–836**. Extraneous cited poems and the modern editor's notes are excluded, and this exclusion is explicit rather than counted as translated material.

# II. Supplementary primary passage: chapter IV, two senses of “knowing God” (p. 839)

**Heading:**

> الفصل الرابع في تحقيق ما روي عن سراج الأمة الإمام الأعظم أبي حنيفة رضي الله عنه

**Translation:** Chapter Four: Establishing the meaning of what has been reported from the Lamp of the Community, the Greatest Imam, Abū Ḥanīfa—may God be pleased with him.

### 1. Why an apparent affirmation does not contradict the denial of comprehension

**Translation:** It is related that [Abū Ḥanīfa] said: “We have not worshipped You as You deserve to be worshipped, but we have known You as You deserve to be known.” Know that by the right of knowing he intended the knowledge prescribed by revelation and imposed by God upon His servants. In the legal perspective this stands in place of the completion of true knowledge, as is indicated by his expression in *al-Fiqh al-Akbar*. There is, then, nothing in this transmitted statement that contradicts the purpose of our argument, for by complete knowledge we mean insight into the ultimate reality [*kunh*] of the divine Essence exactly as it is.

A further possibility is that the Imam intended his awareness of the subtle and exceedingly fine aspects of the divine affair, whose obscurity, concealment, transcendence of delimitation, and resistance to enumeration exclude encompassing or exact determination. In this respect the proper recognition consists in refraining from attempting to survey and possess it, and in acknowledging one's incapacity for comprehension. Knowledge of the non-encompassment of what cannot be encompassed is genuine knowledge. As al-Ṣiddīq said: “The inability to grasp the grasping is itself a grasping.”

**Notes:** The different uses of “true knowledge” are set apart: (i) knowledge sufficient for the obligations of religion; (ii) knowledge of the *kunh* of the absolute Essence; (iii) authentic recognition of the limit of objectifying cognition. The text does not supply a source-critical demonstration for the attribution of the first saying to Abū Ḥanīfa.

### 2. Absolute nondetermination is not an object of another, higher, determination

**Arabic anchors:**

> غيب الهوية والإطلاق والأزلية المندرجة فيه حكم الأبدية
>
> لا يشهد ولا يفهم ولا يعلم ولا يدرك من حيث التعيّن أصلاً
>
> ولا يتقيّد بقيد بل هو منزّه عن التقيّد واللّاتقيّد

**Translation:** Know that this station requires some extension of the discourse, even if its explanation can ultimately be nothing but allusion. It is the Presence of the Most Holy Essence, the unseen of identity, unrestrictedness, and pre-eternity—within whose scope the judgement of endlessness is included. Insofar as we speak of determination, it is not witnessed, conceived, known, or apprehended at all. It does not fall under the ruling of any determination or become restricted by any qualification. Rather, it transcends both restriction and nonrestriction, for it is the reality of *wujūd* insofar as it is *wujūd*. Apart from it there is only absolute nonexistence, not established in the intellect or in the external world. 

If, however, the most complete apprehensions, knowledges, witnessings, and understandings reach their limit, and confession of incapacity and deficiency becomes manifest—if a person recognises that beyond the limit of his apprehension and the endpoint of his witnessing there are oceans in whose currents every diver and every observer is bewildered—this itself reveals an important limit of the station. To comprehend the ultimate reality of that which exceeds the full range of all comprehension cannot become an ordinary object of cognitive possession. [The paragraph continues with descriptions of the innumerable stations and the impossibility of mastering them by human powers; the English here renders the metaphysical core rather than claiming to reproduce every clause.]

**Notes:** This is perhaps the strongest passage for our comparative thesis. **Even “nonrestriction” is denied as an opposing qualifier** when the subject is the absolutely nondetermined Essence. The text's negative discourse is not a positive classification of the Essence as an additional being situated beyond a series of lower beings.

# III. Other newly checked textual points and translations

## III-A. The mirror illustration (p. 837, argument nine)

**English translation of the principal illustration:** A small child looks into a mirror and sees in it a form. He desires the form, comes near to the mirror, and stretches his hand towards it in order to take it. His hand meets the mirror's iron [frame], while the form proves inaccessible. Likewise, intellect looks into its own representation; a divine disclosure occurs, and the intellect supposes that what it perceives through a particular appearance is a complete apprehension of the original. The mistake is to confuse the reflection with the reflected reality. The Real does not thereby come to be enclosed within the mirror or the seeing power.

**Note on scope:** This is a close translation of the author's simile and metaphysical conclusion, not the entire ninth argument including its two Persian poems. It should not be counted as a full page of literal translation.

## III-B. Distinguishing an unmanifested divine reality from its knowable determinations (p. 840)

**English rendering:** The unseen, essential Presence indicated here is the inward aspect of the noble scriptural statement being discussed; it is still beyond determinations that become established in the Preserved Tablet and in the second degree, and beyond sensible and imaginal objects. The author then quotes a lengthy account of the spiritual way that culminates in the effacement of the servant's individual traits and in the divine Reality assuming the effects of the servant's acts. Such language is a description under the standpoint of realization, not a statement that the separate creature has physically become a divine substance.

**Attribution warning:** The long description of progressive stations is attributed within the treatise to earlier Sufi authorities, notably ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Jīlī. It is *not* al-Iznīqī's independently authored systematic list and has not been continuously translated here.

## III-C. The mirror, the Absolute, and the retained truth of knowledge (p. 842)

**Arabic anchors:**

> والحق لما صدق على كل واحد من الحق والخلق أنه مظهر وظاهر وغيب وشهادة اختلط الأمر واشتبه على الناظر
>
> فاقتضى في بعض المشاهد والمشارب الحيرة والإيمان، فأقرّ صاحبه بالعجز واعترف بالجهل

**Translation:** Since it can be truly said, in different respects, of both the Real and creation that there is a manifesting aspect, a manifested aspect, an unseen and a witnessed aspect, the matter becomes confused for the observer. The reality of witnessing becomes concealed and the perception of unveiling becomes difficult, and the possibility of encompassing and mastering the affair ceases. Under some stations of witnessing and forms of spiritual tasting, this demands bewilderment and faith: the one concerned acknowledges incapacity and confesses ignorance. This itself is a true knowledge, for knowing what one does not know is knowledge that one does not know it. But ignorance of what one should know through the proper modality of knowledge remains ignorance.

**Attribution caution:** This passage is part of an extended discussion invoking older Akbarī formulations, including al-Jandī's commentary. It must not all be treated as al-Iznīqī's original conceptual vocabulary.

## III-D. The most comprehensive witness and language (pp. 842–843)

**English rendering:** The complete knower is not restricted to a special form of self-disclosure. In the all-comprehensive station the diverse realities and forms of disclosure are gathered, without a single one of them cancelling the others. The apparent opposition between outward and inward, or unity and multiplicity, may be transcended when the knower does not absolutize any specific way the Real appears. The text speaks of the supreme human reality as comprehensive both outwardly and inwardly, connecting the prophetic manifestation with the inward reality of sainthood.

**Note:** The large passage is an embedded discussion invoking al-Jandī and Ibn ʿArabī; the above is analytical rather than a claimed continuous line-for-line translation.

## III-E. A key warning: descriptions of the Essence belong to human discourse (p. 844)

**Translation of opening principle:** The Real is not unified by someone who performs an act of unifying Him; if an individual establishes the act and attributes it to himself as an accomplishment, he has already established a relation to something other than the pure oneness he purports to describe. True *tawḥīd*, viewed from the absolute standpoint, removes claims of independent attribution to the unifier, the act, and the forms that follow from them.

**Note:** This paragraph is part of a chain of earlier mystical sayings explicitly cited by al-Iznīqī. It is recorded as **cited teaching in his treatise**, not as a novel doctrine invented by him.



## III-F. A substantial continuous passage: true unity beyond the act of unifying (p. 844)

**Context:** Al-Iznīqī quotes a three-line Sufi poem on the inadmissibility of claiming to unify the One by an independent act of one's own, and then supplies the following extended interpretation. The poem is *reported speech*; the interpretative prose is the relevant material in the present treatise.

**Arabic opening of the quoted poem:**

> ما وحّد الواحدَ من واحدٍ  / إذ كلُّ من وحّده جاحدُ
>
> توحيدُ من ينطقُ عن نعتِه / عاريةٌ أبطلها الواحدُ
>
> توحيدُه إياه توحيدُه / ونعتُ من ينعته لاحدُ

**Fresh English translation of the poem:**

“No one has made the One one through his own unifying, for whoever claims to unify Him [as an act of his own] denies [what he claims]. The unification uttered by one who speaks in description of Him is a borrowed thing, rendered void by the One. His unifying Himself is His unifying; the description of one who describes Him deviates [from that truth].”

**Continuous translation of al-Iznīqī's explanation following the poem:** By this he means that nobody has unified the Real, exalted is He, through the Real's essential unification, for whoever unifies Him [as an act attributed to himself] has thereby established his own act and has drawn a delimiting representation of Him through his act of unifying. Thus he has denied [the very truth he is trying to affirm], by establishing what is other than the Real. There is no unification, in the sense here under discussion, except through the vanishing of all forms and traces. Hence the unification expressed by someone speaking through his descriptions is a borrowed thing, because in the Presence of One-Onliness there is neither qualification, speech, nor a delimiting representation of a thing. These would demand the establishment of a distinguishable form. Whatever seems to possess a fragrance of existence is borrowed from the Real by what is other than Him: let it be returned to its Owner, so that unification can be rightly affirmed and the Real remains One and Alone. Therefore the One who is Real invalidates that borrowed unification in which the form of another still remains; it is invalid in the Presence of One-Onliness.

“His unifying Himself is His unifying” means that **the Real's unification of His own Essence through His Essence is true unification**. In their technical terminology, true unification is the one-onliness of the station of gathering and differentiation. It is the witnessing of unity in multiplicity and multiplicity in unity, with the disappearance of multiplicities in the one Entity; and the witnessing of the Reality in nondelimitation and delimitation, a witnessing free from both restrictions. Such a knower sees the Real [under the two considerations of] the restricted and the unrestricted: neither the consideration of His being restricted exhausts His unrestrictedness nor does the consideration of His unrestrictedness exclude His restriction in the sense intended here. Thus nothing lies outside His encompassing reality. It is this station to which the foremost of the people [of this path] referred [... the author then cites further Sufi sayings and verses].

**Strict translation caveat:** The final sentence of the printed Arabic is grammatically dense, and the phrase describing the restricted and unrestricted should be collated against the two manuscripts used in the 2023 edition before publication as a definitive text. Bracketed interpolations above make the interpretation transparent; the English does **not** claim a stronger ontological identification of Creator and creature *under every respect*.

**Why this passage matters:** This is a full, continuous movement from a **first-person speech act** (“I unify God”), through its *apophatic cancellation*, to a **higher comprehensive predication** (unity in multiplicity, multiplicity in unity). It explicitly distinguishes a lower register where human unification constitutes a paradoxical attribution of another agent, from a comprehensive register where unity and plurality are predicated together under distinct considerations. That is relevant to both Qūnawī's lightning-like disclosures and Guénon's distinction between conditioned realization and the unconditioned Principle. The passage does not itself claim a one-breath episode.

## III-G. The author's conclusion: do not confuse religious assent with exhaustive knowledge (p. 850)

**Heading:**

> خاتمة — الباب الأول: في بيان معنى التخطئة وكشف ما هو الصواب

**Translation:** Conclusion. First section: Explaining the meaning of declaring [another opinion] mistaken and clarifying the correct view.

**Continuous translation of the first substantive paragraph on p. 850 (ending where the printed page breaks):** Know that this argument rests on the claim that faith does not increase or diminish according to the Lamp of the Community, Abū Ḥanīfa—may God be pleased with him. For what is required is certainty, which does not admit of degrees, since difference in such certainty would arise through admitting the possibility of the contradictory, and that is entirely opposed to certainty. Accordingly, to take “We have not known You as You deserve to be known” as meaning “our knowledge has not reached firm and certain assent” is mistaken. I say: by knowledge here we do not mean assent to the existence and oneness of the Real; rather, in the terminology of the Sufis, the intended meaning is insight into, and knowledge of, the ultimate reality of the divine Essence, His names, attributes, and acts, through direct witnessing, not merely through theoretical certainty—just as was explained in the first inquiry of the introduction when the conventions of the technical terms were discussed. The terminology adopted by a group must be sought from that group itself when arguing with them. Yet, even if it is granted that what is intended is assent, this too is susceptible of increase and decrease according to the Greatest Imam, through the multiplication of the things to which assent is attached: in the Prophet's time, because one duty was revealed after another, and after his time through greater acquaintance with the details of the obligations and through consideration of— **[the sentence continues on p. 851 and is not counted here as complete].**

**Critical notes:** This passage confirms al-Iznīqī's two levels of the dispute: (i) the *semantic* error of importing an opponent's definition of *maʿrifa*, and (ii) the *theological* distinction between certainty considered in its formal strength and the indefinite enlargement of the subject-matter to which certainty applies. It also demonstrates why a translation that merges *ʿilm*, *maʿrifa*, *taṣdīq* and *shuhūd* would conceal the argument's actual structure.

## Relationship to the Qūnawī ‘lightning’ argument

The present direct translation supplies a necessary *negative* constraint: if the Absolute cannot be delimited even by the predicate “non-delimited,” then describing a disclosure as “one breath” cannot automatically predicate brevity of the Absolute. That distinction is licensed by these passages.

It does **not** supply a *positive* proof that the same self-disclosure is perpetual in a supra-individual state. That would require a primary source explicitly discussing the mapping between experiential duration and multiple levels of being. Qūnawī's own argument from *jamʿiyya* remains an additional and more specific claim.

## 2. Selected primary-text translation: al-Iznīqī

**Editorial convention:** A brief quotation in Arabic is given where needed to secure the technical term. Elsewhere, the content below is a **selective English analytical rendering** of identified medieval arguments; it is not a continuous literal translation of all text on that page. Quotes attributed by al-Iznīqī to earlier authorities are identified as quotations *within* his work, not as original claims of his.

### A. Pp. 831–833: *ʿilm* and *maʿrifa* are not stable synonyms in specialist discourse

**Textual locus:** Authorial introduction, first inquiry, printed pp. 831–833.

**English rendering:** In ordinary usage knowledge and recognition can be used interchangeably. Among the Sufis, however, their technical meanings may diverge. One convention makes knowledge the general category and recognition the apprehension of what is known in a specific manner; another distinguishes recognition arising after ignorance from knowledge not preceded by it. One therefore cannot decide whether someone has affirmed or denied the knowledge of God until the particular meaning of *maʿrifa* in that sentence has been recovered.

**Research point:** The author is already separating what we might call the *semantic register* from the *epistemological register*. A disputed sentence need not have the meaning assigned to it by an opponent who imports terminology from another discipline.

### B. Pp. 833–836: insufficiency of formal cognition does not mean absence of divine Reality

**Textual locus:** The first two chapters of the argumentative body, pp. 833–836.

**English rendering:** The limits of intellect arise from its ways of apprehending and bounding objects; they do not impose deficiency on the Real. Examples drawn from sight and light explain how exceeding brilliance can prevent apprehension just as much as insufficient light can. The same metaphor cannot be transferred literally to God; it illustrates the difference between a cognitive power and what it attempts to apprehend.

**Research point:** The negative proposition “not encompassed by intellect” must not be turned into the positive ontological proposition “the Real is absent or nonexistent.”

### C. P. 836: time and place as conditions of ordinary intellect

**Arabic, brief verified excerpt:**

> العقل لا يتصرف إلا فيما يكون في زمان ومكان

**English translation:** “Intellect operates only in what is situated in time and place.”

**Contextual qualification:** Al-Iznīqī is contrasting ordinary intellectual operations with the Real, not proving that every possible intellect or form of cognition is temporally conditioned. The claim concerns the limited intellect under discussion.

**Relevance:** A mystical disclosure dated “one breath” may be temporally conditioned as *apprehended* without implying that the Essence itself has a one-breath duration.

### D. P. 837: a child before a mirror

**Textual locus:** Ninth argument and following observation, printed p. 837.

**English rendering:** A child sees an image in a mirror and moves to grasp it, but meets the mirror instead. Likewise, a knower may mistake a representation corresponding to his own conceptual constitution for the reality he seeks. The reflected appearance is not the independent original, and grasping the reflection is not possessing what is reflected.

**Relevance:** This is an explicit warning against illegitimately transferring the attributes of a phenomenal form to its metaphysical source. The metaphor is particularly apt for distinguishing an event of witnessing from the Absolute in itself.

### E. Pp. 838–839: excess of light, and the object of knowledge

**Textual locus:** Third and fourth arguments on intellectual insufficiency; pp. 838–839.

**English rendering:** A cause of failure in comprehension may be the extraordinary intensity of that which discloses rather than its concealment. An apparent affirmation that God is known must be unpacked: knowing the Real through intelligible or revealed determinations is not the same operation as encompassing the absolute Essence without any determination. Confusing these yields a spurious contradiction between affirming divine knowledge and denying exhaustive comprehension.

**Relevance:** The predicate “known” is valid under a definite *ḥaythiyya* while its unlimited application to the Essence is rejected.

### F. Pp. 840–841: the unseen of divine Identity and the successive spiritual journey

**Textual locus:** The discussion of a particularly inward unseen and the long cited description of spiritual stations; pp. 840–841.

**English rendering:** The author refers to the inward unseen of divine Identity as beyond the determinations commonly represented by the Preserved Tablet and the second intelligible degree. He then cites a progressive account of spiritual discipline and inner purification whose successive states culminate in the disappearance of ordinary attributes and a different mode of divine apprehension.

**Attribution caveat:** A substantial portion of the successive stages is explicitly *quoted* from another Sufi authority. It must not be treated as al-Iznīqī's independent list of stations. Nor does the reference to unarticulated unseen matters establish the introduction of new knowledge into the divine Essence.

**Relevance:** The source distinguishes the descending classification of intelligible degrees from the ascending transformations of the knower. These orders should not be collapsed into a single chronological ladder.

### G. Pp. 841–843: genuine essential self-disclosure and limits of the mirror

**Textual locus:** Extended mirror passage and transition to the mystical explanations of unrestricted unity; pp. 841–843.

**English rendering:** Even when what appears in a mirror is genuinely derived from a source, the appearance is not identical with the source in every respect. The person to whom divine disclosure occurs cannot infer that the entire, unqualified Essence has become present as a bounded form. A complete realization must therefore distinguish an authentic divine relation from the limitations of the locus in which it is received.

**Important distinction:** Al-Iznīqī incorporates language attributed to Ibn ʿArabī. The writer's agreement with the cited account does not authorize treating every clause as his original independent doctrine.

### H. Pp. 843–844: essential unicity is not one more predicable divine attribute

**Textual locus:** Final discussion of essential identity followed by the account of the highest *tawḥīd*, pp. 843–844.

**English rendering:** The highest claim about unity is not the assertion of one finite entity against another. Naming, attributing, and even describing a speaker as one who unifies can reintroduce the distinction that the utterance seeks to transcend. The discussion of authentic unicity is therefore deliberately apophatic, without suggesting that the Real becomes nonexistent.

**Relevance:** This identifies an *edge of predicability*: the words “unity,” “knowledge,” and “union” are permissible in a qualified register but cannot constitute an exhaustive description of the absolute Essence.

### I. Pp. 847–849: claims of annihilation and the continuing status of revealed law

**Textual locus:** Warnings about apparently antinomian statements and the senses of *fanāʾ* and *baqāʾ*; pp. 847–849.

**English rendering:** The negation of personal independence in a powerful disclosure is not a warrant to claim exemption from religious obligations. The language of annihilation, union, and enduring with God must be understood as expressing changes of the servant's spiritual state and self-understanding, not as obliterating every valid differentiation between divine Reality and created responsibility.

**Relevance:** This supplies a nontrivial *normative register* in addition to ontological and experiential registers. Even an ultimate metaphysical affirmation does not allow its predicates to be imported directly into law or ordinary conduct.

### J. P. 850: resolution through the senses of “knowledge”

**Textual locus:** Conclusion, first section, p. 850.

**English rendering:** Al-Iznīqī distinguishes certainty sufficient for assent to God's existence and unity from the further and very different question of an encompassing direct knowledge of the Essence. A denial of complete experiential comprehension need not amount to denial of certain religious knowledge. Apparent conflict between theological statements may arise because the word *maʿrifa* is being used in different senses.

**Relevance:** A particularly clear case of two apparently contradictory predications becoming compatible once their cognitive object and mode of knowledge are stated.

## 3. Al-Khūyī: conclusions supported by an actual manuscript study, not by direct access to all leaves

The 2014 study by M. Necmeddin Bardakçı includes manuscript shelfmarks and detailed references to Pīr Muḥammad b. Quṭb al-Dīn al-Khūyī's commentary. The following findings are **English analytical renditions of that study's Turkish manuscript reports**, NOT an original Arabic translation of al-Khūyī.

### Khūyī-01 — The order of existentiation as unveiling (Ali Emiri 980, fol. 2a)

Bardakçı quotes a substantial passage from the commentary in which creation is described as transition from summary to differentiation, hiddenness to appearance, and unseen to witnessed reality. Al-Khūyī compares this to the transition from first to second determination and from essential to name-related self-disclosure. The word “transition” must be read as describing how differentiated effects are explicated; it cannot, without further evidence, be converted into an assertion of temporal change within the Essence.

**Unresolved:** What exact Arabic terms does al-Khūyī use to distinguish conceptual ordering (*tartīb*) and actual emanation (*fayḍ*, *ījād*)?

### Khūyī-02 — Threefold account of manifestation (fol. 2a)

The reported analogy of a syllogism coordinates receptivity, the influence of divine love, and the effective granting of what the recipient's preparedness demands. This is potentially a causal grammar of divine manifestation. A complete Arabic transcription is still required before mapping its three terms onto logical premises or ontological constituents.

### Khūyī-03 — The Book of wujūd, the unseen book, and the Qurʾān (fol. 6a)

Bardakçı reports that al-Khūyī sees the concrete book of existence as a reflection of a divine unseen book, the Qurʾān as a corresponding comprehensive articulation, and the Fātiḥa as summarizing the Qurʾānic order. This offers a promising three-register analogy: intelligible principles, cosmic disclosure, and revealed discourse. The analogy must not be treated as implying identical semantics across all three registers.

### Khūyī-04 — Identity and quiddity (fol. 29b)

Al-Khūyī appears to disagree with Qūnawī over the conceptual separation of *huwiyya* and *māhiyya*; the study reports that he characterizes *huwiyya* as an *iʿtibārī* relation rather than a separately subsistent reality. This needs original-text scrutiny; the precise assertion attributed to Qūnawī is not fully recoverable from the secondary summary alone.

### Khūyī-05 — Seven degrees of wujūd (fol. 35a)

The manuscript study describes a sevenfold exposition: the absolute Essence, the first determination, the effusion of possibles, relations among universal Presences, spirits, the First Intellect as cosmic principle, and the celestial order. The First Intellect apparently appears in more than one place: the same named principle may be discussed under different *iʿtibārāt*. Do not reify these into seven independent existents before checking the Arabic.

### Khūyī-06 — Priority reading locations

- Ayasofya 4805, fols. 1a–13b: his separate *Risālat al-asʾila wa-l-ajwiba* (22 questions/answers), **not retrieved here**.
- Fols. 14a–21b: another treatise on faith and spiritual excellence; **not retrieved**.
- *Zubdat al-taḥqīq* opening through fol. 26a: metaphysical introduction; **not retrieved**.
- Fol. 29b and 35a: *huwiyya/māhiyya* and degrees of existence; **not retrieved**.
- Fol. 95a and 98b–106a: unseen, spiritual disclosure, and comprehensiveness; **not retrieved**.

**Important caveat:** The 2014 study directly checked *Ali Emiri 980* and found Pīr Muḥammad al-Khūyī named in the manuscript despite the library catalogue's competing al-Iznīqī attribution. Kātib Çelebi separately lists an al-Iznīqī commentary and one by al-Khūyī. Therefore neither “same work” nor “two distinct extant Zubdat texts” has yet been proven manuscript-critically.

## 5. Provisional comparative thesis

These materials already support the usefulness of a disciplined six-field interpretive apparatus:

| Field | Question | Exemplary passage |
|---|---|---|
| **Reference** | What reality does the term pick out? | Iznīqī pp. 831–833 on *ʿilm/maʿrifa* |
| **Ontological condition** | Is it the Essence, first determination, names, fixed entity, or created form? | Khūyī fol. 35a (secondary witness) |
| **Noetic condition** | Is knowledge rational, scriptural, unveiled, or comprehensive? | Iznīqī pp. 835–839 |
| **Experiential condition** | Which *ḥāl*, *maqām*, or degree of preparedness governs the recipient? | Iznīqī pp. 840–844 |
| **Linguistic condition** | Is the predication unqualified, analogical, negative, technical, or relative? | Iznīqī p. 850 |
| **Normative condition** | Is a metaphysical claim being wrongly transferred into the ethics/law of the servant? | Iznīqī pp. 847–849 |

**Consequential inference:** A temporal disclosure and a timeless Essence are not direct competitors for the same predicate. The predicate “lasts one breath” can apply to a specific reception, while not applying to the Essential Reality under the consideration of absolute nondelimitation. Al-Iznīqī provides conceptual support for that distinction; none of the current materials proves that he explicitly interprets Qūnawī's particular lightning passage in these terms.
