### English

In the name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful. From Him we seek help.

Praise belongs to God, in the seas of whose knowledge the intellects of the intelligent have drowned; in the wilderness of whose greatness and awe the hearts of the spiritual chiefs have become bewildered; in the courtyard of the assaults of whose Essence the spirits of the substitutes have passed away. The hearts of the elect are powerless to lift the veils of His divinity; the inmost hearts of the sages wander around the sanctuary of His quiddity; the tongues of the eloquent and articulate are silenced before the unveiled beauty of the perfection of His attributes and Names. The promptings of estimation cannot pierce the veils of His beauty, nor the whisperings of reflection traverse the unseen depths of His majesty. He is veiled from the eyes of sight by the light of His splendour, and withdraws from the thoughts of contemplation through the might of His majesty. He is exalted above determination by symbols and indications, and beyond being encompassed by imaginings, understandings, or passing thoughts. Blessings be upon His chosen Beloved and selected confidant—the voyager through the deep of “he was two bow-lengths away or nearer,” and the swimmer along the way of “to your Lord is the utmost limit” and “to your Lord is the return”—and upon his family, lamps in the darkness, and his companions, stars of guidance and missiles against the foes.

Now, the occasion for dictating and recording this treatise, and the reason for writing out and composing these pages, is that among the litanies of the great trustworthy masters and those who receive acceptance and regard occurs: “Glory be to You! We have not known You as You deserve to be known.” Some people have accused its speaker of error and foolishness, and others of unbelief and deviation. We seek God's refuge from their ugly expression and reprehensible assertion. May God pardon them for their slips and failings, and for the injury and harshness they have shown to the people of the radiant religion. This weak servant who belongs to them, Muḥammad b. Quṭb al-Dīn al-Iznīqī—may God expand his breast with the true light—rolls up his sleeve to defend these people of inward and outward insight against their accusers, also at the request of some of my good brethren. Do not put me to shame, O Bestower of favour, over this perplexing and intractable question. Through Your gentleness open my heart, and by Your care turn my reins toward the clear road of proof. Amen. I have arranged the treatise in an introduction, chapters, and a conclusion.

SUPPLEMENT

Recognition and knowledge have the same meaning in ordinary language. The statement reported at the beginning of the chapters of al-Najāt, ‘Every recognition and knowledge is either conception or assent,’ indicates that they are used synonymously. Sometimes, however, knowledge is used for acts of assent and recognition for conceptions. This accords with the grammarians' observation that ʿalima takes two objects, whereas ʿarafa takes one. Sometimes knowledge is used for composites, whether considered as conceptions or assents, and recognition for apprehending simples, whether by conception or assent. Hence one says ‘I recognized God’ rather than ‘I knew Him’ [using these respective verbs]. Knowledge may also be used for universals—a universal concept or a universal rule—and recognition for particulars. Recognition may denote apprehension after ignorance, or the latter of two apprehensions of a single thing separated by an absence of apprehension. Neither of these qualifications is required in knowledge. Consequently, the Creator, exalted is He, is not described as a recognizer, but is described as knowing. ### Editorial notes

1. Corrections of prior prose: «وحش الله» is “God’s wild creatures,” not “merciful to people”; the Solomon example says that even his kingdom would not distract the recognizer, not that possessing the kingdom fails to confer knowledge.
2. The striking «وعن الله» is preserved, not silently removed. Within the quotation it can concern the passing away of a separate intentional awareness of God, but that explanation is not substituted for the source wording.
3. The quoted definition of recognition as encompassing reality is retained even though later passages deny encompassing divine knowledge. The author’s task of distinguishing senses must not be resolved in advance by changing the quotation.
