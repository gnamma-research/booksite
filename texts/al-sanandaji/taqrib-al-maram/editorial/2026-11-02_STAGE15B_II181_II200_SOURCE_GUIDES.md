# Sanandajī + Wasīm — Stage 15B, II:181–200

Source is the reuploaded complete 632-image `taqribmaram(2).pdf`; **printed II:181–200 = source PDF 476–495**. Each original scan page was directly image-inspected. These are **twenty preliminary page-specific editorial argument guides, not twenty fully translated pages**. All small-print margins remain substantially untranslated, and no marginal passage is newly attributed to Wasīm.

## Exact original chapter divisions

- Printed **II:192**: «فصل في الحسن والقبح» — chapter on goodness and badness, distinguishing descriptive/perfective, purpose-relative and normative senses.
- Printed **II:197**: «فصل في التكليف بما لا يطاق» — chapter on obligation beyond capacity, a consequence of the preceding dispute.

## Page-by-page orientation (English synopsis, NOT continuous translation)

### P2.181 — Eternal knowledge and voluntary acts
Printed II:181; physical scan 476. Selected Arabic anchor: «العلم الأزلي».
Divine foreknowledge is used in an objection about the necessity of acts. An explicit response distinguishes one in whom an action occurs from one who produces it in another subject. Extensive lower commentary tests the consequent responsibility of the servant.
*Complete upper source and lower marginal notes still require verbatim transcription and English.*

### P2.182 — Scriptural acts of the servant
Printed II:182; physical scan 477. Selected Arabic anchor: «كل امرئ بما كسب رهين».
Verses about acquiring acts, commands, prohibitions and disobedience are set against divine creative authorship. The lower argument turns to praise, blame and the limited explanatory force of a created human will.
*Complete upper source and lower marginal notes still require verbatim transcription and English.*

### P2.183 — The disputed scope of acquisition
Printed II:183; physical scan 478. Selected Arabic anchor: «والجواب أن بعضها غير متنازع».
The response concedes that some predications of human action are not contested, but the inference to independent origination is. A long lower gloss examines readings of scripture about divine production of acts and moral disapproval.
*Complete upper source and lower marginal notes still require verbatim transcription and English.*

### P2.184 — Human power, choice and coercion
Printed II:184; physical scan 479. Selected Arabic anchor: «أفعال العباد».
The upper argument distinguishes proximate grounds of human acts from remoter causal dependence. The lower apparatus critiques claims that divine creation necessarily eliminates voluntary agency.
*Complete upper source and lower marginal notes still require verbatim transcription and English.*

### P2.185 — Divine determination of all originated events
Printed II:185; physical scan 480. Selected Arabic anchor: «بل جميع الحوادث».
The shorter upper closing lines affirm divine decree concerning originated events. The much longer lower argument compares the divine creation of acts with the human subject's experienced choice.

### P2.186 — Divine decree and the decreed act
Printed II:186; physical scan 481. Selected Arabic anchor: «الرضا إنما يجب بالقضاء لا بالمقضي».
One objection asks how unbelief could be by divine decree without requiring approval of unbelief. The reply distinguishes God's decreeing from the decreed occurrence, and cites a competing position concerning action following its motive.
*Complete upper source and lower marginal notes still require verbatim transcription and English.*

### P2.187 — Eternal divine power and human proximate causation
Printed II:187; physical scan 482. Selected Arabic anchor: «لا خلاف في قدم القدرة».
The text contrasts divine power and creaturely power and reports Muʿtazilī objections. The smaller commentary asks whether acts can be attributed to human agents through proximate causes without making them independent creators.

### P2.188 — Power, destiny and action
Printed II:188; physical scan 483. Selected Arabic anchor: «القدرة».
Theological uses of destiny, power and willing are tested using reported examples and objections. The lower text debates whether dependence on the divine decree entails that human action is compelled.

### P2.189 — Divine will and voluntary action

The claim that what God wills occurs is discussed alongside the moral status of actions. The lower register challenges inferences from divine will to either compulsion or approval.

Printed II:192; physical scan 487. Selected Arabic anchor: «فصل في الحسن والقبح».
The original chapter distinguishes goodness/badness as perfection/deficiency, conformity to an aim, and entitlement to divine praise or blame and reward or punishment. The debated rationality concerns especially the last, normative sense.

A new printed heading opens the question of obligation to perform the impossible and expressly identifies it as a consequence of the disagreement concerning rational goodness and badness. The gloss examines kinds of impossibility and the force of 'permissible'.

Printed II:200; physical scan 495. Selected Arabic anchor: «القبح العقلي».

## Critical source cautions

**II:186:** The contrast is ‘decree’ (*qaḍāʾ*) versus ‘the decreed act’ (*maqḍī*); approval of God's decree must not be equated with moral approval of unbelief.

**II:192:** Different meanings of ḥusn and qubḥ must not be collapsed. Ashʿarī rejection of reason as an independent source of divine praise/blame does not entail denying rational recognition of descriptive perfection or suitability to a purpose.

**II:193–196:** The extensive lower-register objections invoke truth, falsehood, and an apparent self-reference paradox. Historical author of this marginal discussion is **unverified**, not presumed to be Wasīm.

**II:197–200:** Several distinct senses of impossibility and of what constitutes an intelligible obligation are under debate. The lower-register dialectic is longer than much of the upper running text and cannot be treated as already translated.
