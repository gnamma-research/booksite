### II:201 — whether the divine acts are purposive

**Selected opening and reasoning (original upper text, with source elisions marked):**

> فصل. ذهبت المعتزلة إلى تعليل أفعاله تعالى بالأغراض والأشاعرة إلى عدمه. ... الحق أن تعليل بعض أفعاله ... سيما الأحكام الشرعية كإيجاب الحدود والكفارات وتحريم المسكرات ... بالأغراض، أي الحكم الراجعة إلى العباد، ظاهر ... ثابت بالنص ... والإجماع ... وعليه مبنى حجة القياس.

**Close English:** “Chapter. The Muʿtazilites held that God's acts are explained through purposes, while the Ashʿarites maintained the contrary. … The view presented as correct is that some of His acts admit explanation … especially legal rulings such as prescribing sanctions and expiations and prohibiting intoxicants … through purposes, that is, wise ends returning to God's servants. This is evident … supported by scripture … and consensus … and upon it rests the evidentiary force of analogy (*qiyās*).”

**Source-facing notes:** `بعض` (“some”) is indispensable: the author is not conceding that **every** divine act has a requisite external end. The legal examples are specifically adduced as instances where wise ends are intelligible, not as a proof that God is intrinsically perfected by an effect. The printed discussion directly references Qurʾānic verses about worship, prescriptions for Israel and the marriage of Zayd's former wife (Q 51:56, 5:32 and 33:37) and ends with legal `qiyās`. The original parenthesized lemma spans must remain distinct from intervening explanation.

### II:202 — necessity versus universality of divine purposes

**Separate original phrase-units (not a continuous sentence):**

> فالأقرب جعل الخلاف ... على عدم لزوم ذلك ... أو على عدم عمومه

**Close English:** “The more plausible approach is to construe the disagreement … as denying that this is necessary … or as denying that it applies universally.”

**Logical issue:** `lāzūm` is a thesis of *necessity*, not a mere assertion that particular acts have ends; `ʿumūm` is a thesis about the *scope* of such explanation. A purposive benefit arising from a legal act may be accepted without making an extrinsic good indispensable for every divine act. The upper source introduces objections concerning eternal punishment, and whether the supposed purpose returns to the Creator or to created beneficiaries.

**One short directly selected lower-register gloss, individual author not verified:**

> وخلاف هذا القول هو القول بأن أفعاله تعالى ليست معللة بالأغراض كما عليه الأشاعرة

“The opposing position is the assertion that His acts are not explained through purposes, as maintained by the Ashʿarites.”

This phrase is in the source's **small print**, not a new Sanandajī running sentence; it is NOT a positively authenticated passage from Muḥammad Wasīm.

### II:203 — the formal goodness of obligation

**Selected original source phrases:** `التعريض للمنفعة` — “exposing [the person] to a benefit”; `التمكين من أسباب السعادة الأبدية` — “enabling access to the means of everlasting felicity”; `هي الجهة الحسنة للتكليف` — “this is the respect in which imposition of obligation is good.”

The original discusses whether the morally good-making aspect of an obligation lies in offering the person an opportunity for beneficial action. This is not equivalent to saying the benefit is a need within the divine being. The small-print discussion develops objections from actual reward, culpability and unfulfilled commands; it is not fully transcribed here.

### II:204–208 — guidance versus the creation of guidance

**Original II:204 division:**

> فصل في مباحث الهدى والإضلال والتوفيق ...

“A chapter on questions concerning guidance, leading astray and enabling [a servant].”

**Source-witness phrases:** `نسبة الهداية` — “the attribution of guidance”; `نسبة الإضلال` — “the attribution of leading astray”; `معنى خلق الهداية` — “the meaning of creating guidance”; `معنى خلق الضلال` — “the meaning of creating misguidance.”

**Critical interpretation:** The relevant distinction is not simply whether guidance is “from God” or “from a messenger.” The *mode of predication* matters: (1) indicating the correct way; (2) bringing about the resulting state or power; (3) withholding aid or permitting the contrary; and (4) inducing error. The long marginal objection/reply discussions in II:206–208 contest the transfer from these distinct senses to an unqualified assertion of responsibility for misguidance. They must be translated in their argumentative order.

### II:209–210 — the appointed term of death

**Original title and selected upper lemmata (separate spans):**

> فصل والأجل ... في اللغة الوقت ... والمقتول ميت بأجله

“Chapter. The appointed term (*ajal*) … linguistically, a time … and someone killed dies at his appointed term.”

The text distinguishes (a) the time of death known to God from (b) the killer's act and moral responsibility. The asserted fixedness of the term does not mean the killing is no one's culpable deed. The source also considers reports concerning apparent extension of lifespan, while retaining the theological distinction between an independently altered fixed term and an increase in the blessing of life.

### II:211 — two *different* printed chapters on one original page

**First section:**

> فصل في الرزق ... ما ساقه الله تعالى إلى الحيوان لينتفع به

“A chapter on provision (*rizq*) … that which God conveys to a living creature so that it may benefit from it.”

The source distinguishes actually received provision from mere legal ownership and considers whether nourishment obtained unlawfully may be called divine provision; the position reported for the Muʿtazila must be retained as a separate position rather than normalized into the commentator's own usage.

**Second section, printed near the bottom of the SAME page:**

> فصل. أجمعت الأمة على أن الله تعالى لا يفعل القبيح ولا يترك الواجب

“Chapter. The community agrees that God does not do what is reprehensible nor omit what is obligatory.”

The difficult qualification immediately following is what “reprehensible” and “obligatory” mean when predicated of God. The agreement in **words** does not by itself show that Ashʿarī and Muʿtazilī analyses affirm the same propositions in the same sense. This chapter continues into the controversy over `aṣlaḥ`, reward and punishment on II:212–215.

### II:216–220 — the divine names

**Actual new section at II:216:**

> فصل في أسماء الله تعالى ... فالاسم ... والمسمى ... والتسمية

“A chapter on the names of God … the name … what is named … and naming.”

**Further directly read terms:** `اللفظ المفرد الموضوع للمعنى` — “the simple expression conventionally appointed to signify a meaning”; `الاسم نفس المسمى` — “the name is the named itself.”

The source distinguishes the linguistic sign (a word), its semantic content or denotation, the referent, and the act that institutes a name. These are not uncontroversially interchangeable. II:217–220 works through the consequences of saying “the name is the named,” including common reference under different divine attributes, the status of wording versus object, conventional naming, and which expressions may be used as divine names. The extensive lower comments have not been fully transcribed or assigned to an individual marginal author.
