## Volume I: motion, relation, and the remaining accident categories

### P1.261: Growth, withering, rarefaction and condensation

**Source:** vol. I, printed 261 = PDF image 274; layout: `upper-short-lower-extended`.

**Short Arabic anchor (not full text):** «والذبول وهو عكس النمو».

**English argument guide — NOT a continuous translation:** The exposition completes the taxonomy of motion in quantity. Growth and withering concern a body's increase or decrease according to its nature, whereas rarefaction increases its volume without the addition of another body and condensation decreases it without removing a constituent. The lower register tests whether mere displacement of parts should count as quantitative change rather than only alteration of their spatial arrangement.

## Volume II begins: Book Four, on substance (al-jawāhir)

### P2.002: Opening of Book Four: substances

**Source:** vol. II, printed 2, original PDF image 297.

**Short Arabic anchor:** «الباب الرابع في الجواهر».

**English argument guide — NOT a continuous translation:** Volume II opens the discussion of substance (*jawhar*). The commentator sets out the theologians' distinction between divisible body (*jism*) and indivisible atom (*jawhar fard*) and contrasts it with the philosophers' conception of body as a substance capable of three mutually perpendicular dimensions. These are rival criteria for defining bodily substance; they should not be silently merged.

### P2.003: The Ashʿarī and Muʿtazilī definitions of body

**Source:** vol. II, printed 3, original PDF image 298.

**Short Arabic anchor:** «الجسم عندنا».

**English argument guide — NOT a continuous translation:** The commentary differentiates Ashʿarī usage, on which a body is a substance admitting division, from Muʿtazilī formulations that specify the three dimensions or require more atoms in the composition of a body. An objection turns on whether a two-atom compound deserves the name of body and what is involved in the predicate 'divisible'.

### P2.004: The philosophical definition in terms of three dimensions

**Source:** vol. II, printed 4, original PDF image 299.

**Short Arabic anchor:** «وعند الفلاسفة».

**English argument guide — NOT a continuous translation:** The philosophers' body is a substance in which three mutually perpendicular dimensions can be supposed. The discussion separates *the ability to receive such dimensions* from possession of a particular length, breadth and depth and asks whether the body persists through the alteration of its dimensions. The difference between potential and actually designated dimensions is central.

### P2.005: The natural body and bodies of diverse elemental natures

**Source:** vol. II, printed 5, original PDF image 300.

**Short Arabic anchor:** «الجسم البسيط».

**English argument guide — NOT a continuous translation:** A short main-text passage addresses the philosophers' qualification of the body and the question of various natural bodies with different natures. Extensive marginal notes examine how far a definition by dimensional receptivity settles the issue of matter, form and the identity of a body when its measured dimensions change.

### P2.006: Potential divisions and actually distinct constituents

**Source:** vol. II, printed 6, original PDF image 301.

**Short Arabic anchor:** «الانقسامات الممكنة».

**English argument guide — NOT a continuous translation:** The commentator distinguishes the possibility of a body's division from the claim that its possible parts are already separate in act. The theologians' atomist view and the philosophers' matter–form view disagree over what is actually found in an extended body. A full proof must examine whether a continuum can be considered divided by the intellect without being physically divided.

### P2.007: Is every possible division already actual?

**Source:** vol. II, printed 7, original PDF image 302.

**Short Arabic anchor:** «فإن الانقسامات الممكنة».

**English argument guide — NOT a continuous translation:** A dispute about possible and actual parts is conducted through the criterion of an actual whole and the status of its divisions. If each possible part were actually distinct before being divided, the whole would already possess an unlimited multitude of actual partitions. The counterarguments turn on whether divisibility describes a feature of the whole or a set of already existent constituents.

### P2.008: The indivisible atom and objections from motion

**Source:** vol. II, printed 8, original PDF image 303.

**Short Arabic anchor:** «الجزء الذي لا يتجزأ».

**English argument guide — NOT a continuous translation:** The atomist claim of an indivisible constituent is scrutinized against arguments concerning motion, contact and separation. The original treatment distinguishes the atom's numerical simplicity from the intellectual ability to distinguish its sides or relations. One must not mistake diverse relational descriptions for an actual internal division without argument.

### P2.009: Indivisible bodies, continuity, and infinite divisibility

**Source:** vol. II, printed 9, original PDF image 304.

**Short Arabic anchor:** «الانقسام».

**English argument guide — NOT a continuous translation:** The discussion evaluates the theologians' atom and philosophical continuity through geometrical and physical examples. If an extended body were composed of indivisible atoms, the number and arrangement of those atoms would bear on its dimensions; if division continues without a last part, a proof must explain why this does not require actually infinite parts existing at once.

### P2.010: Competing accounts of natural and mathematical body

**Source:** vol. II, printed 10, original PDF image 305.

**Short Arabic anchor:** «الجسم التعليمي».

**English argument guide — NOT a continuous translation:** The text distinguishes natural body from the quantified or mathematical body considered through dimensions. Arguments concerning a body's divisibility, a line, a surface and volume depend upon what is being treated as existent in nature and what as an abstraction. The extended lower notes dispute whether geometrical divisibility is sufficient to demonstrate a specific physical ontology of matter or atoms.
