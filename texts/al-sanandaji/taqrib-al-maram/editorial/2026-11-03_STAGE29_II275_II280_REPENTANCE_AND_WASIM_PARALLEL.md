# Stage 29: Repentance and the Sanandajī–Wasīm parallel apparatus
## Original *Taqrīb al-Marām*, printed II:275–280 (PDF 570–575)

**Primary visual witness:** the complete 632-image scan supplied in this conversation. Six original printed pages, II:275–280, were rendered individually at high resolution, including the larger upper register and the much longer small-print lower register. The original **title page** explicitly reads `مع حاشية المحاكمات للشيخ محمد وسيم الكردستاني وحواشي متفرقة لبعض الأفاضل`. Thus a lower-register placement establishes only that a text is *marginal*, NOT that its individual author is Wasīm. An independent printed-witness or explicit author marker is required for that.

### A. Actual chapter architecture and scope

- **II:275:** the first part of the upper printed page still concludes the previous debate over major sinners and intercession. A genuine original `فصل في التوبة` begins **partway down the page**, not at its top. Repentance is described linguistically as *returning* and technically as remorse for transgression precisely because it is a transgression.
- **II:275–277:** an objection distinguishes regret arising from a sin's *reprehensibility* from regret arising from damage to one's health, wealth or standing, then asks whether fear of punishment can motivate genuine repentance. The debated extra condition is a future **resolve to refrain**, and the lengthy lower discussion tests how resolve is possible if the sinful possibility never occurs to one, cannot be performed, or one dies first.
- **II:278:** the source reports a distinct formulation associated with the Muʿtazila, including believing that the act was wrongdoing and wishing, if it were possible, to undo it. It contrasts this with an account requiring present grief; it then distinguishes whether repentance is commanded by transmission or required by reason.
- **II:279:** an upper discussion distinguishes whether God is **obliged to accept** repentance and whether repentance itself is obligatory without delay. The separate lower commentator debates the limits of inferential moves from a revealed promise to a rational obligation upon God.
- **II:280:** the text discusses the possibility of repenting of **some** sins while still not repenting of all, the timing of repentance, and the consequences of treating repentance as an instantaneous obligation. The long lower note clarifies that an 'hour' may mean an interval sufficient to repent, not necessarily a clock hour.

### B. Continuous bounded upper source excerpt at II:275 (first pass, NOT complete page)

**Record:** `ST29.UPPER.II275.01`. Printed II:275; physical PDF 570.

**Original Arabic:**
فصل في التوبة هي في اللغة الرجوع يقال تاب إذا رجع وفي الشرع هي الندم عن المعصية لكونها معصية فإن الندم عن المعصية لأضرارها البدنية أو إخلالها بعرضه أو ماله لا يكون توبة

**Close English:**
Chapter: On repentance. In language it means returning; one says that a person repented when he turned back. In religious usage, it means remorse over a sin precisely because it is sinful. Remorse over a sin on account of bodily harm, or because it damages one's reputation or wealth, does not constitute repentance.

**Source separation:** The continuous print above is a MIXED upper register containing an original heading, parenthesized Taftāzānī wording and Sanandajī's explanatory prose. The 24 shorter records below preserve their provisional separate voice tags. It does not include the rest of the upper source or the long lower-register disputation. It has not undergone an independent second diplomatic reading.

### E. Technical issues raised by the newly read arguments

**1. Repentance for the sin qua sin.** The phrase `لِكَوْنِهَا مَعْصِيَةً` is the formal cause proposed in the definition. Regretting an act because it harms reputation or wealth is not automatically regretting it *as disobedience*. The presence or absence of painful emotion does not, without additional premises, settle which formal object makes remorse religiously qualifying.

**2. The additional condition of resolve.** The controversy is not merely whether repentant people should intend to stop committing a sin. It asks whether the resolve is **part of repentance's definition**, what its intentional object is, and whether the object must be currently conceivable and within one's power. The source's response is conditional: resolve on the supposition that the relevant act comes to mind and could be performed. The lower-register excerpt explicitly identifies *two preconditions*: mental presentation and ability.

**3. Grief, counterfactual restitution, and inability.** The reported Muʿtazilī alternative includes acknowledging wrongdoing and wishing one could undo the act, rather than necessarily producing grief at will. A counterfactual disposition to reverse an event is not identical to an attainable present action. That distinction has consequences for people unable to restore the past or for involuntary emotional states.

**4. Commanded repentance versus divine duty to accept it.** The source separates the revealed obligation upon the sinner to repent (e.g. `توبوا إلى الله جميعا`) from the logically independent thesis that God is rationally obliged to accept that repentance. The marginal discussion makes the inference from scriptural wording to rational obligation contentious. This is continuous with the preceding pages on reward, threat, pardon and grace.

**5. Temporal immediacy and its unit.** A strict command to repent immediately raises a question of what *fawr* is: the next possible opportunity to form a valid act, or an arbitrarily exact external clock instant? The small-print interpretation of `السّاعة` as a span sufficient to repent addresses precisely that problem.

**6. Partial repentance.** `وتصح التوبة عن بعض الذنوب خاصة` distinguishes the object of a particular act of repentance from a comprehensive life-history judgement. Validity as to one wrongful act does not automatically settle whether failing to repent of a different sin compromises it; the long source argument has not yet been translated fully.
