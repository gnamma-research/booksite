## I:13 — The subject of kalām and the relation between its end and its intermediate questions

### I013.upper.01 — Predicates of theological questions
**Arabic:** «من الحيثية المذكورة يتناول محمولات مسائله» · «العقائد الدينية» · «المحمولات ...» · «فلا إشكال».

**English:** Under the respect already specified, the expression also embraces the **predicates** in the propositions that constitute its questions. One may therefore say that the religious doctrines intended here are the things predicated within the discipline, and what serves as the means of reaching those doctrines. On that understanding, the objection—that not every matter investigated is itself one of the credal conclusions—does not arise. [The precise intervening formulation after *al-maḥmūlāt* is difficult in this impression; the paraphrase is explicitly provisional.]

**Status:** Source-facing, interpretative at one indistinct phrase; no independent second proof.

### I013.upper.02 — Benefit and ultimate end
**Arabic:** «وفائدته أمور وغاية الكل الفوز بسعادة الدارين» · «وكان المصنف يرى أنه من العلوم التي غايتها أنفسها فاكتفى ببيان ...».

**English:** The discipline has several benefits, while their ultimate end is **attaining felicity in the two abodes**. The commentator suggests that the author regarded this as one of the sciences whose immediate aim is, in one respect, its own knowledge. That helps explain why he was satisfied with specifying the discipline itself rather than treating every benefit or remoter aim as its defining subject.

**Status:** Connected upper-text reading; final word of the last clause requires a high-resolution recheck.

### I013.ruled.01 — Sufficiency of the subject-based distinction
**Arabic:** «المعلوم المذكور لا يعتبر من المحمول» · «المقصود من ذلك تمييز العلم من سائر العلوم باعتبار الموضوع» · «والبيان المذكور كاف فيه» · «اه منه».

**English:** The ‘known’ that was mentioned is not itself treated here as one of the predicates. The purpose is to distinguish this science from other sciences **by reference to its subject**, and the explanation given is sufficient for that. Allowing the discussion also to range over predicates does not destroy the proposed distinction. [The closing «منه» denotes a source or speaker, but does not independently identify a named glossator.]

**Status:** Separately ruled text; source-located reading.

### I013.lower.01 — Is the 'known' an adequate general subject?
**Arabic:** «من الذهني وغيره يساوي الموجود المعدوم في العموم» · «إنما يحتاج إليه عند من يجعل مباحث المعدوم والمحال من مسائل علم الكلام» · «أن المراد بالعقائد الدينية المحمولات».

**English:** The marginal writer begins by challenging how the ‘known’ is supposed to extend to what exists only in thought as well as to what exists outside thought. Making the known object of inquiry fully coextensive with existence and nonexistence may be broader than the argument warrants. The issue becomes pressing especially if one counts discussion of the nonexistent and the impossible among the questions of kalām. The writer therefore asks whether the alleged subject is being construed as the *subject term* of the propositions, their *predicates*, or the whole range of things to which theological demonstration must refer. He reports the suggestion that ‘religious doctrines’ denotes the predicates, but cautions that this may not settle the problem by itself.

**Status:** Lower-gloss source-facing exposition, not a complete literal rendering of every line.

### I013.lower.02 — Indirect propositions and intermediate bases
**Arabic:** «من حيثية التعلق بالمحمول كما يتناول الموضوع» · «ليس هذا الإخراج من ورطة» · «قال المصنف» · «ليس تصوراً لموضوعه».

**English:** The text develops a difficulty in restricting the discipline to its directly professed conclusions. It is not enough simply to say that what enters a proof as a premise is itself a credal conclusion: a science often studies conditions of its own arguments without taking each such condition as its final object. A predicate may be introduced under the respect in which it relates to an ultimate doctrinal result, just as a subject can be. The commentator uses this to question whether the proposed escape from the difficulty is genuine, rather than a shift between two ways of describing the same material.

**Status:** Connected argumentative paraphrase; several compact expressions at the top of the lower stream remain uncertain.

### I013.lower.03 — ‘Ultimate’ and ‘proximate’ ends of the discipline
**Arabic:** «(قوله الفوز بسعادة الدارين)» · «ذكروا أن الفائدة والغاية والمنفعة أمور لا تختلف إلا بالحيثية والاعتبار» · «بالأحكام الشرعية اعتقاداً حقاً» · «لا يؤدي إلى الفساد في الآخرة».

**English:** On “attaining felicity in the two abodes”: benefit, end, and utility are not always three distinct realities; they may describe one result under different **considerations**. The nearer end of kalām is to establish firmly and correctly the doctrines that belong to religion, removing uncertainty and error. Its more remote end is a right faith that prevents misdirection in this life and leads toward deliverance in the next. The marginal writer objects to confusing this final good with the particular cognitive act that defines the science; something may be sought through the science without thereby becoming the science's own formal subject.

**Status:** Lower text partly normalized in English; the precise wording of the final lines joins I:14.

## I:14 — Whether knowledge can be defined and why some knowledge is necessary

### I014.upper.01 — A further end and the inadequacy of a strict definition
**Arabic:** «فيكون هذا كذا غاية الغاية» · «والعلم لا حدّ له بوجهه» · «واختاره الإمام الرازي لوجهين».

**English:** In that case, this is the **end of the end**: a remoter result beyond the more immediate purpose of the science. The discussion then turns to *knowledge*. One position holds that it cannot be defined by a strict essential definition and instead must be made known in another way. The commentator reports that Imām Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī preferred this view and adduces two arguments for it.

**Status:** Upper text, first-pass connected translation.

### I014.upper.02 — Two arguments for immediacy
**Arabic:** «الأول أن علم كل أحد بأنه موجود ضروري» · «وهذا أعم خاص والمطلق جزء منه» · «والثاني أن غير العلم يعلم بالعلم فلو علم العلم بغير العلم لزم الدور».

**English:** First, everyone’s knowledge that **he himself exists** is necessary or immediate; this is a determinate case of knowing, and knowledge considered without that determination is more general. It is argued that, if the particular is immediate, then the more general conception should be no less so. Second, other things are known **by means of knowledge**; if knowledge itself could be known only by way of a further act of knowledge, a vicious circle or regress appears to threaten. The text specifies that the second objection bears against one who says that knowledge is known but **not** necessarily or immediately.

**Status:** Upper text close reading. The inferential move from particular to general is an **argument being reported**, not endorsed by the editor.

### I014.lower.01 — The two possible senses of an end
**Arabic:** «العلم غاية لنفسه» · «لا تكون غاية الغاية» · «أن الأمور التي غايتها الفوز لا الفوز نفسه».

**English:** The marginal writer asks what the earlier reference to the end intends. If knowing itself is the immediate aim, a further end such as felicity can only be called the ‘end of the end’ under a particular ordering of aims. Not every benefit produced by a discipline is part of its essence or immediate purpose. The criticism is directed against sliding between the **knowledge sought**, the **good achieved through possessing it**, and the **farther religious outcome** of such possession.

**Status:** Lower-register source-based reading, selected clauses; not all words diplomatically transcribed.

### I014.lower.02 — Does knowledge of one's existence require prior knowledge of knowledge?
**Arabic:** «تصور العلم بديهي» · «لوجهين الأول أنه معلوم» · «اكتساب الماهوية» · «فحكم الوجدان» · «إما ضرورة ... أو اكتساب».

**English:** The margin distinguishes knowing that one exists from possessing a definition of what ‘knowledge’ means. One may immediately have a cognition without being able to analyse its nature. The fact that something is given in awareness is not in itself a proof that its **quiddity**, as an object of reflective analysis, is also immediately apprehended. If one says that the conception of knowledge is acquired, a question remains: what knowledge must be present for the acquisition to take place? The argument is delicate because it can use *knowledge* for the act of apprehension in one premise and for the concept being studied in another.

**Status:** Partly interpretative lower reading; this distinction is explicit in the print, but the full argument is lengthy.

### I014.lower.03 — Circularity, ‘beginning’ and the limits of the proof
**Arabic:** «يلزم الدور» · «بداهته مستلزمة لعدم تجدد» · «مستلزم للمطلوب المذكور» · «معلوم لا بالضرورة بل بالاكتساب».

**English:** The critic tests the regress argument by distinguishing an unqualified prior act of knowing from a reflexive act in which knowledge becomes its own object. If every act of knowing presupposed a previously **defined** notion of knowledge, a circle would indeed appear; but if an initial act can occur without reflective grasp of its nature, the inference requires another premise. Conversely, declaring that all cognition must be obtained through acquisition raises the question of its beginning. The marginal discussion does not treat these alternatives as automatically equivalent.

**Status:** Close source-guided synthesis. The sentence continues onto I:15.

## I:15 — Occurrence of knowledge is not identical to its conception

### I015.upper.01 — Resolving the first argument's equivocation
**Arabic:** «على عدم التفرقة بين تصور العلم وحصوله» · «والضروري حصول علم جزئي بوجوده» · «وهو غير تصوره وغير العلم».

**English:** The objection rests on a **failure to distinguish between conceiving knowledge and its occurrence**. What is necessary here is the occurrence of a particular cognition of one’s own existence. That cognition's occurrence is not the same as the conception of the cognition, still less a universal definition of knowledge. Hence the first argument cannot simply carry the immediacy of one cognitive event over to the concept of knowledge as such.

**Status:** Upper text connected translation, with opening join from I:14.

### I015.upper.02 — The objection from acquired conceptions
**Arabic:** «أما ... حصول علم جزئي ... يتوقف تصوره على تصوره» · «وقيل لا يجدي ...» · «وإليه ذهب الغزالي».

**English:** If the concept of knowledge is acquired, it is argued that some prior grasp of what one is acquiring may be required. Yet that cannot be established merely from the fact that a particular act of knowing has occurred. The discussion reports a further objection to the proposed distinction and associates a position in this dispute with al-Ghazālī. The crucial point is whether what requires a prior conception is **the acquired representation of knowledge**, or every instance of knowledge occurring in a knower.

**Status:** Upper passage partly compressed; attribution and precise final words should be rechecked before citation.

### I015.ruled.01 — Conception of the acquired cognition
**Arabic:** «(قوله وهو غير تصوره)» · «لكن تصوره لازم لحصوله كما في سائر الوجدانيات» · «اه منه».

**English:** On “and it is not its conception”: nevertheless, one might contend that some conception is required for its occurrence, as with other objects of inner experience. Thus the distinction, while real, does not by itself establish that the cognition can occur without any accompanying apprehension of what is taking place. [Closing annotation has no positively established personal author.]

**Status:** Short separately ruled note, provisional source reading.

### I015.lower.01 — Knowledge as an actuality of the knower
**Arabic:** «حصول العلم» · «الضروري ... حصول علم جزئي بوجوده وهو غير تصوره» · «لم يصح وجهها إلا على عدم التحديد فقط».

**English:** The marginal commentator explains that the occurrence of a particular knowledge is not the same as an explicit, reflexive conception of the nature of knowledge. A person can be aware of his existence without arranging a definition of cognition. If one uses such awareness to argue that knowledge resists **essential definition**, that is a more modest conclusion than showing that no explanatory account of knowledge can be given. The second proposition needs a separate proof.

**Status:** Source-facing lower reading, not a literal complete mapping.

### I015.lower.02 — Whether self-consciousness is unavoidable
**Arabic:** «أن حصول العلم العلم لا يعني حصول النفس إلى الذي وحصوله فيها» · «يكون حصوله في النفس تصوراً وحكاية» · «بالدليل الثاني».

**English:** The margin considers whether every occurrence of a cognitive state includes awareness of that occurrence. It distinguishes the presence of the knowing **act** in the soul from a subsequent representation or conception **of that act**. If the two are identified, the claim that self-awareness is necessary may appear plausible; if distinguished, the claim needs qualification. The discussion leads to the second proposed route for making knowledge understood, namely division and example rather than a strict definition.

**Status:** Several tightly printed clauses are insecure, especially the lead-in to I:16; the Arabic anchor is deliberately fragmentary.

## I:16 — Knowledge made known by division and example

### I016.upper.01 — Four divisions of assent
**Arabic:** «بالدليل الثاني وطريق معرفته عنده القسمة والمثال» · «الاعتقاد إما جازم أو غير جازم» · «والجازم إما مطابق للواقع أو غير مطابق» · «والمطابق إما ثابت أو غير ثابت».

**English:** The second route consists in making knowledge known through **division and example**. Under division: an assent is either firm or not firm; a firm assent is either in accordance with reality or not; and an assent in accordance with reality is either stable or unstable. In this way, one distinguishes certain knowledge from mere non-firm opinion, firm error, and even a correct belief that is not settled or secure. The divisions are supplied **to identify** knowledge, not as a strict definition giving its genus and essential differentia.

**Status:** Upper source text; each ternary split visually checked.

### I016.upper.02 — Example and the limits of an essential definition
**Arabic:** «وأما المثال فكأن يقال العلم إدراك البصيرة» · «كاعتقادنا أن الواحد نصف الاثنين» · «لا يحدّ بعبارة جامعة للجنس والفصل الذاتي» · «والإحساس به معروف».

**English:** Under example, one might speak of knowledge as the insight of the inner faculty, or point to our certain conviction that **one is half of two**. The intention of the thinker cited is not that the word can never be explained, but that a definition collecting an essential genus and difference is difficult or impossible in the present case. Many things are recognised perfectly well through experience or example without an analysis that displays their ultimate essence. The example helps a reader identify the intended state even where a genus-and-difference definition cannot be supplied.

**Status:** Upper source-facing reading; punctuation and the exact phrase concerning insight need verification against a second impression.

### I016.ruled.01 — Why an example is not a definition
**Arabic:** «(قوله وأما المثال)» · «يطلق على المشابه وعلى الجزئي» · «فالأول ... والثاني ...».

**English:** On “as for the example”: *mithāl* can refer to something **similar** or to a **particular instance**. The text assigns the two examples their different roles accordingly. An illustrative instance can make a state recognisable without supplying the essential features demanded of a strict definition.

**Status:** Short ruled note, first visual reading; precise ending requires recheck.

### I016.lower.01 — Is the knowledge of a division itself known through knowledge?
**Arabic:** «وحصول العلم به يستلزم إمكان العلم بأنه عالم» · «فيلزم حصول العلم بالعلم الخاص قبل حصول العلم بمطلق العلم» · «مفهوماً مطلق العلم».

**English:** The lower commentator contests the logic of the proposed division. Knowing by an act may permit one to know that one is a knower, but this does not mean the **unrestricted concept of knowledge** must have been defined before the particular act occurred. Conversely, if the mind has to grasp the general category before any example becomes intelligible, the explanatory procedure may tacitly presuppose what it was intended to provide. The point hinges on keeping distinct a concrete cognitive act, reflective awareness of that act, and the general concept of knowledge.

**Status:** Connected reading of a major lower-register objection; unverified compressed subclauses remain.

### I016.lower.02 — The particular and the general
**Arabic:** «حصول العلم بالعلم الخاص» · «حصول العلم بالعلم الغير» · «مطلق العلم» · «ليس فقط».

**English:** The marginal reasoning also asks how an item can be recognised as a **particular kind of knowledge** without prior conception of the general description. The author considers the contrary order as well: one might have a specific cognition and acquire a generic concept through subsequent comparison or reflection. That opposition is not resolved by simply calling either a particular or a general concept ‘necessary’; the alleged priority must itself be clarified.

**Status:** Source-facing exposition, not all lower lines supplied in literal English; the final marginal clause continues to I:17.
