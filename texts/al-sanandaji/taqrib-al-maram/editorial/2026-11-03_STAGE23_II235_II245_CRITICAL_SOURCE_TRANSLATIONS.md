# Taqrīb al-Marām — Stage 23 critical translation dossier
## Printed volume II:235–245, original physical PDF pages 530–540

**Basis:** Original user-uploaded raster volume, visually read and rendered at enlarged resolution. This is a new **source-language / close English selected-clause edition**, correcting the Stage 17 topic map. Pages II:235–239 are not merely a continuous treatment of prophetic ʿiṣma: they move from reports on prophets to angels, comparative rank of humans and angels, and the marvels of saints. II:240 begins the chapter on resurrection. This dossier contains **54 bounded source selections**, **one near-complete individually bounded short gloss** and **three additional selected lower-register fragments**, not eleven complete page translations.

**Cautions:** A parenthesized al-Taftāzānī lemma in Sanandajī's printed large text is not identical with a Sanandajī explanation. A lower note is not automatically by Muḥammad Wasīm. The source sometimes interweaves scriptural citations, reported positions and responses; their voices are distinguished where possible, and none is silently treated as established external fact. All snippets require a second independent diplomatic proof before a definitive edition.

### A. Corrected section architecture

- **II:235:** culmination of debates on prophetic protection and numbers; transition to whether the angels are protected from disobedience.
- **II:236:** the Iblīs objection: the command directed to angels versus the explicit statement that Iblīs was of the jinn; the comparative status of angels and prophets.
- **II:237–238:** conflicting hierarchies of excellence (angels, prophets and other humans), arguments from the demands of obedience amid appetite and anger, Adam's honoured rank and the angels' prostration.
- **II:239–240:** *karāmāt* of God's friends and their difference from a prophetic *muʿjiza*, with no automatic implication of a new prophethood.
- **II:240–245:** the return (*maʿād*), reuniting bodies and souls, the possibility of reinstating what is annihilated, and identity of an entity across temporal interruption.

### D. Scholastic analysis with source limitations

**I. Reported numbers of prophets are not a definitional limit.** Printed II:235 places a report of 124,000 prophets and 313 messengers beside an argument that their total cannot be confined by enumerating a report. The source is recording a transmitted number within a scholastic dispute; this edition does not independently validate the report's transmission. Conflating the number report with a dogmatic bound on prophecy would miss the tension explicitly visible in the page.

**II. Angelic obedience and Iblīs.** The scriptural argument from the angels' not disobeying God's command is tested against the account of Iblīs' disobedience while addressed in the angelic prostration narrative. The rejoinder appeals to the designation of Iblīs as belonging to the jinn. The precise logical issue is whether inclusion in the scope of an address implies membership in the class addressed, and whether a possible exception overturns a general account of angelic nature. Do not assume the source supports a general theory of nonhuman free will beyond its own explicit argument.

**III. Rank of humans and angels.** On II:237–238 an advocate of human prophetic rank points to acts of obedience achieved despite appetite, anger and practical distractions; an opposing argument can point to angelic constant worship. Neither the excellence of a particular act nor its difficulty automatically establishes the rank of the entire species or every individual. The text uses the prostration before Adam and his instruction in names, yet also disputes how much comparative rank these examples prove. Preserve the distinction between **act**, **faculty**, **class of beings**, and **individual**.

**IV. A saintly wonder is not a prophetic miracle by definition alone.** At II:239 the source defines the *walī* through knowledge of God, observance of obedience and avoidance of sin, and describes an extraordinary occurrence lacking a claim to prophethood. The earlier miracle argument made a **challenge** and **absence of successful counter-performance** part of its conditions. Merely sharing an extraordinary appearance is insufficient to make a saint's wonder a proof of a new revelatory mission. The printed source's examples are historical-theological reports, not independently adjudicated empirical claims.

**V. Two distinguishable accounts of the bodily return.** The actual II:240–241 definition admits either (a) a return to existence after annihilation, or (b) the reassembly of previously separated bodily parts, restoration of life, and return of souls to bodies. These are not the same ontology. The difference changes which premise is required: the first depends on the **possibility of reinstating the very nonexistent individual**; the second depends on **continuity and reassembly** of material constituents. The small lower note at II:241 clarifies that the philosophers' soul becomes *separated from the body*, not that the soul suddenly becomes intrinsically immaterial only at death.

**VI. Modal possibility versus mere reassembly.** The argument at II:241 invokes **الإمكان الذاتي لا يزول بحسب الأوقات**: intrinsic possibility is not abolished by time. But persistence of logical possibility is not yet a proof that the exact individuation of a destroyed subject can recur. II:242 connects the Qurʾānic description of the return as easy with an interpretation involving reuniting constituents and restoring their earlier form; that need not amount to literal reinstatement after absolute annihilation. Different premises and objections attach to these two models.

**VII. The problem of numerical identity after interrupted existence.** The II:244 objection asks: if an annihilated thing returns **بعينه أي بجميع مشخصاته**—in its very numerical identity and with all individuating characteristics—what distinguishes first origination (*mabdaʾ*) from restoration (*maʿād*)? The source answers by considering **prior and later occurrence**, and II:245 adds the difference between first occurrence without a preceding existence and the second occurring with an earlier existence behind it. The metaphysical question is whether such temporal priority/posteriority genuinely individuates an occurrence or merely changes its description. The original lower note is lengthy, and this dossier has *not* closed its competing replies or textual variants.

### F. A short CONTINUOUS upper-register source reading at II:242 (independent from the selected fragments)

**Original printed II:242 / original PDF 537.** The entire short **upper** printed text between the page header and first horizontal dividing rule has now been transcribed in a distinct source unit `TQ24.II242.UPPER.01`. The significantly longer **lower marginal commentary on the same page is NOT translated**. An independent second reading of the 38 Arabic words and another primary witness are still pending, so this is **not** a completely critically certified original page.

**Arabic, continuous first-pass printed upper text:**

> المقاصد الأقرب أن تحمل الإعادة التي جعلت أهون على إعادة الأجزاء وما بقيت من المواد إلى ما كان عليه من الصور والتأليفات على ما يشير إليه قوله تعالى قل يحييها الذي أنشأها أول مرة لا على إعادة المعدوم

**Close English:**

“In the *Maqāṣid*, the more plausible interpretation is to take the restoration described as easier to mean the restoration of constituent parts and surviving material to their previous forms and combinations, as indicated by His saying, exalted is He, ‘Say: He who originated it the first time shall give it life,’ rather than the reinstatement of what has ceased to exist.”

**Technical distinction:** `al-ajzāʾ` (constituents), `al-mawādd` (underlying material), `al-ṣuwar` (forms), and `al-taʾlīfāt` (combinations) are not redundant terms. The cited interpretation permits a restoration that depends on material continuity and reconfiguration, without necessarily affirming the metaphysical possibility of restoring an annihilated individual as numerically the same after total nonexistence. The source context and the long lower note must decide whether this is the commentator's chosen answer or a position reported for examination. The source heading `al-Maqāṣid` requires further bibliographical and exact-quotation identification.

**Editorial counting:** One **continuous** upper-registry source record has been added, but it overlaps several of the 54 selected short phrases and cannot be counted as an additional translated whole original page or as newly translated independent marginalia.


### G. Further actual lower-register source passages (writer unidentified)

**Printed II:244, original PDF 539, selected small type (not the complete note):**

> ولا يكفيه العلم به بالوجه الكلي لأن إدراكه بالوجه الكلي لا ينحصر فيه

“A merely universal knowledge of it is insufficient, because apprehension of it under a universal description does not uniquely identify that very individual.”

This is an important **epistemological objection** to a purely modal account of return. A statement that a *possible kind* can exist again does not yet identify which *numerical individual* would be restored. The text thus distinguishes the ability to conceive a kind and the means of reidentifying the prior subject.

**Printed II:245, original PDF 540, another selected lower fragment:**

> والحاصل أن الفرق حاصل بأن المبدأ هو ما وقع في الدفعة الأولى

“In sum, the distinction is that origination is what took place on the first occurrence.”

This is one short phrase in a much longer source argument, not a complete marginal gloss. It frames the attempted reply that the first and later existence differ by their temporal order even if the same essence is in view.

**Both fragments are author-UNASSIGNED**; no Wasīm attribution is inferred from lower-case print. Neither supplies the missing complete source arguments of the same pages, and independent second proof remains necessary.
