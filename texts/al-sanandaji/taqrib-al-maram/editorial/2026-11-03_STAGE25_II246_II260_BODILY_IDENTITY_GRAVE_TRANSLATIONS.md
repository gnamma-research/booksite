# Stage 25 — Sanandajī: body, numerical identity, grave and reckoning
## Original printed II:246–260 · physical scan PDF 541–555

**Status:** Direct per-page original-image review and **34 carefully selected Arabic–English source expressions**; this is not a complete running-text translation of fifteen pages. Particularly substantial original objections concerning the eater and the eaten, the original elements of a body and the life of the grave remain much longer than the excerpts translated here. Small-print individual historical writers, including Wasīm, are *not assumed* without identifying the individual note. This dossier supersedes the overly broad Stage 17 source-section mapping for II:246–260.

### A. Corrections to the original source map

- **II:246–248:** whether the soul alone or body and soul return; a supposed spiritual resurrection does not exhaust the transmitted corporeal doctrine. The source moves from bodily substance and original parts to how material can become assimilated into another person.
- **II:248→249:** the eater/eaten objection begins near the foot of II:248 and continues at the top of II:249; the two physical page locations must be preserved when presenting a joined argument.
- **II:249–251:** essential/original constituent parts (`al-ajzāʾ al-aṣliyya`) versus later acquired or surplus material; whether matter can belong to two restored persons; relative roles of the soul and body in numerical identity.
- **II:252–255:** possible restoration, the first and the later occurrence, the relation of original matter to later individual forms; then Paradise and Hell, their current or subsequent existence, and whether the conditions of nourishment there follow the material processes of terrestrial life.
- **II:256:** a genuine original `فصل` opens the questioning, punishment and recompense of the grave. The print invokes the Qurʾānic description of Pharaoh's people exposed to the Fire morning and evening, before the more intense punishment at the Hour.
- **II:257–259:** whether an intermediate state requires sensation and how to interpret two deaths and two lives, including `ربنا أمتنا اثنتين وأحييتنا اثنتين`. Large marginal notes debate whether ordinary bodily states are required for such perception.
- **II:260:** revealed eschatological particulars: reckoning, balance, bridge and associated testimony.

### C. Two longer source-read sequences (selected words, visible ellipses for intervening omissions)

**1. The eater and the eaten, printed II:248→249**

**Selected original source:** `فإن أكل ... إنسان إنسانا بحيث صار بعض أجزائه ... فالأجزاء إما أن تعاد ... في بدن الآكل أو المأكول ... الأجزاء الأصلية ... الأجزاء الفاضلة`.

**Close translation of the actual selected spans, not a verbatim translation of omitted portions:** “If someone eats ... another human, such that some of the latter's constituents become [assimilated] ... then the relevant parts are either returned ... in the eater's body or in the eaten person's body ... [this raises the distinction of] original constitutive elements ... and acquired or surplus parts.”

**Interpretive objection:** The alleged dilemma presupposes that the same material can count as an essential bodily constituent of both persons, yet the bodies are numerically distinct. An account that identifies persons solely through every particle of matter may be unable to specify whose constituent it is. The source's answer appeals to `al-ajzāʾ al-aṣliyya`, not simply to the possibility of unlimited bodily creation. How the original constituents are individuated remains philosophically contentious and is the subject of the longer marginal objections. The upper text and small-print author must ultimately be translated continuously before any definitive authorial position is ascribed.

**2. The questioning of the grave, printed II:256**

**Selected connected original source text:** `فصل ... سؤال القبر وعذابه ... حق بالآيات والأحاديث المتواترة المعنى ... النار يعرضون عليها غدوا وعشيا ... ويوم تقوم الساعة أدخلوا آل فرعون أشد العذاب`.

**Translation, preserving omissions:** “Chapter. ... Questioning and punishment in the grave ... are established through scriptural verses and reports of collectively transmitted meaning ... ‘The Fire, to which they are exposed morning and evening’ ... ‘And on the day the Hour arises: admit Pharaoh's people to the severest punishment.’”

**The actual source's inference:** The interpretive argument distinguishes exposure to fire before the Hour from punishment associated with the Hour itself. That is an argument for an intermediate stage, but it does not automatically establish the physical manner of a disembodied person's suffering. The smaller objections on II:256–259 then examine the phenomenology, power and conditions of awareness and life. The main text and lower authorial explanation must remain separate.

### D. Four interpretive distinctions not to lose in a definitive critical translation

**Numerical identity is not equivalence of matter.** The eater/eaten puzzle requires distinguishing a person's original bodily identity from parts acquired through nutrition or later assimilation. Calling the latter 'surplus' (*faḍila*) does not by itself settle the former's precise metaphysics; it identifies the kind of material that the objection can safely exclude without claiming it belonged eternally to the original subject.

**Affirming the spiritual return does not negate the bodily return.** In the reported philosophical dispute, a spiritual *maʿād* concerns the soul. The theological evidence presented for bodily resurrection adds claims concerning embodied individuality; the two must not be handled as merely interchangeable senses of one word.

**A verse may be evidence for a temporal distinction rather than a physiology.** The Pharaoh passage is invoked to distinguish the intermediate punishment from the event of the final Hour. Whether a mode of life is restored in the grave and what kind of consciousness obtains require further independent premises.

**Count death and life only under a fixed definition.** The appeal to two deaths and two lives can be read differently depending upon whether pre-birth nonlife counts as death, and whether an intermediate grave life is assumed. The source's arguments require attribution to the opposing interpreter, not just putting a Qurʾānic verse beside a modern doctrinal paraphrase.
