## I:9 — Knowledge of religious doctrines and the meaning of certainty

### I009.upper.01 — Religious convictions rather than juridical practice
**Arabic:** «الاعتقاد، أيضاً المقصود من العلم به علم الفقه» · «(الدينية) المنسوبة إلى دين نبينا محمد صلى الله عليه وعلى آله وسلم» · «سواء أكانت من الدين في الواقع ككلام أهل الحق أم لا ككلام الفرق المبتدعة».

**English:** What is at issue is **doctrinal assent**, not the objects of jurisprudential knowledge in their practical aspect. “Religious” means attributed to the religion of our Prophet Muḥammad—God bless him and his family and grant him peace. The designation can therefore include both propositions that truly belong to that religion, as the author understands the discourse of the orthodox, and propositions that are only **claimed** to belong to it, as in the discourse of groups he calls innovators. Such attribution does not imply that every proposition so named is correct. The author is defining the class of questions discussed in a discipline, not certifying every proposed answer.

**Status:** Upper source-facing, terms read from the scan; polemical labels belong to the historical text.

### I009.upper.02 — Evidence that yields certainty
**Arabic:** «فإذا كان المراد من العلم مطلق التصديق سواء طابق الواقع أو لا ...» · «(عن الأدلة اليقينية) أي المكتسب».

**English:** If “knowledge” is interpreted indiscriminately as any assent, whether or not it corresponds to reality, the definition would also include mistaken conviction. The qualification “from **certain proofs**” is introduced to delimit the intended sort of acquired cognition. But it raises a further question: does certainty here qualify the *proof objectively*, the *knower's state*, or the *evidence as deemed certain by the proponent*? The next page examines these readings rather than presuming their equivalence.

**Status:** Upper passage close reading with terminal continuation to I:10.

### I009.ruled.01 — The intended scope of ‘acquired’
**Arabic:** «(قوله أي المكتسب)» · «الدليل متعلق بالعلم» · «باعتبار تضمّنه معنى الاكتساب لا ...» · «الإذعان في المقاصد».

**English:** On “that is, acquired”: the phrase about proofs is understood as relating to knowledge **insofar as it is acquired**, rather than as an independent condition attached indiscriminately to every belief. This is a grammatical and conceptual clarification: it explains why the definition appeals to proofs where the activity of the discipline is demonstrative, without implying that every occurrence of the same theological proposition must be acquired by a proof.

**Status:** Short ruled gloss, provisional; final source reference requires verification.

### I009.lower.01 — Why the distinction does not reduce kalām to fiqh
**Arabic:** «الفقه ذكرنا لا إجماع حيث يتعلق الغرض باعتقادها دون العمل بها» · «تدخل في التعريف» · «في أصول الفقه مقصودة لذاتها».

**English:** The marginal discussion distinguishes an act's being relevant to *belief about a ruling* from the act's *practical observance*. Some propositions examined in law or legal theory can enter theology under a different respect, namely when what matters is assent to a revealed judgment rather than deriving an action from it. That change of perspective does not turn kalām into jurisprudence: in legal theory, the principles are pursued for their role in establishing rulings; in kalām, a proposition can be discussed because of its relation to religious belief itself. The writer tests which disciplines would be improperly included if this regard were omitted from the definition.

**Status:** Lower-register source-facing explanation, several lines compressed in English.

### I009.lower.02 — The imitator, fallible assent and status of evidence
**Arabic:** «(قوله عن الأدلة اليقينية)» · «(قوله سواء طابق الواقع أو لا)» · «(قوله أي المكتسب)».

**English:** On the phrase “from certain proofs,” the gloss presses the distinction between possession of a true belief and possession of it **through an argument**. The imitator may assent to a true theological proposition without himself knowing the proof. If every correct conviction were counted as the science of kalām, its boundary would expand beyond the intellectual activity under definition. Conversely, treating any proof the speaker calls “certain” as genuinely demonstrative risks admitting erroneous arguments. The author of the gloss does not allow the subjective impression of certainty to settle this issue.

**Status:** Argument summary grounded in visible anchors; literal clause mapping incomplete.

## I:10 — Evidence, opinion, and the science's subject

### I010.upper.01 — Is certainty in the proof or in the mind?
**Arabic:** «ذلك العلم عن أدلتها» · «العلم اليقيني» · «لأن الدليل اليقيني ما يفيد اليقين» · «المراد بالأدلة العقدية المعتقدة بزعم المستدل».

**English:** The qualification directs one to knowledge **through its proofs**, and the evidence called certain is, strictly speaking, that which gives rise to certainty. Yet a different reading is also reported: the proofs intended might be those **believed to be conclusive by the person arguing**. These are not equivalent. In one reading the definition selects genuinely warranted cognition; in the other it describes the kind of claim put forward by practitioners, including those who dispute one another.

**Status:** Upper text close reading; the narrow wording around *al-adilla al-ʿaqdiyya* is unclear and must be reviewed.

### I010.upper.02 — The boundaries of acquired religious knowledge
**Arabic:** «ثم إنهم اعتبروا في أدلتها اليقين» · «لئلا عبرة بالظن في الاعتقادات» · «وعلم الرسول بالاعتقادات وكذا اعتقاد المقلد».

**English:** Because these are questions of faith, some authors demand certainty rather than mere probability. But the commentary asks about exceptional forms of correct theological assent: God's knowledge, prophetic knowledge, and the conviction of a person who merely follows another. The function of the word “science” in the definition must be **acquired and disciplined doctrinal knowledge**, not the simple occurrence of a true judgment in any knower whatever. The argument is continued on I:11.

**Status:** Upper text, joined I:10→11.

### I010.lower.01 — Error, opinion and objective conformity
**Arabic:** «(قوله العلم اليقيني)» · «المطابق للواقع في نفس الأمر» · «(قوله الدليل اليقيني)» · «لا يخرج إدراك المخطئ».

**English:** On “certain knowledge,” the gloss asks whether *conformity to reality* is built into the notion of knowledge. If it is, a wrong judgment cannot become knowledge merely through its firmness. If the proof is “certain” only **in the disputant's opinion**, incorrect judgments may again enter through that concession. A likely intended response distinguishes correctness from the psychological condition of firmness; both must be checked. The marginal writer is investigating the **scope of the definition**, not asserting that all reported opinions have the same evidential standing.

**Status:** Partial lower translation, no definitive transcription.

### I010.lower.02 — The difficult case of the imitator
**Arabic:** «(قوله العقيدة بزعم المستدل)» · «المطابقة للواقع باعتقاده» · «التصديق الجازم سواء طابق الواقع أم لا» · «المقلد».

**English:** A person who imitates a teacher can hold a firm conviction and even, by good fortune, a true one; but that alone does not mean he possesses the **science** characterised by justified inference. The gloss tests whether the exclusion is achieved by “certain” or only by specifying the act of acquisition from proofs. It also distinguishes an opinion which is merely *probable*, a categorical **error**, and a firmly held **true** assertion. None should be conflated with the activity of establishing religious doctrines through arguments.

**Status:** Source-facing marginal account; lower proof remains open.

### I010.lower.03 — Does kalām concern only necessary propositions?
**Arabic:** «اليقين ...» · «هل المراد ... القطعية» · «علم الكلام».

**English:** The discussion notes that a proof of a theological claim must not be judged conclusive merely because its proponent feels no doubt. Nor does the word *certainty* mean that every theological issue is a self-evident proposition. It concerns the character of the assent appropriately established by the investigation. The closing observation returns to the stated limits of the discipline and supplies the join to I:11.

**Status:** Condensed lower text; final compact sentences still need separate Arabic extraction.

## I:11 — Naming a discipline and identifying its subject

### I011.upper.01 — The Companions and the historical term ‘kalām’
**Arabic:** «علم الصحابة» · «وإن لم يسمَّ في ذلك الزمان بالكلام» · «كما أن علمهم بالحساب ... لم يسمَّ به».

**English:** The knowledge possessed by the Prophet's Companions was not called “kalām” at the time, just as their knowledge of other matters was not necessarily named after the later disciplines in which those matters were organised. The historical **name** of a discipline does not determine whether earlier persons possessed the relevant knowledge. One must distinguish the activity or insight from the subsequent technical label applied to it.

**Status:** Upper short passage read from scan.

### I011.upper.02 — Subject of the science
**Arabic:** «(وموضوعه المعلوم من حيث يتعلق بذلك)».

**English:** “Its subject is **the known**, insofar as it is connected with that”—that is, with the establishment of religious doctrines. The restriction “insofar as” is doing essential work: kalām is not simply an inventory of all knowable things, but a study of things under the regard in which they enter demonstrations concerning belief.

**Status:** Upper lemma and concise sharḥ, joined to I:12.

### I011.lower.01 — Whether imitation can count as justified knowledge
**Arabic:** «(قوله وكذا اعتقاد المقلد)» · «أهل الكشف» · «المعتقد المقلد» · «الاعتقاد الجازم».

**English:** The lower author tests the previous claim about imitation. An imitator's conviction may be firmly held and correspond to reality, yet lack the evidence appropriate to a demonstrative science. In contrast, the settled cognition of a competent investigator may be acquired through the orderly pursuit of proof. The gloss distinguishes the issue **whether a belief is true** from the issue **whether its possessor can establish it**. That distinction bears directly on the definition of kalām by proofs.

**Status:** Lower argument partly interpretative.

### I011.lower.02 — Is ‘the known’ too broad a subject?
**Arabic:** «(قوله موضوعه)» · «لفظ المعلوم» · «الموضوع عام» · «العقائد الدينية».

**English:** The subject term “the known” appears excessively broad, since it can include both what exists and what does not, and things studied by many other sciences. The commentator therefore insists on the delimiting **relation to religious doctrines**. Another question arises: does the word “known” refer to the *subject term* in every theorem, to all entities in the field, or to whatever is considered for the sake of proving theological conclusions? An adequate definition must not switch silently between these senses.

**Status:** Marginal source-facing reading; line-by-line correspondence pending.

### I011.lower.03 — Propositions versus constituent terms
**Arabic:** «التصديق بوجود الموضوع» · «جزء من المعلوم» · «المبادئ التصورية» · «المبادئ التصديقية».

**English:** The margin elaborates the relation between knowing the *terms* of a proposition and assenting to the *proposition itself*. Demonstration presupposes conceptions of its terms, while the truth of a conclusion involves judgment. On that basis, subjects, predicates and first principles may all enter a discipline without each separately being the final object of its assent. This is relevant to whether the subject of kalām should be stated in terms of “the known” or in terms of the propositions it establishes.

**Status:** Provisional lower explanation, ending connects to I:12.

## I:12 — The known, the nonexistent and what helps establish doctrine

### I012.upper.01 — The range of auxiliary questions
**Arabic:** «أي بالعقائد الدينية» · «أحوال المانع من القدم والوحدة» · «أحوال الجسم والعرض والحدوث والتركيب» · «أحوال المحال والمعدومات».

**English:** The connection intended is to **religious doctrines**. The scope of inquiry therefore includes characteristics that exclude a body's beginninglessness or absolute unity, the properties of body and accident, origination and composition, and questions about substantiality, individuation and other categories. It also extends to **impossibilities** and **nonexistents**, so far as propositions about them are needed to establish the desired doctrines. The scope is not limited to propositions that themselves state an article of faith; the inquiry can examine premises and distinctions used to demonstrate such propositions.

**Status:** Upper passage connected translation, lexical refinements still pending.

### I012.upper.02 — A common subject broad enough for nonexistents
**Arabic:** «فالمعلوم المتناول للموجود والمعدوم» · «وقد قيل المعلوم».

**English:** Consequently the “known” can be taken broadly enough to embrace **existent and nonexistent** alike. This is why the discipline's subject cannot simply be “body,” “existent substance,” or another restricted ontological genus. Whatever is needed as a subject of intellectual judgment under the specified theological relation can enter consideration.

**Status:** Upper final sentence joins I:13.

### I012.ruled.01 — Does a subject have to be demonstrable in every proposition?
**Arabic:** «(قوله لا تبحث الخ)» · «بموضوعية الموضوع» · «اه منه».

**English:** The marginal note tests the claim that inquiry must always be about the subject *as such*. It is enough in some cases to understand how the subject or a property of the subject figures in a chain of demonstration. The distinction is again a matter of how propositions are organised within the science, not a proof that the things considered are all actual existents.

**Status:** Middle ruled note, partial reading. A compact clause requires another image check.

### I012.lower.01 — The author's use of ‘connection’
**Arabic:** «(قوله أي بالعقائد الدينية الخ)» · «الأحكام والنسب» · «لا يتم بيان الموضوع بدون غير الموضوع».

**English:** The lower commentary objects that the phrase “insofar as it is connected with religious doctrines” requires explanation. The proof of a theological assertion may use not only doctrines but **relations, negations, and judgments** concerning items considered in other disciplines. Some things are not taken up because of their intrinsic nature, but because denying or affirming them makes a difference to an eventual conclusion. The author tests whether that consideration is sufficient to distinguish kalām from other sciences, or merely imports their questions wholesale.

**Status:** Connected lower source interpretation; incomplete literal transcript.

### I012.lower.02 — Predicates and the relation of the ‘known’
**Arabic:** «(قوله المتناول للموجود والمعدوم)» · «المعلوم» · «الموضوع» · «المحمول».

**English:** The breadth of the known object matters especially when a question concerns a thing **that cannot exist**. The impossible is still an object of conceptual judgment: one can affirm its impossibility without giving it external existence. The gloss turns from this to a logical objection. If the theological matter is **predicated** of a subject rather than itself functioning as the subject term, can the definition still say “the known” is the science's subject? The discussion prepares the solution on I:13, where the predicates of theological problems are explicitly mentioned.

**Status:** Source-facing partial translation of the long lower continuation; the opening should be checked together with I:11.
