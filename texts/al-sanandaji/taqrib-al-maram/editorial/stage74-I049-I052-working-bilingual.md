## I:49 — mental forms, written names and the representation of an object

### I049.upper.01 — from name to written mark to the thing signified

**Arabic anchors:** «(الاسم)» · «(و) في الخط (صورته) المرقومة» · «لو أضيف إلى اللفظ الموضوع» · «الوجود في الأعيان».

**English.** [The author completes a comparison involving the] **name** and its representation **in writing**, the inscribed shape of the expression. If the word signifies something, an inscribed form can signify the word, and through it lead us to what the word designates. But the ink or written pattern is not numerically identical with its spoken expression or the external object. The different relations of signification need to be distinguished from the actual mode of existence of the object signified.

### I049.upper.02 — the mental status of what cognition is about

**Arabic anchors:** «(والدليل على) الوجود (الذهني)» · «مع أن كون العلم سواء» · «في الأعيان».

**English.** The argument for **mental existence** draws upon the fact that an object can be known and judged concerning even when the external thing is not present. It is possible to form an intelligible object of cognition, but the inference from that possibility to the **real presence of the object's quiddity in the mind** needs to be tested. Merely having a sign, or even a mental image, does not yet establish the exact metaphysical thesis.

### I049.lower.01 — the inscription may point to words without being words

**Arabic anchors:** «(قوله نعم لو أضيف إلى اللفظ الموضوع الخ)» · «الوجود اللفظي المجازي» · «الوضع» · «النقش».

**English.** On the remark that the name might be added to the expression established by linguistic convention, the marginal writer clarifies the difference between the *conventional association* that makes an utterance significant and the *graphic inscription* that reproduces its shape. A written word is related to a spoken word, but the writing's existence on paper is not the spoken sound's existence in the air. It is a derivative or figurative mode of the word's availability to the reader.

### I049.lower.02 — does the representation preserve its object's identity?

**Arabic anchors:** «الألفاظ» · «الصور الذهنية» · «الأعيان الخارجية» · «اختلاف الأوضاع والأشخاص».

**English.** The discussion examines a chain: object in external existence, the form by which one apprehends it, a spoken expression and a written mark. The links may be used to identify an object, but the identity of the thing represented must not be confused with identity of the representations. In particular, the same written or spoken name can refer to different particulars, whereas different names may indicate one referent. Consequently, one cannot demonstrate mental existence simply by asserting that the image and external object must share the same individual existence.

### I049.lower.03 — information about a nonexistent object

**Arabic anchors:** «الحكم على الأشياء» · «الصورة الحاصلة في الذهن» · «الأمور المعدومة» · «العلم».

**English.** The commentator then draws attention to judgement about absent or nonexistent objects. A person may acquire information about a thing not before the senses, because the concept or form is available for intellectual consideration. Yet the availability of this concept requires explanation: does it give the quiddity another mode of being, or does it supply a sign or likeness capable of sustaining judgement? The author indicates that the answer determines whether the opponent has proved the thesis of mental existence or only the more modest fact of conceiving what is absent.

## I:50 — affirmative judgements about impossibilities

### I050.upper.01 — the intellect can affirm what is not externally possible

**Arabic anchors:** «(أنا نعقل الأمور الثبوتية في الخارج إذ نحكم على الممتنعات)» · «إيجابا» · «الممتنع» · «من المفهومات».

**English.** The intellect forms concepts and even makes **affirmative judgements about impossibilities**. An object of judgement can therefore be present to thought without being an external thing. If the judgement contains a positive predicate, it is not enough simply to say that a positive judgement requires its subject to exist externally, since the very instances under discussion are said to be impossible externally. What exists in the mind may be the concept or representation under which the judgement is made.

### I050.upper.02 — the possible and impossible under a concept

**Arabic anchors:** «(ولا)» · «لا وجود لموضوعها في الخارج أصلا» · «كقولنا كل عنقاء حيوان».

**English.** Consider a proposition about a phoenix: “Every phoenix is an animal.” The subject-expression can be understood and the predicate related to it in judgement although no phoenix need exist outside the intellect. The argument demands an explanation of what, exactly, is the subject of that predicate. Treating the conceptual subject as a presently existing external animal would be to misunderstand the example.

### I050.lower.01 — the claim need not be that the identical external object exists mentally

**Arabic anchors:** «(قوله مقتضيا الثبوت الخ)» · «الوجود الذهني» · «ملاحظة» · «لا حاجة إلى الدليل».

**English.** The lower commentator objects that the proof may establish no more than a meaningful presentation of a non-external object. The mind can attend to the subject and predicate and judge their relation. This does not by itself show that the very same quiddity, with all the attributes of an external existent, has migrated into another receptacle. Without distinguishing conceptual availability from extra-mental actuality, one risks making the conclusion true by definition rather than establishing it by demonstration.

### I050.ruled.01 — affirmative predication is not an ordinary external inherence

**Arabic anchors:** «(قوله إذ نحكم على الممتنعات الخ)» · «الحكم في السلب» · «ثبوت».

**English.** The separately marked note qualifies the inference from affirmative judgement: an affirmative proposition involving an impossible subject is not the same as the external inherence of an accident in an existing substance. The intellect may combine concepts in a judgement; the ontological status of the object of that judgement remains the matter to be demonstrated.

### I050.lower.02 — the phoenix and the proposed 'mental subject'

**Arabic anchors:** «العنقاء» · «موجودا في الذهن» · «لم يكن حاصلا في الخارج» · «الوجود الذهني».

**English.** The author's reply is that a concept of the phoenix is possessed by the knower, and a judgement may be made with respect to it. But the mental concept is not thereby shown to have all the external predicates of a creature. In particular, saying “the phoenix is an animal” does not make a phoenix breathe in the room. The discussion is thus about the scope of predication and the dependence of cognition on a representation, not about a straightforward instance of an external relation.

## I:51 — the nature of the predication that serves as a proof for mental existence

### I051.upper.01 — only an affirmative relation, not an externally subsisting accident, is required

**Arabic anchors:** «كل جسم حادث والقضية» · «فالتعقل» · «(إن كان بالمحمول)» · «في الذهن» · «إضافة».

**English.** In a judgement such as “Every body is originated,” the intellect understands the subject and the predicate and affirms their relation. But the **act of understanding** is not identical with the external coming-to-be of each subject to which the judgement applies. If the relevant relation is obtained through the predicate's belonging to the subject conceptually, one must explain whether that belonging is an external accident or the relation employed by the mind in judging.

### I051.upper.02 — predication and addition are not the same

**Arabic anchors:** «ولا يكن محصله فيه» · «(فلا محالة يقتضي)» · «التعقل (إضافة)» · «بين العاقل والمعقول».

**English.** A proposed proof says that cognition requires a kind of **relation** (*iḍāfa*) between a knower and what is known. In the absence of the known thing externally, this relation must have a terminus in the mind. The respondent demands clarification: does the claim concern the existence of the known object itself, the presence of a concept through which it is known, or simply the logical relation expressed by the proposition? These are different claims, and their differences matter to the argument for mental existence.

### I051.lower.01 — what is meant by 'the intelligible is present'

**Arabic anchors:** «(قوله والفهم والتميز عند العقل الخ)» · «وجوده في الخارج» · «وجوده في الذهن» · «الوجود المجرد».

**English.** The longer gloss explains that *intelligible presence* need not mean actual presence of the external subject. The mind may understand a proposition about a thing absent from the senses. If the thesis is only that a concept is present in cognition, it will hardly be disputed; if it is that the quiddity itself obtains a genuine additional form of existence, the argument needs premises that discriminate the two. The author considers a reply that the same quiddity can exist in different modes, while its externally associated accidents need not follow it into the mind.

### I051.lower.02 — a claimed additional existence and the danger of circularity

**Arabic anchors:** «حاصل تجرد» · «الوجود في الذهن» · «الإضافة بين العاقل والمعقول» · «لا إثبات في الخارج».

**English.** The gloss examines whether the relation of knower and known can be established before the mental presence of the known object is proved. If the relation is used as evidence for mental existence, one cannot simply define its terminus as a mentally existent instance of the object; that would assume the conclusion. Yet some relation appears involved in the cognitive act. The issue is whether its terminus must be an externally existing thing, an existent mental form, or an object given through the mind's intellectual act. The author's reply remains conditional on the interpretation of cognition.

### I051.lower.03 — ending of the proof and transition to the objection

**Arabic anchors:** «حصول الصورة» · «الثبوت في الخارج» · «المعقول» · «الوجود».

**English.** The marginal passage closes by requiring the reader to distinguish the existence of a *form of what is known* from the external actuality of the *thing itself*. The proponent regards the mental form as supplying the predicate-relation; the opponent may grant the form without granting what the proponent wishes to infer about the known object. The objection on I:52 will ask why, if the real object's essence is mentally present, it does not undergo all the same qualifications it would undergo in the external world.

## I:52 — why mental existence does not make the object subject to external contraries

### I052.upper.01 — the familiar paradox of contradictory external properties

**Arabic anchors:** «إذا قد حصل الأمر المطلوب بحصول المثال هذا» · «لو كان المعقول وجود» · «في الذهن» · «الأعراض المتصورة حتى المتضادات».

**English.** The opponent asks: if the actual object known really comes to exist in the mind, why do the accidents ordinarily associated with the object not arise there? Mental representations can include **contrary descriptions** or things whose external conjunction is impossible. Yet the mind does not thereby become the site of incompatible external qualities. Therefore the argument must make clear what “mental existence” means, rather than transferring the rules of external inherence into cognition without qualification.

### I052.upper.02 — the representation is present without the external attribute inhering

**Arabic anchors:** «(وهو وجود غير متأصل)» · «(لا يقتضي الاتصاف)» · «كالكون يتصور الكفر».

**English.** The response is that mental existence is **not the same kind of rooted, independent external existence**. Thus the mind's conceiving a quality does not entail that the mind becomes externally qualified by that quality. The one who conceives disbelief does not thereby become an unbeliever, just as someone may conceive a colour or a contradiction without inheriting the external attribute described. The consequence at issue is limited to apprehension, not to the occurrence of the objects' real physical properties in the knower.

### I052.upper.03 — water in a house versus water in a mind

**Arabic anchors:** «(كالماء في البيت)» · «بواسطة الكون» · «وجود الشيء في وجود أصيل».

**English.** A comparison is drawn with water in a house. The water's occupying a room is an externally realised spatial relation: its actual location in that room accompanies a particular condition of existence. When the *water* is apprehended by the mind, this does not mean that the very same water now occupies a corner of the brain as it occupies the house. The difference between external emplacement and intellectual representation explains why external attributes are not all transferred into cognition.

### I052.lower.01 — forms need not be mental instances of every non-external object

**Arabic anchors:** «(قوله قد يحصل المثال الخ)» · «المعقولات التي لا وجود لها في الخارج» · «صورة المعقول» · «العقل الفعال».

**English.** The commentator cites a discussion in the *Sharḥ al-Maqāṣid* about intelligibles that do not exist externally. A concept of a nonexistent or impossible thing is not a reason to suppose that the object itself has an external instance. The theory that mental forms are impressed or supplied through an intellectual cause must also explain how incompatible or impossible objects can be known without requiring those things to be realised. The objection is a problem for a simplistic account of mental existence, not a demonstration that there can be no representation of nonexistents.

### I052.lower.02 — how the known form can be present without inhering as the thing's external nature

**Arabic anchors:** «صور الكائنات في العقل الفعال» · «الصفات» · «الوجود في الأعيان» · «المفهومات».

**English.** The author distinguishes the being of a form **in an intellect** from the thing's existence among external individuals. An intelligible can be available to the mind without the mind being an instance of every property predicated of the object. If one says the form is present as an intelligible, that requires no such external inherence; but if one says that the object itself is reproduced there in the complete manner of its external existence, the absurdities alleged by the opponent return. The debate continues at I:53 with the question whether the mental form and what the form depicts are identical.
