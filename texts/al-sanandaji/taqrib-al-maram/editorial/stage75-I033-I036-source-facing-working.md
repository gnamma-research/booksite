## I:33 · Opening of Book Two: general questions

### I033.upper.01 — Transition from logic to general metaphysics
**Arabic anchors:** «العقائد وإثبات مباحث أخرى تتوقف عليها العقائد فهذا أوان الشروع في المقاصد فنقول»؛ «(الباب الثاني في الأمور العامة)».

**English:** The discussion has established [the preliminary instruments for examining] articles of belief and other matters upon which such articles depend. It is now time to turn to the principal objectives. The text therefore announces **Book Two: General Matters** (*al-umūr al-ʿāmma*), that is, questions which recur in the investigation of existence and do not belong exclusively to one special kind of thing.

### I033.upper.02 — The scope and divisions of *al-umūr al-ʿāmma*
**Arabic anchors:** «الشاملة للموجودات بأسرها من الواجب والجوهر والعرض كالوجود والوحدة»؛ «فإن كل موجود وإن كان كثيراً له وحدة ما باعتبار»؛ «وكثرة الموجودات»؛ «كالإمكان الخاص والحدوث والوجوب بالقيد والكسرة والعلية والمعلولية»؛ «(تصور الوجود)».

**English:** These general matters include those applicable across what exists—Necessary Being, substance and accident—such as **existence and unity**. Even a many-sided existent has some unity under an appropriate consideration. Other general matters include plurality, possibility in the restricted sense, origination, necessity, and the relationships of cause and effect, though some do not belong to all existents in precisely the same manner. The discussion follows an established pedagogical order and begins with the **conception of existence** (*taṣawwur al-wujūd*). [The main paragraph continues on I:34.]

### I033.ruled.01 — Why these matters precede the special inquiries
**Arabic anchors:** «(قال رحمه الله الباب الثاني في الأمور العامة)»؛ «لما أن فيما يختص بواحد من الواجب والجوهر والعرض ما يحتاج إلى باب معرفة الأحوال المشتركة»؛ «الوجود والعلية أو بين الاثنين».

**English:** On the author's heading “General Matters”: the reason for examining them here is that the discussions specific to the Necessary, substances or accidents depend upon an earlier investigation of concepts common among different kinds of existent. Existence and causality are obvious examples, while other concepts apply to two of those kinds or concern the contrast between them. The note is a justification of **the sequence of inquiry**, not another independent proof of the particular theses to follow.

### I033.lower.01 — The possibility of demonstrations in this general science
**Arabic anchors:** «التعقل والتوقف الشيء على نفسه»؛ «لكن ينبغي أن يعلم أن ذلك إنما هو فيما يقصد منه حصول القطع»؛ «بمجرد إفادة الظن»؛ «التواتر نظرياً واستدلالياً»؛ «(قوله من الواجب والجوهر والعرض الخ)».

**English:** The margin returns to a methodological warning: if the act of arriving at knowledge required, as a necessary prior condition, the very knowledge to be acquired, the inquiry would be circular. But the objection must be qualified by the **epistemic aim** of a proof. An argument intended to produce certainty is not the same as an indication that yields only probability. Some scholars regard the cognition established by recurring reports or testimony as depending on intellectual attention to particular conditions, while others take its force more immediately. The writer cautions against using the uncertainty of a particular example to reject the whole field of demonstrations. In the ensuing discussion of existents, the categories “Necessary,” “substance” and “accident” serve as a survey of what can be considered; they do not imply that every shared predicate is an independently existing universal.

## I:34 · Is the idea of existence immediate?

### I034.upper.01 — Existence and nonexistence must already be conceived
**Arabic anchors:** «ضروري»؛ «فإن قولنا الشيء إما موجود أو معدوم تصديق بديهي»؛ «وأنه يتوقف على تصور الوجود والعدم فيكون بديهياً»؛ «فإن قلت إن توقفت بديهة جميع أجزائه ...»؛ «وإن زعمت أن الحكم ... بعد تصور الطرفين بديهي».

**English:** The notion of **existence** is immediately intelligible. One argument appeals to the evident truth that “a thing is either existent or nonexistent.” That judgement must involve a conception of existence and nonexistence. But the critic objects: from the evident character of the judgement it does not follow without further qualification that all its constituent conceptions are themselves primitive and immediate. Perhaps the judgement becomes evident only **after** the two sides have been conceived; its being self-evident under that condition would not prove that the conception of either side has no dependence on earlier cognition. The main text records this objection rather than silently treating the initial argument as conclusive.

### I034.lower.01 — The scope of general notions
**Arabic anchors:** «لا يشمل الموجود أصلاً كالامتناع»؛ «وما يخص الواجب كالقدم والوجوب»؛ «وما يخص الممكن في مقابلة الإمكان»؛ «أما العدم فكذلك عند المتكلمين»؛ «(قوله إنه يتوقف على تصور الوجود الخ)».

**English:** The gloss first considers what qualifies as a **general matter**. Not everything discussed under this heading applies positively to every actual existent; for example, impossibility is a concept concerning what is not actual, while eternity and certain kinds of necessity are specific to the Necessary, and contingency pertains to possible beings. Nonexistence too is a general contrast, but it is not an existent thing of the same order. Philosophers and theologians differ in how they classify such topics. Thus “general” concerns the scope and utility of the inquiry, not the claim that all its subjects are actual substances. On the evidence from the self-evident judgement, the gloss asks whether one means immediate conception or merely a certain affirmation once the terms have been understood.

### I034.lower.02 — The logical difference between *badāha* and *muṣādara*
**Arabic anchors:** «بديهة تصور الموجود بالضرورة»؛ «(قوله فصادرة)»؛ «إن أراد بديهة الحكم»؛ «بداهة جميع أجزائه»؛ «العلم بالمركب»؛ «جميع أجزائه».

**English:** A judgement may be self-evident *once its terms are understood*, while understanding those terms can require earlier attention. The objection therefore rejects an **equivocation** between an evident proposition and an evident conception. If the original argument assumes that the constituent notions are already self-evident, it has assumed what it seeks to prove; if it means only that the combination yields an immediate assent, the proof is insufficient. The marginal author also asks about the difference between grasping the **whole proposition** and grasping each of its **parts**, and why certainty of the former should be thought to entail separate certainty concerning each part. The beginning of the next page continues this line of criticism.

## I:35 · Knowledge of a whole and knowledge of its constituents

### I035.upper.01 — The limit of the argument from immediate assent
**Arabic anchors:** «كسبياً فلهذا التصديق بديهي مطلقاً ولا مصادرة»؛ «لأن بديهة مطلقاً في نفس الأمر».

**English:** The text distinguishes a judgement which is evident without inference from a knowledge which has been acquired. If the judgement is evident *in itself*, the description “self-evident” belongs to its manner of assent; it still does not follow that every preparatory conception has been established without a distinct process. The response to the charge of circularity thus depends on exactly what is being claimed as immediately known.

### I035.lower.01 — Does knowing a composite entail knowing each part?
**Arabic anchors:** «(قوله هذا التصديق بديهي مطلقاً الخ) أي بجميع أجزائه»؛ «بداهة المجموع»؛ «بداهة كل جزء مفصلاً»؛ «الاستدلال ببداهة العلم ببداهة المجموع».

**English:** “This judgement is self-evident without qualification” may be understood as saying that the whole composite judgement, including its constituent terms, is immediately known. The gloss denies that this is simply established: **the immediacy of the whole** cannot automatically serve as evidence for **the immediacy of each constituent part**. One can know an ordered composite under a general description without separately articulating each element's nature. Conversely, awareness of each element does not by itself yield awareness of the full arrangement. So the argument needs to specify whether the parts are taken distributively or under a collective consideration.

### I035.lower.02 — Avoiding a circular proof of the primitive concept
**Arabic anchors:** «بديهة العلم بجهة المجموع»؛ «الجزء الأول»؛ «يلزم المصادرة»؛ «لا يستلزم المدعى»؛ «بداهة تصوّر الوجود».

**English:** The note tests alternatives: if the self-evidence of the whole is inferred from the self-evidence of its pieces, the conclusion about one such piece is being **assumed**. If the self-evidence of each piece is inferred from that of the whole, a further rule is needed to licence the inference, and that rule has not been established merely by citing the whole. A person may possess an immediate conception of existence, but the proposed proof may fail to demonstrate that fact. The writer distinguishes the truth of the underlying claim from the sufficiency of this particular argument. ## I:36 · Necessary conception, definition, and the division of existence

### I036.upper.01 — Why understanding a whole does not automatically provide every part
**Arabic anchors:** «الأمر يتوقف على بديهة أجزائه في نفس الأمر»؛ «لكن لا يتوقف العلم ببداهته مطلقاً على العلم ببداهة أجزائه أي كل واحد منها مفصلاً»؛ «لا يتصور منه كسب ...».

**English:** A judgement may depend, in the order of reality, on the intelligibility of its constituents; however, knowledge that the judgement itself is self-evident does **not** necessarily depend on explicitly knowing, one by one, that each constituent is self-evident. The interlocutors dispute whether this rescues the original argument or simply changes its subject. The question is a logical one about the scope of self-evidence and the status of an inferred explanation of what is evident.

### I036.upper.02 — The interpretation of definition
**Arabic anchors:** «(والتعريف له) بالكون والتحقق والشيئية»؛ «قصده تفسير مدلول اللفظ»؛ «والبداهة إنما تمنع عن التعريف الحقيقي».

**English:** The text next discusses “defining” existence through terms such as **being**, **realization**, or **thinghood**. Such words may explain what a speaker means by the word “existence,” but they do not supply an **essential real definition** proving that existence is constituted by prior components. The fact that a concept is immediate rules out a real definitional analysis of that kind; it does not prevent a **lexical clarification** or an explanation of how an unfamiliar expression is used. This point introduces the dispute over whether existence has one common meaning.

### I036.ruled.01 — On “knowledge is not stopped”
**Arabic anchors:** «(قوله لا يتوقف العلم الخ) بناء على أن العلم قد يكون إجمالاً وقد يكون تفصيلاً»؛ «حصول الأول لا يتوقف على حصول الثاني»؛ «يجوز أن يجعل حصول الأول هنا ... بديهية أجزائه».

**English:** On “Knowledge is not dependent, etc.”: knowledge may be **summary or general** (*ijmālī*), and it may be **detailed** (*tafṣīlī*). Possession of the former need not depend on prior possession of the latter. The author of this separate small note proposes that an immediate grasp of the whole can coexist with a later detailed apprehension of its parts, and therefore resists moving without qualification from one form of knowledge to the other. The formula at the end is an abbreviated source note, not evidence of a named writer.

### I036.lower.01 — The cost of inferring existence from existence
**Arabic anchors:** «وجود ضروري وهذا تصوّر وجود خاص فبداهته تستلزم بديهة العام»؛ «لكن أظهر من هذا الاستدلال ... الاستدلال على بديهة تصور العلم»؛ «(قوله ما يتصور منه كسب ... الخ)».

**English:** The marginal commentator observes that a particular case of *existing* may be grasped immediately. Whether that establishes the immediate conception of existence **as a general notion** is another matter. He compares this argument with attempts to demonstrate the primitive intelligibility of **knowledge itself**, and argues that such demonstrations risk presupposing the conception they are meant to establish. Even when a proposition involving existence appears evident, its proof cannot be improved by confusing the immediate recognition of an instance with the logical analysis of a universal.

### I036.lower.02 — The relation between primitive conception and demonstrations
**Arabic anchors:** «أن حصول العلم بالوجود ... بديهة تصوّر العلم»؛ «الوجود»؛ «بديهي التصديق»؛ «استدلال»؛ «لا يترتب إلا بأحد الأوجه».

**English:** An argument which says “I know that something exists” already employs both **knowledge** and **existence**; if the point is to explain how either term is acquired, it must avoid covertly using that very acquisition as a premise. The author accordingly distinguishes a *dialectical illustration* of an immediate concept from a *demonstrative derivation* of it. What can be shown is how the terms behave when used in evident judgements; one should not claim thereby to have proved every part of their intelligibility from something more primitive. The marginal note ends by bringing the discussion toward the common concept of existence on I:37. [The last line of the lower gloss is compressed and remains unverified.]
