# ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Sanandajī — Taqrīb al-Marām, I:13–62

This is a page-aligned *interpretive draft*, not a fully literal sentence-by-sentence translation. Some arguments are abbreviated and must not be represented as complete authorial prose. ### I:13 · Arabic scan p. 26


 From the aforementioned aspect [the subject of the science] embraces the predicates of its
 questions. It might be said that by religious doctrines what is meant is the propositions ascribed
 to Islam and investigated through their evidences; in that case the difficulty disappears. The
 benefit, or ultimate end, of all this is attainment of felicity in both abodes. It is as though the
 author considered this to be one of the sciences whose end lies in the science itself and therefore
 contented himself with stating its definition.


**Explanation:** The opening continues a discussion from printed p. 12. The relation between the subject and the
    predicates of its questions concerns the technical architecture of a science.

### I:14 · Arabic scan p. 27



 Then what has been mentioned would be an end of that end. Knowledge does not admit of
 definition, for several reasons. The Imām al-Rāzī selected the first two. The first is that
 everyone's knowledge of his own existence is immediate; this is knowledge of a particular
 instance, whereas the absolute or unqualified concept [of knowledge] is its constituent. The
 latter ought therefore to be even more immediate. The second is that something other than
 knowledge is known either through knowledge or through something else; the second
 alternative entails circularity. This is an objection against one who claims that knowledge is
 acquired yet not immediate. The assertion that knowledge of the other comes through something
 else needs the original formulation and the associated distinction between conception and actual
 knowing.


**Explanation:** The last sentence is a cautious paraphrase of a difficult printed transition; the exact syllogistic form
    should be checked against secondary witnesses.

### I:15 · Arabic scan p. 28



 [The preceding argument] is challenged on the ground that it fails to distinguish conceiving
 knowledge from having knowledge. The immediate cognition involved is the occurrence of a
 particular piece of knowledge, namely knowledge of one's own existence, whereas conceiving
 that knowledge is something different from its occurrence. It therefore does not follow that an
 acquired conception of knowledge depends upon an already acquired conception of the same
 thing. Another view denies that knowledge can be defined because of its obscurity; this is
 ascribed to al-Ghazālī. Al-Rāzī answers this with the following discussion.


**Explanation:** The precise printed grammatical ties across pp. 14–16 are partly difficult to distinguish; this is a
    source-following interpretive rendering rather than a certified word-by-word text.

### I:16 · Arabic scan p. 29



 [Knowledge may be made known] by division and example. As for the division: belief is either
 firm or not firm; firm belief either corresponds to reality or does not; and reality-conforming
 belief is either stable or unstable. As for the example, one might say that knowledge is like visual
 apprehension of a particular person, or like the belief that one is half of two. Know that the point







 of the person advancing this explanation is that knowledge cannot be defined by a formula
 comprising its essential genus and differentia. That sort of definition is difficult in most things,
 and even in most objects of sense; how much more so in the case of cognition itself.
 Nevertheless, a description may generate some conception of knowledge, and in that respect
 furnish an account of it. When it is said that knowledge cannot be defined, what is intended is a
 definition exposing its essence. The ensuing explanations are not therefore excluded: they
 include the obtaining of a thing's form in the intellect and firm, reality-conforming, settled belief.


**Explanation:** Al-Rāzī's division and analogy clarify knowledge without claiming to yield an essential definition by
    genus and differentia.

### I:17 · Arabic scan p. 30



 Or knowledge may be described as 'a quality through which what is mentioned becomes
 manifest.' Here 'becomes manifest' means a complete disclosure without possibility of its
 opposite; 'what is mentioned' is whatever can be referred to or spoken of; the intended
 disclosure is thus complete disclosure, not the partial showing that belongs to conjecture. For
 those who adopt this explanation, knowledge is opposed to conjecture. 'In the one in whom it
 subsists' identifies the subject possessing that quality. Alternatively, knowledge is called
 apprehension of the composite or of the universal, while apprehension of what is simple or
 particular is called acquaintance (maʿrifa). The alternatives are mentioned to indicate
 differences of technical usage. None claims to be a definition that communicates the very
 essence of knowledge: rather, they state what different specialists have conventionally called it.
 Consequently they do not contradict the claim that it cannot be defined. The 'reality of rational
 inquiry' is now explained as the movement of the soul toward a sought thing that remains
 unknown in some respect.


**Explanation:** This page distinguishes a terminological account of ʿilm from a metaphysical definition. The text has
    the compound/universal versus simple/particular division, which should not be conflated with taṣdīq/taṣawwur.

### I:18 · Arabic scan p. 31



 The movement is 'among intelligibles' which the soul brings under consideration, until it finds
 what is needed, and it proceeds 'back' along the initial course. This qualification excludes
 immediate intuitive grasp (ḥads), in which the returning movement is absent. 'To the beginning'
 refers to the initial movement; 'in order to obtain' the 'unknown sought' means obtaining it
 insofar as it was unknown. The upshot is that inquiry consists of the two movements together;
 the ordering of premises commonly mentioned in definitions of inquiry is one of the features
 resulting from it. That inquiry produces knowledge at least in certain instances is not disputed
 between [the relevant parties], although the extent and character of that knowledge will be
 disputed.


**Explanation:** Sanandajī's two movements are the search for a middle term and the return to the desired
    conclusion; absence of that two-stage movement differentiates ḥads from naẓar.

### I:19 · Arabic scan p. 32



 [The possibility of obtaining knowledge] is acknowledged even in divine matters. If such
 knowledge is theoretical, to demand in every instance a further theoretical demonstration of the






 efficacy of inquiry would itself threaten an infinite sequence; that objection does not establish
 that inquiry fails. Some contend, however, that rational inquiry cannot yield knowledge of divine
 realities, because the essences and attributes of the Divine cannot be adequately conceived,
 whereas assent presupposes some conception. Against this it is maintained that not every item
 of divine knowledge demands an essential comprehension of the Divine. Knowledge achieved
 without rational consideration is immediate, which does not require the same discursive
 explanation. The objection to reason's operation in divine matters must be assessed in the light
 of these distinctions.


**Explanation:** A compressed but source-guided translation of the upper running text; the subordinate syntactic
    clauses and attribution require further collation. Distinguish limits of comprehending divine quiddity from the
    possibility of knowing that God exists or bears attributes.

### I:20 · Arabic scan p. 33



 For even with respect to what is immediate, disagreement may arise through obstinacy and
 inadequacy of apprehension. As for the claim that divine realities cannot be conceived [the
 author now replies in the continuation].

### I:21 · Arabic scan p. 34


 Those realities may not be conceived in their innermost essence; yet it remains possible to
 assent to them in other respects. And a person's guidance of someone else is not effective except
 insofar as that person is known to be trustworthy and his truthfulness acknowledged. If the truth
 of his assertion is established by rational consideration, then consideration is capable of yielding
 knowledge; if it rests solely on another's report, the same question recurs. The arguments
 directed at denying knowledge in divine matters therefore do not succeed merely by invoking
 the incomprehensibility of the Divine essence. The sophist, who denies sensible apprehensions,
 first principles, or both, does not have a claim that must simply be accepted as an objection. The
 author refers to such a denial as obstinate rather than treating it as a refutation of evident
 truths.


**Explanation:** Some following etymological remarks on sūfisṭāʾiyya are source-derived but the precise
    foreign-language terms are difficult to read in this printing.

### I:22 · Arabic scan p. 35



 The sophist disparages sense-cognition by alleging that the senses issue mistaken judgments:
 the motionless is sometimes seen as moving and the moving as stationary, as when a ship is in
 motion; there are other optical and sensory errors. Since some sense-judgments are mistaken,
 the skeptic supposes that none can be relied upon. The response is that even if particular
 sensory judgments can go wrong, this does not abolish evident cognition of other cases, such as
 the sun's illumination or the burning character of fire. Some likewise challenge the first
 principles, especially the proposition that affirmation and denial cannot both be true and cannot
 both be false—that a thing must be or not be. Yet their objection depends on already
 understanding existence and nonexistence, being and not-being; it cannot escape using the very
 first principles it sets out to deny.


**Explanation:** The example of sensory illusion does not establish universal sensory unreliability. The final
    reasoning appeals to the unavoidable conceptual distinction between being and non-being.



 Reasoning, obligation and proof

### I:23 · Arabic scan p. 36


 This is to establish the sense in which something is a subject or a predicate and in which the
 truth of an evidently self-evident proposition is presupposed. The claim that all the first
 principles depend upon one such proposition is illustrated by 'the whole is greater than the part':
 if the part were not smaller, it would be equal to the whole, contrary to what is evident. Such an
 argument should not be mistaken for a genuine proof of a first principle. Intellectual
 self-evidence supplies assent to the proposition without a prior act of demonstration. Others
 challenge all such propositions, but the nature of their challenge has already been considered.


**Explanation:** The precise example of whole and part belongs to the discussion of first principles; the printed page
    has additional subordinate examples. This English is a tightly source-following preliminary rendering.

### I:24 · Arabic scan p. 37



 The foregoing answers the objections and arguments. '[Whether knowledge follows inquiry] by
 way of customary practice' means, on the Ashʿarī doctrine, that all contingent things depend
 directly upon God, exalted is He, from the beginning: He is a free and choosing agent, and no
 action is necessitated to issue from Him. Or it may follow 'by generation' (tawlīd), on the
 assumption that not every action is immediately ascribed to Him, but that an agent's action can
 give rise to another action through an intermediary.


**Explanation:** The first view is explicitly an Ashʿarī customary-conjunction account; the second is distinguished as
    tawlīd, not a second wording of occasionalism.

### I:25 · Arabic scan p. 38



 By generation is meant that one action proceeds from an agent through the mediation of another
 action proceeding from that same agent. Thus the movement of a key may arise through the
 movement of the hand that turns it: the key's motion occurs by means of the hand's motion.
 Direct action, by contrast, is an action issuing from its agent without that intermediary. It was
 objected that knowledge is not in the category of action. The reply is that what is at issue is its
 generation by an act, not its classification as an action in that category. Or the knowledge may
 follow inquiry 'by necessity', on the view that, once the receptive subject has attained complete
 preparedness, the effusion of the event from the bestowing source becomes necessary.


**Explanation:** The distinction between an effect generated by an action and an effect that is an action preserves
    the force of the objection. The necessitarian reading is reported, not adopted.

### I:26 · Arabic scan p. 39



 That last explanation rests upon denying free choice to God, exalted far above such a claim.
 'There is disagreement about it' refers to the Ashʿarīs, the Muʿtazila and the philosophers,
 respectively, in the order just given. 'Inquiry directed to knowledge of God, exalted is He' means
 investigation undertaken for the attainment of certainty about His existence.


**Explanation:** Sanandajī explicitly assigns the three causal interpretations of inquiry to different traditions and
    objects to a necessitarian account that constrains divine freedom.

### I:27 · Arabic scan p. 40


 'Is obligatory' completes the author's proposition concerning inquiry into the existence of God.


**Explanation:** Only this short continuation belongs to the main commentary on printed p. 27; the much longer
    material below the rule is a separate gloss.

### I:28 · Arabic scan p. 41



 'By the revealed text' means the Qurʾān and the Sunna; 'and by consensus' is the agreement of
 the community on the obligation. 'Because it [inquiry] is'—that is, because rational inquiry is
 within the subject's power—'a prerequisite for the knowledge that is obligatory according to us
 by those [grounds].'


**Explanation:** The grammatical antecedent of 'because it is a prerequisite' is naẓar. The distinction between the
    obligation of knowledge and the derived obligation of inquiry matters for the coming objection.

### I:29 · Arabic scan p. 42



 That is, according to the revealed texts and consensus, inquiry is a practicable prerequisite on
 which the unqualified obligation depends.


**Explanation:** This page comprises only a brief continuation of the main text; the following extensive discussion is
    lower-register gloss.

### I:30 · Arabic scan p. 43



 'According to the Muʿtazila', inquiry for knowledge of God is obligatory because it is the
 prerequisite for the knowledge that is obligatory, 'since it averts a harm', namely the anticipated
 harm of punishment. Hence, on our account, obligation is established by revelation, and on
 theirs it is rational. 'They say: if [inquiry] were not obligatory except through revealed law', then
 the Prophet—may God bless him and grant him peace—could not rightly impose upon one who
 has yet to acknowledge the revelation 'the examination of the miracle', nor anything else whose
 proof depends upon establishing the miracle, including the existence and attributes of the
 Maker. If the Prophet tells a nonbeliever, 'Consider my miracle so that the truth of my claim
 becomes clear to you,' the direction to inquire cannot already be binding by appeal to the
 revelation which the inquiry is supposed to establish. Thus they object that it would be an order
 to undertake something not yet obligatory 'before the revealed law is established'.


**Explanation:** The objection concerns epistemic and normative priority. The direction to consider the miracle
    appears prior to the recipient's acceptance of the revelation's authority.

### I:31 · Arabic scan p. 44



 The revealed law cannot be established for me unless I investigate, because its establishment
 requires consideration. 'The reply' first distinguishes the being of an obligation from the
 knowledge that it obtains. Their conclusion would hold only if the former necessarily depended
 on the person's knowing it. But the obligation can obtain before the one obliged knows its basis.
 Inquiry into the miracle may therefore be required before a person acknowledges its obligation.





 Inquiry itself is needed to arrange the premises and verify the claim, and that is why the
 distinction does not fall into circularity. 'It is the knowledge of the obligation' which depends
 upon the establishment of the revealed law for the agent; it is not the obligation in itself. 'Then it
 is the first of the obligations sought for their own sake'—that is, knowledge of God—'because the
 other obligations depend upon it'. 'Inquiry into it' is a 'means to it' and is therefore obligatory on
 account of the intended knowledge. The word 'first' concerns duties that are ends in themselves,
 not the first external or instrumental step. Some made inquiry itself the first obligation; others
 said that the first is the intention to inquire. These discussions concern different accounts of an
 ultimate end and its prerequisite.


**Explanation:** This is a substantial running-text passage. The critical distinction is between an obligation and
    knowing that it is obligatory; inquiry is instrumental, knowledge is the end.

### I:32 · Arabic scan p. 45



 There is no dispute that, among duties intended for their own sake, the first is knowledge of God;
 the dispute arises when means to it are included. The intended 'proof' is whatever, when
 considered, can lead one to a judgment, whether that judgment is conclusive or less so.
 Sometimes dalīl is restricted to that which gives a firmly certain judgment, and then it is
 opposed to an amāra, an indicative sign. 'According to reason' and 'according to transmission'
 distinguish the basis upon which the inference rests; a transmitted proof can also yield certainty
 with supporting circumstances. Yet what is dependent for its authority upon revelation cannot
 itself be used to establish the authority of revelation, for that would be circular. Matters
 rationally compatible with either alternative may be known by transmission, but whatever
 validates the transmission itself must be established by reason. Nor should the intellect's
 inability to decide all revealed questions be confused with an inability to establish the credibility
 of the revealer.


**Explanation:** Sanandajī distinguishes broad proof from demonstrative certainty and rational priority from the
    authority of transmitted doctrine. Source-based rendering; some of the printed objections and responses extend
    onto the following page.



 The concept of existence and quiddity

### I:33 · Arabic scan p. 46



 The establishment of the doctrines, and of the other inquiries on which those doctrines depend,
 is the purpose for entering upon these discussions. We therefore say: 'Chapter II, concerning
 general matters.' These are the matters common to all beings, whether Necessary Existent,
 substance or accident, as in the case of unity; or to most beings, as with specific possibility,
 origination, necessity through another, multiplicity and being an effect. These general matters
 are placed before the other investigations because they constitute, as it were, their foundations.
 The author therefore begins by discussing existence and its apprehension, saying: 'The
 conception of existence ...'


**Explanation:** The beginning of this page closes Chapter I and initiates the general-ontology division. Note that
    general means common to all or most beings, not simply abstract in contrast to concrete.

### I:34 · Arabic scan p. 47


 '[The conception of existence] is immediate.' For the proposition 'a thing is either existent or
 nonexistent' is self-evidently true, and judgment presupposes apprehension of its constituent
 notions, existence and nonexistence. Those notions are therefore supposed to be immediately
 understood. Should you say that a self-evident judgment does not require a self-evident
 conception of every constituent, the answer is that the argument concerns the judgment and the
 terms upon which it rests: one may recognise a proposition's truth after conceiving its two sides,
 and nevertheless dispute whether the conception of one or both terms is itself immediate. This
 requires careful distinction between the immediacy of assent and the immediacy of the terms.


**Explanation:** Sanandajī introduces an objection to the inference from a self-evident proposition to the
    self-evidence of each concept. This passage crosses over onto the following printed page.

### I:35 · Arabic scan p. 48



 For the self-evidence of that judgment may be self-evidence without qualification, and the
 conclusion does not require a hidden assumption that each part has already been separately
 established as self-evident. Rather, one must distinguish what is grasped immediately in the act
 of judgment from the discursive proof that this or that constituent concept is immediately
 grasped. The argument turns on that difference; it should not be read as an independent
 demonstration of every disputed property of existence.

### I:36 · Arabic scan p. 49



 The self-evidence of the proposition does depend, in the thing itself, upon the immediacy of its
 parts. But it does not follow that our knowledge of the proposition's immediacy depends upon
 separately knowing the immediacy of each part. If one recognises that the proposition is
 assented to even by a person incapable of acquisition through reasoning, such as a child, one
 may infer that its constituent conceptions are not derived through a learned demonstration.
 Thus, if existence is understood immediately, why undertake to define it? The answer is that the
 'definition' here of existence 'by being, actuality, and thinghood' is merely verbal. The intention
 is to explain how the word is used; an immediate apprehension does not exclude a verbal
 clarification. It only dispenses with an essential definition that would create the apprehension
 itself.


**Explanation:** Conceiving the meaning of existence and explaining the conventional signification of the Arabic
    word are separate operations.

### I:37 · Arabic scan p. 50



 The theologians and philosophers disagree over whether 'existent' expresses a meaning common
 to the things it names, or is merely a word applied equivocally; they also disagree about whether
 existence is additional to quiddity or identical with it. Three considerations are presented to
 indicate that existence is common in meaning. The first is the validity of dividing existence into
 'the existence of the Necessary' and 'the existence of what is other than Him'. Such a division,
 when genuine, presupposes a common meaning that the subdivisions differentiate. Otherwise
 the division would be no more than distributing different, unrelated uses of the same word. Thus
 the author begins with the 'validity of the division into the existence of the Necessary and the






 existence of another', under which the plurality of instances falls.


**Explanation:** The notion of conceptual commonality is not yet the metaphysical claim that all existents possess
    one homogeneous mode of existence.

### I:38 · Arabic scan p. 51



 The first division is understood independently of linguistic stipulation: it concerns the subject
 matter and what is actually signified, and therefore it does not vary with differences of
 languages, whereas an arbitrary division of an equivocal word would do so. The second
 consideration is the ability to judge confidently that something exists while remaining uncertain
 about its particular specification—whether it is substance, accident, or some other kind of being.
 If existence were utterly different in meaning in every such case, there would be no one
 determination that could be known while the distinct specification remained unknown. The third
 consideration is the exhaustiveness of the distinction between the existent and the nonexistent.
 If the term existent had no common meaning, the division 'a human being is either existent or
 nonexistent' would not be unambiguously exhaustive; one could still ask: existent in which of
 several unrelated senses? The argument therefore rests on the intelligibility of the whole
 division, the possibility of affirmation without deciding a species, and the opposition between
 existence and nonexistence.


**Explanation:** The three marks establish a common intelligible notion: division, assent under uncertainty about the
    particular kind, and exhaustive excluded middle.

### I:39 · Arabic scan p. 52



 An equivocal account would permit a thing to be described as existent under one meaning and
 not existent under another without thereby falling simply on one side of the proposed exhaustive
 division. He next adduces evidence that existence is, 'in the mind, additional to quiddity'. This
 means that the intellect can attend to existence without attending to quiddity, and to quiddity
 without attending to existence. Three considerations support this. First, one can truly deny
 existence of the quiddity under consideration: 'it is valid to negate it [existence] of it [the
 quiddity]'. If existence simply were the quiddity as an understood concept, such negation would
 amount to negating a thing of itself, which is impossible. The second consideration follows.


**Explanation:** Addition here is conceptual or intentional. This argument does not yet settle whether an existent has
    two actually distinct constituents outside the intellect.

### I:40 · Arabic scan p. 53



 Second, predicating existence of a quiddity communicates information. If existence were
 precisely the same intelligible content as the quiddity, the predication would add nothing, since
 predicating a thing of itself is uninformative. Third, knowing that a quiddity exists can require
 investigation, whereas understanding what that quiddity is need not do so. We may grasp what
 is meant by jinn or devils, for example, while still requiring evidence that they exist. The quiddity
 and its existence therefore stand differently in our cognition. This is the position of the majority
 of theologians and philosophers. A further qualification concerns the Necessary Existent: some
 hold that His reality is a special existence and that His existence is not something additional to a
 distinct quiddity. They argue that, if the Necessary had an existence superadded to His quiddity,






 then either that existence would depend upon the quiddity or the quiddity upon that existence,
 compromising the claim of necessity.


**Explanation:** It is crucial to distinguish conceptual distinction in possible beings from the debate over whether the
    Necessary Existent has a quiddity distinct from existence.

### I:41 · Arabic scan p. 54



 If the existence of the Necessary depended upon something other than Himself, He would not be
 necessary in Himself. But if the quiddity were treated as the producing cause of its own
 existence, a difficulty would arise: the cause must have an appropriate priority to its effect, and
 the quiddity would have to be prior in existence to the very existence it was to explain, which is
 impossible. An answer distinguishes the kinds of causal priority. What requires existential
 priority is the efficient cause; a quiddity is not an efficient agent that produces its own existence.
 The dispute therefore cannot be settled by simply transferring the law governing one kind of
 cause to every sense in which a thing may be considered a principle. The next passage
 completes this objection and answer.


**Explanation:** Different ways of being a cause or principle must be distinguished. The claim that a quiddity is
    conceptually antecedent to our judgment does not make it an efficiently existing producer of its own existence.

### I:42 · Arabic scan p. 55


 The claim that a possible quiddity requires an agent establishes its dependence, but not that
 existence is an additional self-subsisting constituent. Some argue that, if existence as such were
 additional, the Necessary Existent would also be subject to the same analysis. The reply turns on
 the distinction between quiddity and the existence attaching to it: the existence of a possible
 thing is not identical with its quiddity, which is in itself receptive to existence and nonexistence;
 its possibility is not abolished when its existence is absent. By contrast, the Necessary Existent
 cannot be conceived as having a quiddity awaiting the addition of an existence supplied from
 elsewhere. Thus a conceptual distinction in the case of possible beings need not entail a
 separable, produced existence in the Necessary. The next step is the discussion of what exists in
 the mind and in external reality.


**Explanation:** Source-guided reading of the final upper lines; the printed page's lower commentary elaborates the
    modal and causal distinctions and should not be counted as Sanandajī's main voice.

### I:43 · Arabic scan p. 56



 If we say that existence is necessary in the case of the Necessary Existent, then His reality
 cannot be treated as a quiddity dependent upon an additional, externally supplied existence. The
 existence that is understood immediately and shared conceptually among existents is not the
 same as the special reality of the Necessary Existent. The discussion is not about the general,
 shared concept but about the particular existence which differs from all other existences in its
 reality: that particular existence is not what is immediately conceived in the first place. When it
 is said that existence 'subsists in' the quiddity of a possible thing, what is intended is that it does
 so in the mind, not as an accident subsisting outside the mind as whiteness subsists in a body.
 For the possible quiddity cannot already possess an external actuality independently of its
 existence, and a regress would follow if every existence were treated as an additional existing






 item requiring a further existence.

### I:44 · Arabic scan p. 57


 Moreover, if the quiddity in which existence is supposed to inhere were nonexistent, there would
 be a contradiction; if it were existent, the account would involve a circle or regress. Thus the
 account of existence as additional to quiddity, and of its subsisting in it, concerns what the
 intellect distinguishes, not two separable things already constituted in the external world.
 Whiteness in a body is not the right model for that intellectual distinction. '[The existence of the
 Necessary] shares with it [possible existence] an accident'—namely the notion of 'being' or
 established existence, the common intelligible notion predicated of different existences. It is
 predicated of particular existents 'analogically or by gradation' (bi-l-tashkīk): existence belongs
 more primarily to a cause than to an effect, to substance rather than accident, and in some
 respects to what endures rather than to what passes away. This gradation concerns the shared
 predication, not a single independent entity superadded to all.


**Explanation:** The literal text specifies cause/effect and substance/accident as examples of the gradation called
    tashkīk.

### I:45 · Arabic scan p. 58



 The different instances of this common predication are not thereby made the same in their
 concrete reality. Their particular modes of existence differ, even if the general word 'being' is
 applied to them as one applies 'light' to the various luminous things. Yet this account raises a
 difficulty. If the special existence of each thing is simply its being established, then the 'being'
 which is said to be predicated of that special existence threatens to be identical with what it is
 predicated of: the difference would be only between a general and a restricted way of
 considering the same thing. Such a difference is not sufficient to posit a real accident inhering in
 its subject. If, on the other hand, the special existence were something over and above actuality,
 it would no longer be obvious how mere 'being' is predicated of it. The simile of one kind of light
 relative to other lights is intended to illustrate a common predication among differently qualified
 actualities, not a substantial addition common to them all.


**Explanation:** Sanandajī here notes a tension in construing the common concept as an additional inhering
    property. His example is proportional predication of light, not unqualified identity of all lights.

### I:46 · Arabic scan p. 59



 The light of the sun, of the moon and of a lamp differs in concrete reality, although each shares
 the description of luminosity. In that way what is predicated of the different existences may be
 conceptually common without the things themselves having one and the same reality. As for the
 claim that existence 'is identical with quiddity in every case', whether necessary or possible, this
 is an opinion attributed to Shaykh al-Ashʿarī—may God have mercy upon him. It is perhaps in
 connection with this that he says the nonexistent is not a thing.


**Explanation:** The assertion is attributed to al-Ashʿarī; it should not be silently imposed on every theologian nor on
    Avicenna's distinction between quiddity and existence.



 Mental existence and representation

### I:47 · Arabic scan p. 60


 For if existence were precisely identical with quiddity, the quiddity's nonexistence would be the
 negation of the quiddity itself. When its existence ceased, it would have no independent
 establishment: the nonexistent would be sheer negation. But if existence were an added
 accident, as whiteness is added to a body, the disappearance of the accident need not entail the
 disappearance of the subject. Hence those who defend a thinghood of the nonexistent seek to
 preserve the quiddity when its existence is absent. The response is that the attribution of
 existence to a quiddity is an intellectual consideration, not an external inherence between two
 already actual things. That is the sense in which 'existence' can be additional in thought without
 a separately established substratum. The proposition that a thing is one in the external world
 therefore does not mean that the concept of existence is identical with its quiddity: one may
 distinguish the two conceptually while denying that two realities separately exist outside the
 mind. 'For the meaning is not that the concept of existence is the same as the concept of a thing';
 rather, the issue is whether the existence and the thing to which it is ascribed have two
 independent external realities or only one.

### I:48 · Arabic scan p. 61



 In my judgment, what is meant by existence as something distinct in thought is not an
 independent object which may be found beside its subject in the external world. The effects and
 characteristics by which a thing is recognised belong to the thing's actual reality. Existence is
 divided 'according to meaning' into real or proper existence—the actuality in virtue of which a
 thing is established externally—and mental existence, which is a non-independent mode
 resembling the shadow of an original. In mental existence there is a form conforming to the
 thing, in the sense that, if that form were actual outside the mind, it would be that thing. These
 two, external and mental, are called modes of existence 'properly'. The term is also extended
 'figuratively' to verbal and written existence, since in speech one has the name and in writing
 the inscribed form. A spoken or written name is itself a sound or inscription, not the external
 person and not the quiddity as intellectually understood. Thus, in the case of 'human being', the
 external existing thing is the individual, the mentally existing object is the quiddity, while the
 word and marks refer to them.


**Explanation:** The comparison of mental existence to a shadow is Sanandajī's way of explaining an intentional
    form, not the view that the mind contains an additional external individual.

### I:49 · Arabic scan p. 62



 In speech there is the spoken name; in writing there is its written representation. These can be
 called forms of existence of the thing named only by attribution. Strictly speaking, the spoken
 word has its own actual existence as a sound, and the written sign its own existence as marks;
 neither is the thing signified. One must therefore distinguish what exists as a sign from what is
 signified through it. The proof of 'mental existence' is now introduced. Although knowledge and

### I:50 · Arabic scan p. 63


 We form judgments concerning things that are not externally established. We conceive even
 impossibilities and judge, for instance, that one impossible object falls under a broader concept

 affirmative judgments about them. We understand real or essentially quantified propositions

 an animal, considered under the supposition that such things exist. If an affirmative judgment is
 to concern an object, and that object is not established externally, something must explain its
 availability to cognition. This is a principal argument for mental existence. Another line of
 reasoning holds that intellectual knowledge is the presence of what is known in the intellect; if
 nothing corresponding to the object were there, the relation of knowledge to its object would be

**Explanation:** The phoenix example illustrates a proposition with a hypothetical universal subject, not an assertion

### I:51 · Arabic scan p. 64

### I:52 · Arabic scan p. 65

### I:61 · Arabic scan p. 74



 —‘and an affirmative predication requires identity of the two terms in the subject’ (dhātan
 wa-huwiyyatan): the thing to which the subject-term applies must be the very thing to which the
 predicate-term applies, whether outside the mind or in the mind. For the statement ‘this is that’
 is invalid if the two terms designate quite unrelated things. ‘And [it requires] their difference in
 conception’: the concept of the subject-term and the concept of the predicate-term must differ,

 distinct beings. One may say ‘earth is earth’, but with no difference of meaning the utterance
