### I. Where the original chapter really begins

**Printed II:281 and most of II:282** continue the previous discussion of *commanding what is right and forbidding what is wrong*. The original conditions include knowledge, reasonable possibility of influence, and avoiding overriding harm. One controversial reported argument at II:282 treats the scriptural 'no compulsion in religion' language through an abrogation claim; this edition records that claim as a **position taken in the historical theological text**, not as a modern legal conclusion.

Near the **bottom of printed II:282**, an actual new printed **فصل** opens on the reality of *īmān*, initially defined in language as **taṣdīq**. Its technical/legal implications unfold on II:283–286. The old Stage18 navigation grouping of II:281 under faith should be corrected accordingly.

#### ST30.UPPER.II283.01 — Connected argument: assent to what the Prophet necessarily brought
**Source:** printed II:283, facsimile physical PDF 578, source type **mixed upper (base-text lemmata plus sharḥ)**.
**Arabic:** وفي الشرع تصديق النبي فيما علم مجيئه به بالضرورة أي فيما اشتهر كونه من الدين بحيث يعلمه العامة من غير افتقار إلى نظر واستدلال كوحدة الصانع ووجوب الصلاة وحرمة الخمر ونحو ذلك ويكفي التصديق بالجميع إجمالا وإنما يحتاج إلى التصديق تفصيلا عند ملاحظة التفاصيل
**English:** In religious usage, faith is affirming the Prophet concerning what is necessarily known to have been brought by him. That is, matters so widely recognized as belonging to the religion that ordinary people know them without the need for investigation or inference—such as the unity of the Creator, the obligation of prayer, and the prohibition of wine. General assent to the whole suffices; detailed assent is required when the particulars themselves come under consideration.
**Precise limit:** One substantial contiguous passage in the upper register, with mixed al-Taftāzānī parenthetical matn and Sanandajī explanatory text. It does NOT cover the entire printed upper page or the much longer lower commentary.

#### ST30.UPPER.II284.01 — Argument from the whole and its part answered by two senses of faith
**Source:** printed II:284, facsimile physical PDF 579, source type **mixed upper (base-text lemmata plus sharḥ)**.
**Arabic:** فإن قيل كيف لا ينتفي الكل بانتفاء الجزء ... قلنا المراد أنه ... يطلق على ما هو الأصل وأساس دخول الجنة والنجاة من النار وهو التصديق وحده أو مع الإقرار وعلى ما هو الكامل المنجي ... وهو التصديق مع الإقرار والعمل والأول لا ينتفي بانتفاء العمل
**English:** If it is asked: How can the whole fail to disappear when one of its parts disappears? ... The first sense does not disappear merely through the omission of works.

### V. Critical logic and source interpretation

**1. Faith as the assent to necessarily known religious teachings.** The technical definition is assent to what is necessarily known to have been conveyed by the Prophet, illustrated by the unity of the Creator, prayer's obligatory status and wine's prohibition. The source differentiates general assent to the body of necessary teachings from detailed assent when the individual matters are presented. This is not the claim that every believer must possess a demonstrative proof for every doctrine, nor that any sincerely held opinion automatically counts as religious assent.

**2. Three competing accounts of the elements of faith.** The source identifies inward assent as one candidate, assent with verbal profession as another, and assent, verbal profession and bodily deeds as a further view. These proposals are not interchangeable. A claim of mere profession without inward assent concerns a different subject from the inward believer who never had an outward opportunity to profess.

**3. Inward reality versus worldly legal recognition.** II:283–284 distinguish faith's reality in the sight of God from the outward profession upon which a society may apply legal judgements. A lower note states that the person considered can be a believer **before God** without necessarily fulfilling the conditions for the application of **outward legal rulings**. This is a difference of relation and forum, not simply a contradiction between two definitions.

**4. A formal part–whole objection, and the answer by two uses of the word.** If faith is defined as a whole consisting of assent, profession and action, omitting the action appears to remove a constituent part and therefore the whole. The response distinguishes the **foundation** of saving faith from its **complete/perfected** realization. This is a conceptual division of the predicate, not proof that actions are irrelevant to moral life or spiritual perfection.

**5. The reported positions of the Muʿtazila and Khārijites.** These are reported as different statuses for someone failing in prescribed action: one school is said to treat such a person as outside faith but not thereby classified as an unbeliever, while another treats that departure as unbelief. Historical detail and specific school's internal differences require independent primary-school comparison, not mere repetition of the scholastic doxography.

**6. Distinguish taṣdīq from both formal knowledge and audible words.** The II:285 lower excerpt raises whether assent is properly a cognitive act (`fiʿl al-ʿilm`) or an act of the heart associated with predominant cognition. The II:286 note asks why a verbal expression signifying belief could not be uttered without that belief. These arguments challenge any inference from the semantic content of a phrase or its utterance to the actual inner assent of its speaker.

**7. Qurʾānic and prophetic evidence about the heart.** The original cites verses about faith written in or not yet entering hearts and hadith language about a mustard seed's weight of faith in the heart. Each is evidence within the source's theological argument; the longer source and transmission apparatus have not all been independently authenticated or completely translated.
